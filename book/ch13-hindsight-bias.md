## 13. "Everything Happens for a Reason"

### *Hindsight Bias, Outcome Bias, and the Falsification of History*

Dobelli calls hindsight bias one of the most persistent of all thinking errors,
and his description of it is precise: **in retrospect, everything looks
inevitable.** After the fact, we cannot recover the state of not knowing. The
2008 financial crisis was obviously coming — read the commentary from 2006 and
almost nobody says so. The First World War was the inevitable product of European
tensions — read the diplomatic correspondence from June 1914. The bias is not
lying about the past; it is a genuine inability to reconstruct the uncertainty you
were actually in, which is why Dobelli recommends keeping a journal of your
predictions. He notes that people who do this are consistently shocked, and that
the shock does not stop the bias from returning.

Outcome bias is the sibling: we judge decisions by how they turned out rather
than by whether they were good decisions at the time. The surgeon whose patient
died is judged harshly and the one whose patient survived is judged well, even
where the decisions were identical and the outcome was chance.

Together, these two produce the single most common religious sentence in the
English language.

*Everything happens for a reason.* Notice when it is said. Always afterwards.
Nobody says it in the middle. It is said when a woman who lost a job three years
ago now has a better one, and the redundancy becomes providence. It is said when a
couple who could not conceive adopted a child they now cannot imagine living
without, and the infertility becomes a plan. And in each case the reasoning is
hindsight bias operating on a life that had, in fact, thousands of possible
branches, of which exactly one occurred, and which was then read backwards as
though it had been aimed.

Here is the test, and it is a hard one to sit with. Run the counterfactual. Had
the woman not lost the job, she would now have four years of seniority, a
different circle of friends, and a life she would describe with the same
satisfaction, and if anything remarkable had come of it she would have called
that providence too. Had the couple conceived, they would be holding a biological
child and thanking God for answered prayer — the same God, the same gratitude, the
opposite outcome. **A providence detector that fires on every branch is not
detecting anything.** It is post-hoc narration, and it is available in unlimited
quantities regardless of what actually happens, which is the definition of
worthless evidence.

The mechanism gets crueller at the top of the severity range, and this is where I
think the doctrine does real damage rather than merely being false. When the
outcome is genuinely bad — a child dead, a body permanently broken, a country
destroyed — the machinery still has to run, because the sentence has been promised
in advance. So a reason must be constructed for a thing that has no available
upside, and the results are the sentences that people who have been to enough
funerals learn to dread. God needed another angel. He is teaching you something.
Everything is for a purpose you cannot see yet. This is hindsight bias forced
past its breaking point, and the person on the receiving end can usually feel
that something false is being done to them, even when they believe it.

There is an alternative that most religious people, in practice, prefer:
*silence*. It is worth noticing that the best pastoral instinct — sitting with
someone and not explaining — is the instinct that abandons the doctrine. The Book
of Job, which is the most honest text in the Bible, spends thirty-seven chapters
demolishing every explanation offered by Job's friends and then declines to give
one. The friends are the ones doing theodicy. The text says they are wrong.

Dobelli's *falsification of history* completes the picture. His finding: we
unconsciously adjust our memory of past beliefs to match what we now believe, and
we do it without any sense of revision — studies asking people to recall their own
prior opinions find that they report their current ones and are certain the
memory is accurate. This is the maintenance mechanism for a religious life. Ask
someone how they felt during the two years of unemployment and you will get an
account of learning to trust God. Contemporaneous diaries from those two years,
where they exist, record terror and anger and the suspicion that nobody was
listening. Both accounts are sincere. The first has been rewritten by the outcome.

Which means that the accumulated evidence of a religious life — "I have seen God's
faithfulness over forty years" — is not forty years of observation. It is a
memory that has been continuously edited by every subsequent outcome, in a
direction the editor cannot detect. That is not a slur on anybody's honesty. It
is the same process that makes everyone certain they predicted the election result
they now know.

**In conclusion:** the only defence against hindsight bias is a written record
made before the outcome. Keep one. Write down what you expect, what you are
praying for, what you believe God is doing, and date it — then read it in two
years without flinching. And when you catch yourself saying that everything
happens for a reason, ask what you would have said had the other branch occurred.
If the answer is *the same thing*, then the sentence is not a description of the
world. It is a sound people make when the outcome is already known.
