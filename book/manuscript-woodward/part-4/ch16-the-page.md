# 16. The Page

The reform agenda for Nigerian aviation fits on one page.

It has fit on one page for thirty years.

The page is not the problem.

Every element of what follows has appeared in some ministerial
roadmap, audit response, or national aviation policy since the
1990s. Several elements exist in law today. This chapter's purpose
is therefore not discovery but *specification* — each item stated
with its mechanism, its cost, its owner, and its predictable enemy.

Because an agenda without named enemies is a communiqué.

And it opens with the era's great cautionary tale: the project that
consumed a decade of the ministry's attention while the page went
unexecuted.

## Farnborough, England, July 2018

The unveiling had everything.

A stand at the world's most important air show. A livery — green and
white, with the national colors sweeping the tail. A minister
holding a scale model for the cameras.

Nigeria Air.

The national carrier was returning, fifteen years after Nigeria
Airways had been liquidated with its workers unpaid.

What followed instead of an airline was five years of procurement
opera.

The project was suspended within months, then revived. A strategic
partner was selected — Ethiopian Airlines, the mirror of Chapter 15
now summoned as vendor — over the objections and litigation of the
domestic carriers' association, whose members correctly understood
that a subsidized state competitor would destroy them.

And then, in May 2023, days before the administration left office,
the launch.

A Boeing 737 landed at Abuja in Nigeria Air colors.

It was an Ethiopian Airlines aircraft, chartered, repainted, flown
in for the ceremony.

It flew home afterward.

The House of Representatives committee that investigated the project
rendered its verdict in the harshest available parliamentary
language. The succeeding administration suspended the project.
Anti-corruption authorities subsequently brought charges connected
to the ministry's projects — contested in court as this book went to
press, and on which this book takes no position.

The livery survives as a photograph.

## THE ARGUMENT: Four attempts, one template

Nigeria Air was not the first attempt to conjure a successor to
Nigeria Airways.

It was the fourth.

And four failures on a single template constitute a dataset rather
than a run of bad luck.

**The first came before the old airline was formally dead.** Around
2001, with Nigeria Airways in terminal decline, the aviation
minister of the day, Kema Chikwe, championed the flotation of a new
national carrier — announced under the name Nigeria Global — that
was to rise alongside and then replace the dying flag.

It was launched with ministerial fanfare. It acquired the trappings
of an airline: a name, designated aircraft arrangements, inaugural
ambitions on the London route.

It never became a functioning carrier. It dissolved within a couple
of years amid controversy over its leasing arrangements and funding,
leaving behind legislative queries and a précis of every error the
later attempts would repeat.

**The second was the era's most serious.** Virgin Nigeria, launched
in 2004–05 as a joint venture with Virgin Atlantic — the British
carrier supplying management, brand, and operational standards.

This was a deliberate borrowing of external institutional quality,
on the Kenya Airways model, and it deserves credit: it flew, and for
a period it flew well.

Then the arrangement met the Nigerian political economy. Disputes
over terminal allocation. Friction over brand and control. The exit
of Virgin. Rebranding as Nigerian Eagle and then Air Nigeria under
new private ownership. And collapse into unpaid salaries and
grounding by 2012.

The lesson is Chapter 15's, in reverse: rented institutional quality
works exactly as long as the landlord state honors the lease.

**The third** was a succession of paper carriers and announcements
through the 2010s that never reached a runway.

**And the fourth was Nigeria Air** — the project of Hadi Sirika,
minister of state and then minister of aviation across the Buhari
years, and its most determined champion.

Four attempts. Four ministries. One method.

The airline summoned from the ministry outward. Capitalized in
announcements. And delivered to a state that had still not performed
the unglamorous repairs beneath it.

## THE ECONOMICS: Why the flag carrier keeps returning

The recurrence deserves explanation rather than mockery, because
four ministers across two decades and three parties are not simply
four fools.

The flag carrier project has a political economy that makes it
almost irresistible, and understanding it is necessary to resisting
it.

**Its benefits are concentrated, visible, and immediate.** A livery
at an air show. A ribbon. A photograph on every front page. National
pride, invoked at negligible marginal cost. And, in the procurement
itself, contracts with identifiable beneficiaries.

**Its costs are diffuse, delayed, and technical.** The competitive
damage to existing carriers. The subsidy that will be required
indefinitely. The opportunity cost of the ministerial attention
consumed.

That is the identical asymmetry Chapter 8 identified in the
economics of neglect — operating here in the opposite direction, to
produce action rather than inaction, but on the same principle.

Politics systematically overweights the visible.

And there is a second driver, subtler and more sympathetic. The flag
carrier is *legible* as an achievement in a way that regulatory
capacity is not. A minister can point to an aircraft. He cannot
point to a crash that did not happen.

Which returns us to the deepest structural finding in this book: the
things that keep people alive in aviation are invisible, and the
things that are visible do not keep anyone alive.

The counter-model exists, is unglamorous, and is working one border
away. Ghana, post-liquidation, grew its traffic on other people's
capital while investing its scarce public attention in its
regulator.

Fly the flag on the certificate, not the fuselage.

Let the state's insignia mean rigorous oversight — and let the
market own the metal.

The applause line the politicians keep searching for is Chapter 10's:
a decade without funerals.

## The people the state forgot

There is one more piece of unfinished business from the first flag
carrier, and no honest account of Nigerian aviation omits it.

When Nigeria Airways was liquidated in 2003–04, it carried thousands
of serving staff and a longer tail of retirees. Pilots. Engineers.
Cabin crew. Clerks. Many with two and three decades of service.

Their severance, pensions, and accumulated entitlements became
claims against the estate of a dead airline — owned by the state
that had killed it.

The federal government repeatedly promised settlement.

What followed was a generation of waiting.

The ex-workers organized into associations of the retrenched. They
picketed the ministry and the National Assembly through successive
administrations. Their leaders aged into professional petitioners,
appearing before committee after committee with files of verified
entitlements.

And the associations began keeping a second ledger.

The roll of members who had died waiting.

Former captains and licensed engineers finished their lives in
penury. Some were reduced to public appeals for medical care. By the
unions' counts — never contradicted by better official data — the
dead ran into the thousands before the state substantially paid.

Movement came in 2017–18, when the government approved a settlement
package reported at roughly forty-five billion naira, with payments
beginning in tranches from 2018 and verification disputes and
residual claims continuing for years afterward.

The payment was real.

So was its arithmetic. Settled fifteen years late, in a currency
that had lost most of its value across the interval, the
entitlements delivered a fraction of the purchasing power the
workers had earned — the difference quietly confiscated by time and
inflation from the people least able to absorb it.

## THE ECONOMICS: Why the pension default was a safety event

That saga belongs in this chapter rather than in a footnote, because
its consequences operated *forward*, on the safety of the system.

Institutions run on deferred compensation. The pension is its purest
form: work now, be paid later, on the strength of an institutional
promise.

An employer who defaults on the deferred teaches every current
employee a lesson, and the lesson is learned quickly and never
unlearned.

**Demand everything now, or leave.**

Every serving professional in Nigerian aviation watched, for twenty
years, what the state's gratitude to its aviation workforce looked
like in practice.

And priced the observation.

The exodus that Chapter 3 attributed to Gulf salaries was propelled
by more than salaries. It was propelled by a demonstrated
contingency: the knowledge that a Nigerian aviation career's
terminal benefits were a lottery ticket drawn against the treasury's
attention span.

Which produces a finding that no aviation authority anywhere lists
among its safety indicators, and several should:

*A state that defaults on its aviation pensions is degrading its
future inspectorate.*

The ex-workers of Nigeria Airways paid for the airline's death
twice. Once in their livelihoods.

And once as the cautionary tale that helped drain their successors
abroad.

## The page

**One. Finish the investigator.**

The safety investigation bureau has the statute. Give it the
substance: a statutory publication clock — interim statement within
thirty days, public docket within a year; a recommendation register,
public, in which every addressee must answer within ninety days,
accepted and funded and dated, or refused with reasons; and the
just-culture shield drafted in Chapter 14.

*Cost:* trivial. *Owner:* the bureau's board, and the National
Assembly for the shield. *Enemy:* the blame system — which is to
say, everyone with something pending.

This is the keystone. It is cheap, it compounds, and the honesty of
every other item depends on it.

**Two. Ring-fence the regulator. Permanently, this time.**

The 2006 settlement has eroded at the margins: treasury
consolidation drives eyeing the levies, appointment politics
reclaiming the leadership.

Restore and entrench. Levies protected at the highest available
legal altitude. The Director-General appointed from a professionally
qualified shortlist to protected tenure, with staggered board terms
no single administration can sweep. And technical salaries
benchmarked to the market that actually bids for inspectors — the
Gulf and the international agencies, not the civil service scale.

*Cost:* self-funding from existing levies. *Enemy:* every future
minister of aviation, structurally. The item exists to outlive them.

**Three. Fill the trucks. Run the drills.**

Chapter 13's package, verbatim. Firefighting substance audited
unannounced. One full-scale mass-casualty exercise per international
airport per year, hospitals in the loop, after-action findings
published. A national rescue coordination center with a tested
contact tree. Manifest integrity enforced digitally. A
family-assistance act.

*Cost:* pocket change against any terminal project. *Enemy:* nobody
— which is precisely why it starves. Assign a single accountable
coordinator by statute.

**Four. Keep capital cheap by making promises legible.**

Full performance of the aircraft-finance treaty Nigeria has
ratified: repossession timelines guaranteed through a designated
commercial court list, registry operations at world standard. This
recaptures the financing discount for every Nigerian lessee.

Add transparent slot and route allocation, so carriers compete on
operations rather than on access.

*Cost:* administrative. *Payoff:* basis points on every financed
airframe — the cheapest fleet-safety money in existence.

**Five. Consolidate without cartelizing.**

Higher capital floors and honest mergers, paired with continental
open skies so that consolidation's pricing power is disciplined by
competition rather than by a regulator who will lose that fight.

And resolution planning for the carriers now too large to ground —
Chapter 12's living will — so that the safety decision is never
again hostage to the schedule decision.

**Six. Bring the military inside the perimeter.**

The unfinished chapter from Ejigbo to Attahiru. An independent
military airworthiness authority on the British model: inside the
defence establishment, outside the operating chain of command, with
published mishap summaries as its minimum transparency floor.

*Enemy:* the deepest in this book — the informational sovereignty of
the barracks. Sequence it accordingly: the transparency floor first,
the authority second.

No Nigerian aviation reform is complete while the state's own
aircraft answer to nobody.

**Seven. Rebuild the pipeline.**

Zaria re-equipped against a dated fleet-and-simulator plan.
Instructor pay unhooked from the civil service scale. A national
cadetship financing scheme, so that the pilot pipeline stops
depending on family wealth and diaspora remittance.

*Cost:* the agenda's largest line, and still small against one year
of fuel subsidy. *Enemy:* nobody — pipelines have no constituency
until they are empty.

**Eight. Publish everything.**

Registers, audit outcomes, inspection results, enforcement logs —
machine-readable, on the web, by default.

Transparency is the only regulator that works weekends.

**Nine. Manufacture the constituency.**

The binding constraint throughout this entire book has been the
absence of anyone to demand the other eight items between funerals.

So build the demand deliberately.

An annual, statutory State of Aviation Safety report, debated in
public session with the agency heads on the record — importing, on a
fixed calendar, the scrutiny that currently arrives only by
catastrophe.

A standing consumer-and-victims' advocate with docket standing
before the agencies: the institutionalized descendant of the Loyola
parents.

And continued, deliberate coupling to the external witnesses, whose
audits remain — until domestic demand matures — the system's rented
spine.

## THE ECONOMICS: What the page costs

Sum the program generously.

It remains below the published cost estimates of the stillborn
national carrier's launch.

Below — for a sharper comparison — the value of the foreign
airlines' ticket revenues trapped in Nigerian accounts during a
single bad year of the currency queue.

Against that stand the returns this Part has assembled: the treaty
discount on every financed airframe; the insurance re-rating that
follows investigative transparency; the route economics of a durable
Category 1; the hub revenues that Chapter 17 will quantify.

And the unquantified one. The funerals not held.

State the conclusion plainly, because the framing matters more than
the numbers:

*Nigerian aviation reform is not an expenditure competing with
schools and clinics. It is among the highest-yield fiscal
instruments the federation owns — mispriced for sixty years because
its dividends are paid in absences.*

The budget office that finally prices the absences will wonder what
the country thought it was saving.

## Who holds the pen

An agenda addressed to "Nigeria" fails. Each item has a desk, and
the desks have interests that can be engaged rather than exhorted.

**The presidency** owns what nothing else can move: the appointments
that make or unmake regulator tenure, the military perimeter, and
the political air cover that Chapter 9 proved is reform's actual
fuel. What it gets in exchange is what Obasanjo got — a legacy asset
that outlived every rival accomplishment of his final term. The
pitch to any Nigerian president is not safety. It is that aviation
reform is the republic's most reliably completable monument.

**The National Assembly** owns the statutes and the annual hearing.
Its interest is the one that 2002 discovered: aviation oversight is
high-salience, telegenic, and — unusually among committee subjects —
scored by external referees whose findings a legislator can
brandish.

**The industry** owns the commercial items and is the natural
financier of the advocacy the sector lacks. A trade association that
lobbied for *system quality* rather than against its own regulation
would be pursuing its members' lease rates, insurance premia, and
route rights by the most direct available path. Self-interest,
properly organized, is Chapter 15's third algebraic term. It has
simply never been organized.

**The professions** own the content — the reporting culture, the
pipeline design, the technical memory that has outlasted every
ministry in this book. Their instrument is credibility, and their
historic error has been spending it only on wages.

**And the families** own the conscience.

Every reform in this history moved when those five hands briefly
held the same pen.

The agenda's real sequencing, beneath the phases, is to get them to
the same table — and to keep a chair for the funerals'
representatives, so that the table never again needs new ones.

## The first hundred days

For concreteness, the opening program of a reforming administration
or a newly protected Director-General, sequenced for momentum rather
than for importance.

**Days one to thirty: the publications.** Order the recommendation
register, the certificate registry, the firefighting inspection
results, and the enforcement log onto the public web in whatever
state they currently exist. Imperfect data published beats perfect
data promised, and the act itself signals the era's terms.
Simultaneously: one unannounced firefighting audit at each
international airport, results published within the month. Cost:
server space and nerve.

**Days thirty to sixty: the clocks.** Agree and publish
interim-statement and docket timelines for all open investigations,
with the backlog scheduled and dated. Convene the first quarterly
safety-performance briefing — cameras present, agency heads on the
record, questions taken. Institutions learn tempo from the top's
calendar.

**Days sixty to one hundred: the entrenchments begun.** Transmit the
prepared bills — the just-culture shield, the family-assistance act,
the levy protections — while the honeymoon lasts. Announce the
inspector pay-parity mechanism and the diaspora recruitment round.
Commission the full-scale exercise at the busiest airport, and
attend it.

None of that completes anything.

All of it makes the program's reversal visible, and therefore
expensive — which the Demuren tenure demonstrated is the entire art
of the opening.

## THE ARGUMENT: The ways this fails

An agenda that cannot name its own failure modes is propaganda.
Here are this one's, with the mitigations that exist and the
residual risks that do not.

**It fails by consolidation of the wrong kind.** The treasury raid
dressed as fiscal discipline — the periodic drive to sweep agency
levies into the consolidated revenue fund, which would reverse item
two in a single appropriations act and re-create the begging
regulator of Regime One inside a budget cycle. The mitigation is
drafting altitude and noise. The residual risk is real: a
sufficiently determined fiscal center beats any statutory fence.
Which is what item nine is for.

**It fails by success.** The quiet decade lulling the very coalition
it should recruit. No funerals, no urgency. The annual hearing
degenerating into mutual congratulation; the drills becoming
pageants again. This is the plateau extending itself — the likeliest
single future — and its only structural antidote is the coupling of
safety to money, because where no one profits from vigilance,
vigilance is a mood, and moods end.

**It fails by federal drift.** The reform captured at the center
while the periphery — state airstrips, charter operators, general
aviation, the drone economy — grows faster than oversight reaches.
The 2013 charter crash and the 2021 military losses were peripheral
failures in precisely this sense. The periphery is where the next
Part II begins.

**And it fails by personalization.** The reform identified with a
champion, rising and falling with one career. This is the Demuren
pattern, which this book has praised and must therefore also flag as
the design's known vulnerability.

A reform is finished not when its champion wins.

It is finished when its champion becomes replaceable without event.

The dullest possible test — and the only one that matters.

The honest summary of the risk register: the engineering is solved.
The politics is unsolved. And the politics is the project.

## THE ARGUMENT: What trust actually is

"Rebuilding trust" is the phrase every Nigerian aviation roadmap
uses, and before this chapter closes the phrase should be cashed
out, because trust in a technical system is not a sentiment.

It is an inference from evidence. And it can be specified.

A passenger trusts an aviation system when she can reason — even
implicitly — through four propositions:

*The people operating this machine are qualified by an examiner who
cannot be bought.*

*The machine is maintained on a schedule that someone independent
verifies.*

*If something goes wrong tonight, trained help will arrive inside
the survival window.*

*And if the worst happens, the cause will be found, published, and
acted upon.*

Four inferences. Competence, verification, response, learning.

Each rests on an institution. Each is checkable in principle.

The Nigerian public of 1996 could make none of the four.

The public of the quiet decade can make perhaps two and a half —
which is exactly why its trust remains provisional, cheap to shake,
and expressed in the dark humor that has always been the Nigerian
passenger's actuarial instrument.

The agenda on the preceding pages is, item by item, a machine for
making those four inferences severally true and severally
*visible*.

The publication clauses matter as much as the substance, because an
inference requires evidence, and invisible rigor builds no trust at
all.

And the same specification explains why trust, once built, is an
economic asset of the first order.

Aviation is the advance purchase of a promise. Every doubt about the
promise is priced: in demand suppressed, in the insurance and lease
premia this book has tracked, in the diversion of the country's own
elite to foreign carriers, and in the transfer traffic that connects
through Addis Ababa because a connection through Lagos still carries
a discount for uncertainty.

A single percentage point of restored trust is worth more to
Nigerian aviation's growth arithmetic than any terminal ever
commissioned.

Governments understand buildings.

The case this chapter has tried to make is that trust is also
infrastructure.

The only kind that compounds. The only kind that cannot be
commissioned with a ribbon.

And the only kind whose construction schedule lies entirely,
permanently, within the state's own control.
