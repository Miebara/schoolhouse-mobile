# 16. The Page

The reform agenda for Nigerian aviation fits on one page, and it has
fit on one page for thirty years. The page is not the problem.

Every element of what follows has appeared in some ministerial
roadmap, audit response, or national aviation policy since the
1990s, and several elements exist in law today. This chapter's
purpose is therefore not discovery but specification, with each item
stated alongside its mechanism, its cost, its owner, and its
predictable enemy, because an agenda without named enemies is a
communiqué.

And it opens with the era's great cautionary tale, which is the
project that consumed a decade of the ministry's attention while the
page went unexecuted.

## Farnborough, England, July 2018

The unveiling had everything. A stand at the world's most important
air show. A livery in green and white, with the national colors
sweeping the tail. A minister holding a scale model for the cameras.
Nigeria Air. The national carrier was returning, fifteen years after
Nigeria Airways had been liquidated with its workers unpaid.

What followed instead of an airline was five years of procurement
opera. The project was suspended within months and then revived. A
strategic partner was selected, and it was Ethiopian Airlines, the
mirror of Chapter 15 now summoned as vendor, chosen over the
objections and litigation of the domestic carriers' association,
whose members correctly understood that a subsidized state
competitor would destroy them.

And then, in May 2023, days before the administration left office,
came the launch. A Boeing 737 landed at Abuja in Nigeria Air colors.
It was an Ethiopian Airlines aircraft, chartered, repainted, and
flown in for the ceremony, and it flew home afterward.

The House of Representatives committee that investigated the project
rendered its verdict in the harshest available parliamentary
language. The succeeding administration suspended the project.
Anti-corruption authorities subsequently brought charges connected
to the ministry's projects, contested in court as this book went to
press, and on which this book takes no position. The livery survives
as a photograph.

## Four attempts, one template

Nigeria Air was not the first attempt to conjure a successor to
Nigeria Airways. It was the fourth, and four failures on a single
template constitute a dataset rather than a run of bad luck.

The first came before the old airline was formally dead. Around
2001, with Nigeria Airways in terminal decline, the aviation
minister of the day, Kema Chikwe, championed the flotation of a new
national carrier announced under the name Nigeria Global, which was
to rise alongside and then replace the dying flag. It was launched
with ministerial fanfare and it acquired the trappings of an
airline, meaning a name, designated aircraft arrangements, and
inaugural ambitions on the London route. It never became a
functioning carrier. It dissolved within a couple of years amid
controversy over its leasing arrangements and funding, leaving
behind legislative queries and a précis of every error the later
attempts would repeat.

The second was the era's most serious. Virgin Nigeria was launched
in 2004 and 2005 as a joint venture with Virgin Atlantic, the
British carrier supplying management, brand, and operational
standards. This was a deliberate borrowing of external institutional
quality on the Kenya Airways model, and it deserves credit, because
it flew and for a period it flew well. Then the arrangement met the
Nigerian political economy, through disputes over terminal
allocation, friction over brand and control, and the exit of Virgin,
followed by rebranding as Nigerian Eagle and then Air Nigeria under
new private ownership, and collapse into unpaid salaries and
grounding by 2012. The lesson is Chapter 15's in reverse, which is
that rented institutional quality works exactly as long as the
landlord state honors the lease.

The third was a succession of paper carriers and announcements
through the 2010s that never reached a runway.

And the fourth was Nigeria Air, the project of Hadi Sirika, minister
of state and then minister of aviation across the Buhari years, and
its most determined champion.

Four attempts, four ministries, one method. The airline summoned
from the ministry outward, capitalized in announcements, and
delivered to a state that had still not performed the unglamorous
repairs beneath it.

## Why the flag carrier keeps returning

The recurrence deserves explanation rather than mockery, because
four ministers across two decades and three parties are not simply
four fools. The flag carrier project has a political economy that
makes it almost irresistible, and understanding it is necessary to
resisting it.

Its benefits are concentrated, visible, and immediate, consisting of
a livery at an air show, a ribbon, a photograph on every front page,
national pride invoked at negligible marginal cost, and, in the
procurement itself, contracts with identifiable beneficiaries. Its
costs are diffuse, delayed, and technical, consisting of the
competitive damage to existing carriers, the subsidy that will be
required indefinitely, and the opportunity cost of the ministerial
attention consumed. That is the identical asymmetry Chapter 8
identified in the economics of neglect, operating here in the
opposite direction to produce action rather than inaction, but on
the same principle, because politics systematically overweights the
visible.

There is a second driver, subtler and more sympathetic. The flag
carrier is legible as an achievement in a way that regulatory
capacity is not, because a minister can point to an aircraft and he
cannot point to a crash that did not happen. Which returns us to the
deepest structural finding in this book, which is that the things
that keep people alive in aviation are invisible, and the things
that are visible do not keep anyone alive.

The counter-model exists, is unglamorous, and is working one border
away. Ghana, after liquidation, grew its traffic on other people's
capital while investing its scarce public attention in its
regulator. Fly the flag on the certificate rather than the fuselage,
letting the state's insignia mean rigorous oversight and letting the
market own the metal. The applause line the politicians keep
searching for is Chapter 10's, which is a decade without funerals.

## The people the state forgot

There is one more piece of unfinished business from the first flag
carrier, and no honest account of Nigerian aviation omits it.

When Nigeria Airways was liquidated in 2003 and 2004 it carried
thousands of serving staff and a longer tail of retirees, including
pilots, engineers, cabin crew, and clerks, many with two and three
decades of service. Their severance, pensions, and accumulated
entitlements became claims against the estate of a dead airline
owned by the state that had killed it. The federal government
repeatedly promised settlement, and what followed was a generation
of waiting.

The ex-workers organized into associations of the retrenched. They
picketed the ministry and the National Assembly through successive
administrations. Their leaders aged into professional petitioners,
appearing before committee after committee with files of verified
entitlements. And the associations began keeping a second ledger,
which was the roll of members who had died waiting. Former captains
and licensed engineers finished their lives in penury and some were
reduced to public appeals for medical care. By the unions' counts,
never contradicted by better official data, the dead ran into the
thousands before the state substantially paid.

Movement came in 2017 and 2018, when the government approved a
settlement package reported at roughly forty-five billion naira,
with payments beginning in tranches from 2018 and verification
disputes and residual claims continuing for years afterward. The
payment was real, and so was its arithmetic. Settled some fifteen
years late, in a currency that had lost most of its value across the
interval, the entitlements delivered a fraction of the purchasing
power the workers had earned, and the difference was quietly
confiscated by time and inflation from the people least able to
absorb it.

That saga belongs in this chapter rather than in a footnote, because
its consequences operated forward, on the safety of the system.
Institutions run on deferred compensation, and the pension is its
purest form, in which one works now and is paid later on the
strength of an institutional promise. An employer who defaults on
the deferred teaches every current employee a lesson, and the lesson
is learned quickly and never unlearned, and the lesson is to demand
everything now or leave. Every serving professional in Nigerian
aviation watched, for twenty years, what the state's gratitude to
its aviation workforce looked like in practice, and priced the
observation.

The exodus that Chapter 3 attributed to Gulf salaries was propelled
by more than salaries. It was propelled by a demonstrated
contingency, which was the knowledge that a Nigerian aviation
career's terminal benefits were a lottery ticket drawn against the
treasury's attention span. Which produces a finding that no aviation
authority anywhere lists among its safety indicators and several
should. A state that defaults on its aviation pensions is degrading
its future inspectorate. The ex-workers of Nigeria Airways paid for
the airline's death twice, once in their livelihoods and once as the
cautionary tale that helped drain their successors abroad.

## The page

The first item is to finish the investigator. The safety
investigation bureau has the statute, so give it the substance,
meaning a statutory publication clock requiring an interim statement
within thirty days and a public docket within a year, a
recommendation register held in public in which every addressee must
answer within ninety days by accepting the recommendation with
funding and a date or refusing it with reasons, and the just-culture
shield drafted in Chapter 14. The cost is trivial, the owners are
the bureau's board and the National Assembly for the shield, and the
enemy is the blame system, which is to say everyone with something
pending. This is the keystone, because it is cheap, it compounds,
and the honesty of every other item depends on it.

The second is to ring-fence the regulator, permanently this time.
The 2006 settlement has eroded at the margins, with treasury
consolidation drives eyeing the levies and appointment politics
reclaiming the leadership. Restore and entrench it, with levies
protected at the highest available legal altitude, the
Director-General appointed from a professionally qualified shortlist
to protected tenure, staggered board terms that no single
administration can sweep, and technical salaries benchmarked to the
market that actually bids for inspectors, which is the Gulf and the
international agencies rather than the civil service scale. The cost
is self-funding from existing levies, and the enemy is every future
minister of aviation, structurally, since the item exists to outlive
them.

The third is to fill the trucks and run the drills, which is Chapter
13's package verbatim. Firefighting substance audited unannounced.
One full-scale mass-casualty exercise per international airport per
year with hospitals in the loop and after-action findings published.
A national rescue coordination center with a tested contact tree.
Manifest integrity enforced digitally. A family-assistance act. The
cost is pocket change against any terminal project, and the enemy is
nobody, which is precisely why it starves, so assign a single
accountable coordinator by statute.

The fourth is to keep capital cheap by making promises legible. That
means full performance of the aircraft-finance treaty Nigeria has
ratified, with repossession timelines guaranteed through a
designated commercial court list and registry operations at world
standard, which recaptures the financing discount for every Nigerian
lessee. Add transparent slot and route allocation so carriers
compete on operations rather than on access. The cost is
administrative and the payoff is basis points on every financed
airframe, which is the cheapest fleet-safety money in existence.

The fifth is to consolidate without cartelizing, through higher
capital floors and honest mergers paired with continental open skies
so that consolidation's pricing power is disciplined by competition
rather than by a regulator who will lose that fight. Add resolution
planning for the carriers now too large to ground, which is Chapter
12's living will, so that the safety decision is never again hostage
to the schedule decision.

The sixth is to bring the military inside the perimeter, which is
the unfinished chapter running from Ejigbo to Attahiru. That means
an independent military airworthiness authority on the British
model, sitting inside the defence establishment but outside the
operating chain of command, with published mishap summaries as its
minimum transparency floor. The enemy is the deepest in this book,
being the informational sovereignty of the barracks, so sequence it
accordingly, with the transparency floor first and the authority
second. No Nigerian aviation reform is complete while the state's
own aircraft answer to nobody.

The seventh is to rebuild the pipeline, with Zaria re-equipped
against a dated fleet-and-simulator plan, instructor pay unhooked
from the civil service scale, and a national cadetship financing
scheme so that the pilot pipeline stops depending on family wealth
and diaspora remittance. The cost is the agenda's largest line and
is still small against one year of fuel subsidy, and the enemy is
nobody, because pipelines have no constituency until they are empty.

The eighth is to publish everything, meaning registers, audit
outcomes, inspection results, and enforcement logs, machine-readable
and on the web by default, because transparency is the only
regulator that works weekends.

The ninth is to manufacture the constituency. The binding constraint
throughout this entire book has been the absence of anyone to demand
the other eight items between funerals, so build the demand
deliberately. That means an annual, statutory State of Aviation
Safety report debated in public session with the agency heads on the
record, importing on a fixed calendar the scrutiny that currently
arrives only by catastrophe. It means a standing consumer and
victims' advocate with docket standing before the agencies, as the
institutionalized descendant of the Loyola parents. And it means
continued, deliberate coupling to the external witnesses, whose
audits remain, until domestic demand matures, the system's rented
spine.

## What the page costs

Sum the program generously and it remains below the published cost
estimates of the stillborn national carrier's launch. It remains
below, for a sharper comparison, the value of the foreign airlines'
ticket revenues trapped in Nigerian accounts during a single bad
year of the currency queue, which ran to several hundred million
dollars.

Against that stand the returns this Part has assembled, which are
the treaty discount on every financed airframe, the insurance
re-rating that follows investigative transparency, the route
economics of a durable Category 1, and the hub revenues that Chapter
17 will quantify. And there is the unquantified one, which is the
funerals not held.

State the conclusion plainly, because the framing matters more than
the numbers. Nigerian aviation reform is not an expenditure
competing with schools and clinics. It is among the highest-yield
fiscal instruments the federation owns, mispriced for sixty years
because its dividends are paid in absences. The budget office that
finally prices the absences will wonder what the country thought it
was saving.

## Who holds the pen

An agenda addressed to Nigeria fails. Each item has a desk, and the
desks have interests that can be engaged rather than exhorted.

The presidency owns what nothing else can move, meaning the
appointments that make or unmake regulator tenure, the military
perimeter, and the political air cover that Chapter 9 proved is
reform's actual fuel. What it gets in exchange is what Obasanjo got,
which is a legacy asset that outlived every rival accomplishment of
his final term. The pitch to any Nigerian president is not safety.
It is that aviation reform is the republic's most reliably
completable monument.

The National Assembly owns the statutes and the annual hearing, and
its interest is the one that 2002 discovered, which is that aviation
oversight is high-salience, telegenic, and, unusually among
committee subjects, scored by external referees whose findings a
legislator can brandish.

The industry owns the commercial items and is the natural financier
of the advocacy the sector lacks. A trade association that lobbied
for system quality rather than against its own regulation would be
pursuing its members' lease rates, insurance premia, and route
rights by the most direct available path. Self-interest, properly
organized, is Chapter 15's third algebraic term, and it has simply
never been organized.

The professions own the content, meaning the reporting culture, the
pipeline design, and the technical memory that has outlasted every
ministry in this book. Their instrument is credibility, and their
historic error has been spending it only on wages.

And the families own the conscience.

Every reform in this history moved when those five hands briefly
held the same pen. The agenda's real sequencing, beneath the phases,
is to get them to the same table, and to keep a chair for the
funerals' representatives so that the table never again needs new
ones.

## The first hundred days

For concreteness, here is the opening program of a reforming
administration or a newly protected Director-General, sequenced for
momentum rather than for importance.

In the first thirty days come the publications. Order the
recommendation register, the certificate registry, the firefighting
inspection results, and the enforcement log onto the public web in
whatever state they currently exist, because imperfect data
published beats perfect data promised and the act itself signals the
era's terms. Simultaneously, run one unannounced firefighting audit
at each international airport with results published within the
month. The cost is server space and nerve.

In the second thirty days come the clocks. Agree and publish
interim-statement and docket timelines for all open investigations,
with the backlog scheduled and dated. Convene the first quarterly
safety-performance briefing with cameras present, agency heads on
the record, and questions taken, because institutions learn tempo
from the top's calendar.

In the last forty days the entrenchments begin. Transmit the
prepared bills, being the just-culture shield, the family-assistance
act, and the levy protections, while the honeymoon lasts. Announce
the inspector pay-parity mechanism and the diaspora recruitment
round. Commission the full-scale exercise at the busiest airport,
and attend it.

None of that completes anything. All of it makes the program's
reversal visible and therefore expensive, which the Demuren tenure
demonstrated is the entire art of the opening.

## The ways this fails

An agenda that cannot name its own failure modes is propaganda, so
here are this one's, with the mitigations that exist and the
residual risks that do not.

It fails by consolidation of the wrong kind, through the treasury
raid dressed as fiscal discipline, meaning the periodic drive to
sweep agency levies into the consolidated revenue fund, which would
reverse the second item in a single appropriations act and re-create
the begging regulator of the first regime inside a budget cycle. The
mitigation is drafting altitude and noise, and the residual risk is
real, because a sufficiently determined fiscal center beats any
statutory fence, which is what the ninth item is for.

It fails by success, with the quiet decade lulling the very
coalition it should recruit. No funerals means no urgency, the
annual hearing degenerates into mutual congratulation, and the
drills become pageants again. This is the plateau extending itself,
which is the likeliest single future, and its only structural
antidote is the coupling of safety to money, because where no one
profits from vigilance, vigilance is a mood, and moods end.

It fails by federal drift, with the reform captured at the center
while the periphery of state airstrips, charter operators, general
aviation, and the drone economy grows faster than oversight reaches.
The 2013 charter crash and the 2021 military losses were peripheral
failures in precisely this sense, and the periphery is where the
next Part II begins.

And it fails by personalization, with the reform identified with a
champion and rising and falling with one career. This is the Demuren
pattern, which this book has praised and must therefore also flag as
the design's known vulnerability. A reform is finished not when its
champion wins. It is finished when its champion becomes replaceable
without event, which is the dullest possible test and the only one
that matters.

The honest summary of the risk register is that the engineering is
solved, the politics is unsolved, and the politics is the project.

## What trust actually is

Rebuilding trust is the phrase every Nigerian aviation roadmap uses,
and before this chapter closes the phrase should be cashed out,
because trust in a technical system is not a sentiment. It is an
inference from evidence, and it can be specified.

A passenger trusts an aviation system when she can reason, even
implicitly, through four propositions. That the people operating
this machine are qualified by an examiner who cannot be bought. That
the machine is maintained on a schedule someone independent
verifies. That if something goes wrong tonight, trained help will
arrive inside the survival window. And that if the worst happens,
the cause will be found, published, and acted upon. Four inferences,
covering competence, verification, response, and learning, each
resting on an institution and each checkable in principle.

The Nigerian public of 1996 could make none of the four. The public
of the quiet decade can make perhaps two and a half, which is
exactly why its trust remains provisional, cheap to shake, and
expressed in the dark humor that has always been the Nigerian
passenger's actuarial instrument.

The agenda on the preceding pages is, item by item, a machine for
making those four inferences severally true and severally visible.
The publication clauses matter as much as the substance, because an
inference requires evidence and invisible rigor builds no trust at
all.

The same specification explains why trust, once built, is an
economic asset of the first order. Aviation is the advance purchase
of a promise, and every doubt about the promise is priced, in demand
suppressed, in the insurance and lease premia this book has tracked,
in the diversion of the country's own elite to foreign carriers, and
in the transfer traffic that connects through Addis Ababa because a
connection through Lagos still carries a discount for uncertainty. A
single percentage point of restored trust is worth more to Nigerian
aviation's growth arithmetic than any terminal ever commissioned.

Governments understand buildings. The case this chapter has tried to
make is that trust is also infrastructure. It is the only kind that
compounds, the only kind that cannot be commissioned with a ribbon,
and the only kind whose construction schedule lies entirely and
permanently within the state's own control.
