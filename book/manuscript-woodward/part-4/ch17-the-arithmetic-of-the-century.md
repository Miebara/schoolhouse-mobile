# 17. The Arithmetic of the Century

## What the forecasts actually say

Every forecast about African aviation is a forecast about Nigeria
wearing a disguise.

Open any of the industry's twenty-year outlooks, whether the
manufacturers' market forecasts, the airport traffic projections, or
the demographic tables from which all of them are ultimately
derived, and the same shape emerges. The mature aviation markets are
flattening. Asia's great boom is maturing. And the steepest
remaining curve on every chart belongs to Africa, a continent whose
population will approach two and a half billion by mid-century,
urbanizing at historic speed, with a ground-transport deficit that
makes it, like Indonesia's archipelago, a geography that flight does
not merely serve but completes.

Within that curve, one country is the curve. On the central
projections Nigeria will be the third most populous nation on earth
by the 2050s, and its largest city will contend for the largest on
the planet. And Nigerians today board aircraft at a small fraction
of world-average rates, taking something on the order of one trip
per twenty people per year against a world average many times
higher, which is what makes the arithmetic almost embarrassing to
type.

Because the phrase growth market anesthetizes, spell it out in the
only units that matter. Take Nigerian air travel propensity,
measured as trips per person per year, and hold it merely to the
trajectory that middle-income countries have actually walked.
Indonesia's own path is the natural template, and Indonesia is not a
fantasy comparison, being a country of similar population scale,
similar income trajectory, and similar geographic necessity. Apply
that trajectory to a population heading past three hundred million
and Nigerian air traffic multiplies severalfold within a generation,
adding tens of millions of annual passengers, thousands of daily
movements, second airports for Lagos and Abuja, and a cargo economy
in perishables, e-commerce, and pharmaceuticals that barely exists
today. The manufacturers' twenty-year forecasts for Africa, which
call for well over a thousand new airliners with West Africa's share
concentrated in one country, are conservative translations of
demography into seats.

None of this requires Nigerian policy to succeed. The traffic comes
anyway, on someone's metal, through someone's hub, and that is
precisely the point. Demand is destiny and supply is the choice.

## What growth does to a plateau

Now run that growth against the system Chapter 10 described, because
this is the calculation that should keep the regulator awake.

Surveillance is a coverage function. An inspectorate sized for
today's movements, subjected to three times the movements, is not
three times as stressed. Coverage failures compound the way the
emergency-response stages of Chapter 13 multiplied, because each
uninspected operator, each unaudited maintenance organization, and
each unexamined new entrant is not an additive gap but a
multiplicative one in the probability chain. Which means the margin
the reform era banked is, on the plateau path, an asset being drawn
down at a rate set by traffic growth itself. At five percent annual
traffic growth against a frozen headcount, effective coverage falls
by roughly forty percent in a decade without a single decision being
taken to reduce it.

The quiet decade, extended mechanically into the loud decades ahead,
is not a forecast of safety. It is a forecast of the gap between
load and capacity, widening annually and silently, in exactly the
pattern every chapter of Part II began with.

## The hub Nigeria has never played

Geography has dealt Nigeria a hand it has never played, and the
economics of that hand deserve to be laid out, because they are the
strongest commercial argument for everything in Chapter 16.

Lagos sits at the hinge of the Gulf of Guinea, positioned for the
Atlantic crossings to Brazil and North America, positioned on the
great north-south trunk of the continent, and sitting inside a
natural catchment of some four hundred million people within two
hours' flying, which is a catchment that exceeds Addis Ababa's own.

The hub prize is not vanity, and it is worth understanding why hubs
matter economically rather than symbolically. A hub concentrates. It
concentrates maintenance bases, because aircraft overnight where
they are based. It concentrates training academies, because crews
live where the base is. It concentrates cargo logistics, ground
handling, catering, and avionics shops, which together form the
entire high-wage technical employment cluster that this book has
called the promise-keeping economy. And it concentrates connectivity
itself, which is a productivity input for every other industry in
the country.

Today the West African hub is functionally distributed among Addis
Ababa, Istanbul, Dubai, Paris, and Lomé, and each of them carries
Nigerian traffic that Nigerian institutions forfeited, in the flag
carrier's collapse, in the bilateral giveaways of the lean decades
described in Chapter 2, in every audit cycle that priced Nigerian
operations above their neighbors', and in the terminal experience
that Chapter 2's mirror described.

The hub question is therefore this entire book restated as
economics. The prize goes to the jurisdiction whose system of
customs, transfers, security, reliability, and safety can be trusted
at scale, and systems are what this history has been about. Ethiopia
built one on a fraction of Nigeria's endowment. The endowment waits.

## The continental stage

Nigeria's aviation century will be played on a stage the previous
century never built, and the politics of that stage deserve a
section.

The Single African Air Transport Market, launched under the African
Union in 2018 to operationalize an open-skies decision that had
stalled since 1999, proposes the aviation equivalent of a
continental free trade area, in which any African carrier may fly
between any African cities without the bilateral route-by-route
haggling that has kept intra-African connectivity scarcer and
costlier than intercontinental links. The economics are not
seriously contested, because study after study finds liberalization
multiplying traffic, cutting fares substantially on liberalized
routes, and creating the network density that makes hubs,
maintenance industries, and training economies viable.

The politics are entirely contested, because open skies reward the
prepared, meaning the Ethiopians with a mega-hub and a fleet already
built for continental reach, and expose the unprepared, whose
domestic markets are opened faster than their carriers can compete
for them. That calculation is precisely what has kept Nigeria, a
signatory, hedging in practice, protecting its carriers' domestic
revenues while its international traffic, which is the richest
origin-and-destination market in West Africa, enriches everyone
else's hubs.

The hedge is understandable, and on this book's evidence it is also
backward. Protection has never built a competitive Nigerian carrier,
and six decades of it built Nigeria Airways and the graveyard of
Chapter 12. What built everything that currently works was exposure
to standards, through the Category 1 campaign, the international
operational audit, and the lessors' covenants.

The continental decision is the same choice at larger scale, which
is whether Nigerian aviation's future is negotiated as a rent to be
defended or as a market to be won. A Nigeria that chose the second,
pairing open skies with the Chapter 16 agenda and offering the
continent's carriers its traffic in exchange for its own carriers'
right to compete everywhere, would be placing the bet Ethiopia
placed in 1945 and Indonesia placed in 2007, which is that its
people's competence, given institutions, can win on merit. Two
hundred million passengers' worth of gravity says the bet is
underpriced.

## The new weather

Two forces absent from this book's history will shape its sequel.

The first is technology's changed direction. For the century this
book has covered, aviation technology flowed one way, being designed
in the strong-institution world and absorbed later and older
everywhere else, which is the sorting machine of Chapter 12. The
coming decades scramble that pattern at the edges, and each scramble
is an opportunity and a hazard at once. Unmanned systems are already
outrunning the regulatory apparatus built for crewed flight, and
their accident physics, being cheap, numerous, low-altitude, and
over cities, will stress exactly the capacities this book found
thinnest, which are surveillance, investigation tempo, and
third-party liability. Electric and hybrid regional aircraft, if
their economics mature, suit the African thin-route problem almost
perfectly, and they will arrive as new technology available to first
movers rather than as hand-me-downs, which is an inversion of the
entire second-hand history told in these pages, and the countries
positioning for it are doing so now. And the data revolution in
safety, with satellite-based surveillance ending the radar-coverage
problem outright and flight-data streaming ending the lost-recorder
problem, cheapens precisely the infrastructure whose procurement
sagas filled Chapter 6. None of it self-installs, and each item
lands, or fails to, on the same institutional soil as everything
else in this book. But the soil requirements have dropped, because
for the first time the frontier is affordable at Nigerian budget
levels, and the excuse inventory shrinks annually.

The second force is climate. Aviation's carbon politics will price
and constrain the industry's growth everywhere, and Africa's
position in that bargaining, holding about one percent of historical
aviation emissions alongside the steepest legitimate growth need,
will be a defining diplomatic file. Nigeria, as the demographic
principal, holds the continent's proxy whether it prepares or not.
Meanwhile the operational climate itself turns hostile in exactly
the dimensions this book's crashes made familiar. The convective
weather of Sosoliso and Flight 053 intensifies on every credible
projection. The dust regimes shift. And the coastal infrastructure,
with Lagos's runways sitting meters above a rising lagoon, enters
the exposure tables. The windshear decision that killed a Sultan
will be presented to Nigerian crews more often in 2050 than it was
in 2006, and whether it kills again is, as ever, not a weather
question.

## The generation

The people who will run the Nigerian century in the air are alive
now, mostly under thirty, and they arrive with assets no previous
Nigerian aviation generation possessed.

They arrive into a functioning core, because the cadet who joins the
industry in the 2020s enters a system with radar, a real regulator,
published regulations, and a domestic safety record her instructors
could not have imagined at her age. It is a baseline rather than a
battlefield. They arrive networked, because the diaspora that
drained the 1980s system now functions, in the era of cheap
connection, as an accessible faculty, with the training captain in
Doha mentoring Lagos cadets by video, the inspector benchmarking
with counterparts she met on attachment, and the associations'
webinars erasing the isolation in which the 1990s workforce fought
its lonely arguments. And they arrive with the archive in their
pockets, because the smartphone-documented, freedom-of-information,
published-report environment means that the next generation's
version of this history is checkable from a dormitory, and a
generation that can check is much harder to govern by liturgy.

What they do not arrive with is the memory. The last Nigerians who
watched the whole arc, from Zaria's founding through the boom's
waste, the canal, the lagoon, the school, and the reform, are
leaving the industry and the world, and several of the people whose
recollections inform this book are in their eighties. Memory does
not transfer by inheritance. It transfers by institution, through
the archive, the curriculum, the memorial, and the annual hearing,
or it does not transfer at all, and Part III measured exactly how
little of that machinery exists.

Which identifies the gravest risk this generation faces, and it is
not the plateau, the currency, or the ministry. It is inheriting
quiet skies without the invoice, and mistaking, as their
grandparents' politicians did in 1975, the artifacts for the system.

## The case against hope, answered

It is customary for books like this one to end in optimism, and the
custom has usually embarrassed the books. So state the pessimist's
case at its strongest first, because it is not cynicism but pattern
recognition.

Every argument this chapter has made from demography and interest
was equally available in 1960, when a new nation with a natural
aviation market bought a flag carrier, and in 1977, when oil money
made anything possible, and in 2010, when Category 1 arrived and the
skies went quiet. Each time the structural tailwinds blew, and each
time the institutional sails were not raised, or were raised and
then struck. The pessimist does not deny that reform is possible. He
observes that in sixty years it has been sustained for roughly
eight, and asks why the base rate should not govern the forecast.

It is a serious argument, and here is the answer. Three parameters
have changed that the base rate does not price.

The first is the information environment. Every prior relapse in
this book happened substantially in the dark, and the plateau of the
2020s is the first to occur under continuous public documentation,
through smartphone archives, freedom-of-information law, and a
bureau that publishes. Decay that must happen in daylight happens
slower and costs more.

The second is the interest structure. For the first time the growth
arithmetic makes safety directly and visibly profitable to actors
with power, through fleets financed on covenants that price the
system's integrity, a hub prize that is contested and lucrative, and
an industry in which someone powerful now loses money when the
system rots. That is the coalition Chapter 15 identified as the
third algebraic term, and it did not exist in 1977.

The third is the demonstration stock. The optimists of 1960 had
theory and the optimists of 2010 had one turnaround, while today's
have a shelf that includes Nigeria in 2006, Indonesia, Korea, Ghana,
and the offshore enclave, all of them executed, documented, and
imitable repairs, two of them performed by Nigerians on Nigerian
institutions within living memory.

Base rates govern systems whose parameters are static. The
pessimist's pattern was real and its parameters are moving. That
does not guarantee the second act. It reprices it, from miracle to
decision.

## Two mornings in 2050

Since this entire book has argued that the future is a choice, let
the choice be rendered as the essayist's instrument permits, in two
mornings, in the same city, on the same date, a generation out.

On the first morning, Lagos's second airport, the seaside field that
the 2030s finally built with its safety zones acquired at greenfield
prices before the city arrived, turns its hourly bank of departures.
A regional jet loads for Douala. The six o'clock wave to Accra,
Abidjan, and Dakar boards under continental tariffs that made the
West African shuttle as ordinary as a bus. Across the field the
maintenance campus works the night's heavy checks, and its anchor
tenant is the successor of today's carriers while its second tenant
is Ethiopian, paying rent in Lagos now. In the tower, the watch
supervisor is a woman from Kano whose grandmother watched an
aircraft come down over Gwammaja. The morning's safety bulletin,
pushed to every operations room in the federation, reviews last
month's runway-excursion drill at Port Harcourt. Nobody in the
terminal thinks about any of this, and that is the achievement,
because the system has become boring, and the boring system carries
two hundred million passengers a year.

On the second morning, the same date arrives with the plateau
extended. The traffic doubled anyway, because demography does not
wait, onto the old airports, the old inspectorate, and the old
response archipelago. The fleet is younger than 1995's and older
than 2025's, because the financing dried when the treaty performance
slipped. The inspectorate's vacancy rate is a standing committee
topic and the bureau's backlog is measured in years again. And
somewhere in the system, on this particular morning, a dispatcher is
weighing a thunderstorm line against a schedule, an engineer is
deferring a finding within the letter of a rule, and a fire tender's
pump is awaiting a part. The holes are drifting, as they drifted
through 1991 and 2005, toward alignment, and on this morning the
alignment completes. The liturgy resumes, with mourning, probe,
panel, and promises, in high definition, before an audience of three
hundred million, over the graves of people not yet born on the day
this book was finished.

Both mornings are fully specified in the chapters behind this page,
and neither requires a miracle. Each requires only that the
decisions of the intervening nine thousand mornings lean, on
average, one way or the other.

## Seat 14A, again

Return to the evening flight of the prologue. Abuja to Lagos, the
darkened middle country scrolling beneath the wing, the orange
horizon of the delta, and the improvised electric sea of Lagos
rising to meet the descent.

The view has not changed and the question has. It was how a nation
that cannot keep its lights on kept its airplanes up, and the answer
is now assembled. Because for one compelled interval it built
arrangements that were funded, tenured, witnessed, and published,
and those arrangements let its ordinary competence accumulate. And
everything before that interval, the whole broken-winged chronicle
of Kano and Ejigbo and the lagoon and Lisa and Port Harcourt and
Iju-Ishaga, is what the same competence produced when the
arrangements forbade it to accumulate.

Institutions are how a society remembers, and they are also, in the
same sentence, how a society intends.

Somewhere below the wing, in the dark between the cities, are the
places this book has visited, unmarked in most cases, privately
mourned, and publicly forgotten. And ahead, past the seatbelt chime
and the humid jetbridge air, is the century in which more Nigerians
will fly than all the dead of this history ever dreamed.
Schoolchildren to Port Harcourt at Christmas. Pilgrims to Jeddah in
the white of ihram. Officers to Kaduna. Traders to Kano. The whole
nation airborne at last, as the founders' flypast promised in 1960.

They will board trusting, as passengers everywhere trust, that the
system beneath them is being maintained by people they will never
meet, against pressures they will never see, in offices they will
never enter. That trust is either the next chapter of the reform or
the first chapter of another book like this one, and Nigeria has now
run both experiments and knows both prices.

Broken wings mend. They are mended every day, in workshops, by
method, on schedules. The question, and it is the only question
there ever was, is whether the mending is treated as an event to be
commemorated, or as what it has always actually been, which is a
discipline, daily, unglamorous, and owed.
