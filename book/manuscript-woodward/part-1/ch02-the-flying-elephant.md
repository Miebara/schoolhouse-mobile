# 2. The Flying Elephant

## Ikeja, Lagos, 1974

The engineer, whom I will call Emeka and who is one of a small
number of composite figures in this book, assembled from the
accounts of several men who held his job, remembers the walk across
the apron on his first morning. The tails in green and white. The
elephant with wings painted on the fin. Seven aircraft that day, he
thinks, though he says he may be adding one from memory.

He was twenty-six. The country was fourteen.

"You have to understand what it meant," he said. "We were not
working for a company. We were working for the proof."

The proof of what?

"That it was ours. That we could do it." He was quiet for a moment.
"We could do it. That was never the problem. Nobody ever understood
that the doing was never the problem."

## The most expensive symbol a poor country can own

Every new nation buys three things first, which are a flag, an
anthem, and an airline. The flag is cheap and the anthem is free.

Nigeria Airways was created in 1958 out of the dissolving West
African corporation, in transitional partnership with British firms,
and bought out fully by 1961 so that the flag would be clean. Its
aircraft flew to London and, in the boom years, to New York. Its
logo was an elephant with wings, chosen one assumes for majesty
rather than aerodynamics, though history would have its joke either
way.

The dream had hard logic beneath the sentiment, and it deserves to
be taken seriously before it is criticized. Nigeria at independence
had fifty-five million people spread across a territory twice the
size of California, with three great population centers, poor roads,
and a seasonal river system. It was a natural aviation market if
anywhere in Africa was. A national carrier promised connectivity
that the railways could not deliver, foreign exchange, a training
ladder into modern technical employment, and presence, since in 1960
an airline was how a new state introduced itself to the world. Nor
was state ownership eccentric. In 1960 nearly every airline on earth
outside the United States belonged to a government.

The fork in the road ran somewhere else entirely, and nobody put it
to a vote.

## Two ways to own an airline

A state-owned airline can be governed in one of two ways, and the
choice is invisible on the balance sheet in year one and decisive by
year twenty.

In the first model the airline is an enterprise. The nation owns the
equity and the government behaves as a shareholder, appointing a
board, setting a mandate, and then subjecting the firm to a hard
budget constraint. Management is recruited for competence and fired
for performance. Route decisions are made on contribution margin.
Losses are a signal rather than a subsidy line.

In the second model the airline is an annex of the state. Jobs are
patronage, routes are diplomacy, and aircraft purchases are
procurement politics. Management is appointed the way permanent
secretaries are appointed and removed the way ministers are removed.
Losses are absorbed, so they carry no information at all.

Ethiopian Airlines was deliberately built on the first model in
1945, under an American management contract with an explicit mandate
to train Ethiopians into every seat, and successive Ethiopian
regimes of wildly different ideologies defended that structure as
too valuable to raid. Nigeria Airways drifted onto the second. Not
at once, and never irreversibly, but steadily. The economics of that
drift can be read in three numbers, none of which appeared in any
annual report as a warning.

The first is the number of aircraft types in the fleet. By 1980
Nigeria Airways operated roughly a dozen distinct types across a
fleet of about thirty aircraft, a ratio that is not merely
inefficient but close to unheard of. Commercial airlines pursue
fleet commonality almost religiously, because every additional type
multiplies costs that are invisible to passengers and enormous in
aggregate, including separate spare parts inventories, separate
simulator arrangements, separate engineering certifications,
separate crew qualifications, and separate maintenance manuals. The
most profitable airlines in the world have historically flown one or
two types across hundreds of aircraft, which is a ratio of hundreds
of airframes per type. Nigeria Airways was running fewer than three.
A dozen types across thirty aircraft is not a fleet. It is a
collection, and fleets look like that when acquisitions are decided
as diplomacy and finance-ministry theater rather than as fleet
planning, a wide-body because flags need one and a European aircraft
because Europe called. Complexity was purchased at boom prices and
would have to be maintained at bust prices.

The second number is chief executive tenure. Across its life the
airline ran through more than twenty chief executives, several of
whom lasted months, which works out to an average tenure of roughly
two years against a heavy maintenance planning cycle that runs to
five or six. Consider what that does to an engineering department. A
spares pipeline requires supplier relationships built over years. A
training pipeline pays back over a decade. Every one of those
requires an institution capable of intending something for longer
than its leadership survives. Each new chief executive owed his seat
to a patron, and each froze or reversed his predecessor's
arrangements, so the procurement pipeline, which is the dull artery
through which an airline's future flows, was severed and re-plumbed
every two or three years. Churn is what patronage looks like when
you plot it on an organization chart.

The third number is employees per aircraft. The airline's workforce
swelled to several times industry norms on that measure, reaching a
ratio that would have been remarkable at a legacy European carrier
and was ruinous at a thirty-aircraft operator in a devaluing
currency. It had become, in effect, a federal employment program
with a flying division. Be careful here, because the individuals
were not the problem. The airline contained to its last day
engineers and pilots of world caliber, as its diaspora would shortly
prove by staffing other countries' airlines. The problem was
arithmetic. An airline that exists partly to distribute jobs cannot
shrink to its revenue. It cannot pay competitively at the top of its
technical grades, because the payroll is consumed at the bottom. And
it cannot say no. An organization that cannot say no is accumulating
risk somewhere, and in aviation it accumulates in the hangar.

## The private subscription service

What did the drift feel like from inside a cockpit?

The senior pilots of that era describe, with a consistency that
survives every retelling, a calculation performed privately at the
head of the jetway. There was the trip offered with a deferred
defect list that would have grounded the aircraft on a neighboring
registry. There was the roster torn up overnight because a VIP
charter had commandeered the aircraft. There was the simulator
session postponed for a budget season. Which compromises would a
captain carry into the air, and which would he refuse at the cost of
a summons to headquarters?

"You were not flying for the airline," one retired captain of that
generation put it. "You were flying against it. Every day, a
little."

By the late boom, the safety of Nigeria's national carrier had
become a private subscription service, operated by its professionals
against the drift of their employer. This is a more important
observation than it first appears, and it recurs in every chapter of
this book, so it is worth stating as a principle. A system that runs
on individual heroism is a system spending margins it does not own.

Heroism keeps the schedule alive, and by keeping the schedule alive
it hides the decay from the public, from the press, and from the
treasury, until the day the heroism is one contingency short. Nobody
can say in advance which day that will be. That is the entire
problem.

## What a symbol is for

There is a serious philosophical question buried in the flying
elephant, and dismissing it as vanity misses what was actually at
stake.

Symbols are not decoration in political life. They are coordination
devices. A flag, an anthem, and a national carrier give a new and
internally divided population something to point at and call
ourselves. Nigeria in 1960 was three regions, hundreds of languages,
and a constitution three years old. The case for spending real money
on symbols was not frivolous.

The problem is not that Nigeria bought a symbol. The problem is the
sequence. A symbol that crowns a working system compounds the
system's worth, because it recruits talent, attracts capital, and
creates a constituency that will defend the substance in order to
protect the emblem. A symbol procured in place of a system becomes,
in time, that system's epitaph, and it does something worse than
merely fail. It actively conceals the absence. The flag flies
whether or not anything beneath it works, and a symbol that cannot
fail is the perfect roof under which everything else can.

By the 1980s the joke had turned proverbial in Nigerian markets, to
the effect that elephants do not fly, and the airline died narrated
under elephant metaphors of increasing cruelty. Hold that lesson. In
July 2018, at an air show in England, a Nigerian minister would
unveil a livery for a new national carrier that did not yet exist,
would not exist five years later, and would be denounced by a
parliamentary committee in the harshest available language. The
columnists reached for the elephant within hours.

## Zaria, 1964

Fairness requires this section, because the independence generation
built one aviation institution on the right model, and its fate is
as instructive as the airline's.

In 1964, with United Nations and ICAO support, Nigeria opened the
Civil Aviation Training Centre at Zaria as a school for pilots, air
traffic controllers, and aircraft engineers. Zaria was everything
the airline was not. It was technically governed, internationally
supervised in its founding decade, selective, and oriented toward a
standard rather than a spectacle. It trained students from across
anglophone Africa. For a generation of African aviation
professionals the name Zaria carried the weight that Sandhurst
carries in another profession, and its alumni run through every
institution in this book, usually on the creditable side of the
ledger.

Zaria matters twice. First, it falsifies in advance the laziest
explanation for everything that follows, which is that Nigeria
somehow could not do aviation. Nigeria could and did, wherever the
institutional design insulated competence from politics, which meant
an entrance examination instead of a patron and a curriculum audited
from Montreal instead of a budget negotiated in Lagos.

Second, its later history proves the theorem's other half. Decades
of underfunding followed. Obsolete training aircraft. Instructors
lost first to airline pay and then to the Gulf. Periodic rescue,
followed by renewed decay. Founding design protects an institution
for a while, but only a political constituency protects it forever.
The airline had a constituency, consisting of everyone who could
extract from it. The training college had none. Where the extraction
was, the money went. A country's budget is its actual aviation
policy, whatever its gazette says.

## Kano, January 22, 1973

The Hajj is the largest annually recurring movement of human beings
on earth, and by the early 1970s Nigeria was among its largest
sources, moving tens of thousands of pilgrims in a season compressed
into a few weeks.

Hajj transport was aviation's strangest market. It presented a
colossal demand spike, a few weeks long, running in one direction at
a time, to a single destination, composed of passengers many of whom
had never flown and would never fly again. No airline on earth could
justify keeping a fleet sized for it. The universal solution, then
and now, was the charter, which meant hiring aircraft and crews from
wherever capacity could be found and surging the pilgrims across the
Red Sea and home again.

On January 22, 1973, a Boeing 707 chartered from the Jordanian
carrier Alia for the Nigerian pilgrimage approached Kano at the end
of a flight from Jeddah. The winter Harmattan does to Kano what fog
does to London. The approach went wrong, and the aircraft came down
hard, broke, and burned. One hundred and seventy-six people died. At
that moment it was the deadliest crash in the history of civil
aviation anywhere in the world.

Sit with that fact, because it is genuinely strange. The deadliest
crash the world had yet seen involved Nigerian passengers, on
Nigerian soil, in the fifth year of the Nigerian boom, and it is
barely remembered today outside the families and the city that
buried the dead in long trenches while the north mourned through the
spring.

Part of the amnesia is general. The 1970s were world aviation's
bloodiest decade, and January's record was surpassed before the year
was out. Part of it is specific, and diagnostic. The aircraft was
Jordanian and the crew was foreign. The charter had been arranged
through the apparatus of the pilgrimage rather than through the
airline proper. Investigation belonged formally to Nigeria as the
state of occurrence, with Jordan and the manufacturer's state in
attendance. In the seams between all those jurisdictions,
accountability quietly bled out.

There was grief and there was no reckoning. No public report
Nigerians could read. No named institutional failure. No reform with
a date on it. The state's operative conclusion, as far as the record
shows, was that a foreign airline had crashed a foreign aircraft in
bad weather. An act of God with a Jordanian registration.

## The conclusion that was available

The other conclusion was available in 1973 and it did not require
hindsight. It was that Nigeria had built a system in which the most
dangerous flying its citizens did, meaning mass charters, surge
operations, unfamiliar crews, and minimal continuity, was precisely
the flying over which its own institutions exercised the least
control. A state whose citizens board aircraft it does not oversee
has not avoided responsibility. It has only deferred it.

That conclusion was not drawn. Eighteen years later, at Jeddah, in
an almost perfectly recurved echo involving a chartered foreign
aircraft, a pilgrimage operation, Nigerian passengers, and
institutional responsibility dissolving across three countries, the
deferred bill arrived with 261 names on it.

Institutions that do not digest their first warning are not granted
gentler second ones.

## What the oil paid for

The comparison worth making about the 1970s is one nobody at the
time knew they were running.

In Addis Ababa, Ethiopian Airlines spent the decade doing
unglamorous things. Its American management contract ended in 1971,
having produced the Ethiopianization it was designed to produce, and
the airline kept the systems, the academy, and the maintenance
culture. When revolution came in 1974 and a Marxist military junta
seized the state, nationalizing everything and shooting much of the
old elite, the new rulers left the airline's management
substantially alone. They had grasped that it was one of the very
few reliable hard-currency machines the state owned. The airline
flew through the terror, the famine, and the civil war. It made
money. It trained pilots that other African carriers borrowed.

The lesson is uncomfortable precisely because it removes every
excuse. Institutional protection is a political choice, available to
poor states and available even to brutal states, the moment a
sovereign decides that an institution is worth more functioning than
raided. Nigeria ran the opposite protocol with roughly ten times the
national revenue.

Here is the part that economists will recognize immediately. It was
not despite the oil. It was because of it. A state with petroleum
revenue does not need its airline to earn foreign exchange. The
recomputation in Addis Ababa always returned the instruction to
protect the machine, because the machine was one of the few sources
of dollars. The recomputation in Lagos always returned the other
answer, because the dollars came out of the ground. Oil was Nigerian
aviation's quiet curse, and it operated not through corruption but
through the arithmetic of alternatives.

The 1970s were the years in which all of this was affordable,
because oil paid for the slack. When maintenance was expensive, one
bought spares. When a route lost money, the treasury absorbed it.
When the pilgrimage needed lift, one chartered it. Boom-era
institutions are never tested by booms. The test comes when the
money stops, when the deferred maintenance, the deferred training,
and the deferred regulation must all be paid at once from an empty
account.

In 1981, the oil price broke.
