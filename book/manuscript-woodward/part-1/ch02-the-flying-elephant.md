# 2. The Flying Elephant

## Ikeja, Lagos, 1974

The engineer — I will call him Emeka, one of a small number of
composite figures in this book, assembled from the accounts of
several men who held his job — remembers the walk across the apron
on his first morning.

The tails in green and white. The elephant with wings painted on
the fin. Seven aircraft that day, he thinks, though he says he may
be adding one from memory.

He was twenty-six. The country was fourteen.

"You have to understand what it meant," he said. "We were not
working for a company. We were working for the proof."

The proof of what?

"That it was ours. That we could do it."

He was quiet for a moment.

"We could do it. That was never the problem. Nobody ever
understood that the doing was never the problem."

## The most expensive symbol a poor country can own

Every new nation buys three things first: a flag, an anthem, and an
airline. The flag is cheap. The anthem is free.

Nigeria Airways was created in 1958 out of the dissolving West
African corporation, in transitional partnership with British firms,
and bought out fully by 1961 so that the flag would be clean. Its
aircraft flew to London and, in the boom years, to New York. Its
logo was an elephant with wings — chosen, one assumes, for majesty
rather than aerodynamics, though history would have its joke either
way.

The dream had hard logic beneath the sentiment, and it deserves to
be taken seriously before it is criticized.

Nigeria at independence had fifty-five million people spread across
a territory twice the size of California, with three great
population centers, poor roads, and a seasonal river system. It was
a natural aviation market if anywhere in Africa was. A national
carrier promised connectivity the railways could not deliver,
foreign exchange, a training ladder into modern technical
employment, and presence — in 1960, an airline was how a new state
introduced itself to the world.

Nor was state ownership eccentric. In 1960, nearly every airline on
earth outside the United States belonged to a government.

The fork in the road ran somewhere else entirely, and nobody put it
to a vote.

## THE ECONOMICS: Two ways to own an airline

A state-owned airline can be governed in one of two ways, and the
choice is invisible on the balance sheet in year one and decisive by
year twenty.

**Model A — the enterprise.** The nation owns the equity. The
government behaves as a shareholder: it appoints a board, sets a
mandate, and then subjects the firm to a hard budget constraint.
Management is recruited for competence and fired for performance.
Route decisions are made on contribution margin. Losses are a
signal, not a subsidy line.

**Model B — the annex.** The airline is an arm of the state. Jobs
are patronage. Routes are diplomacy. Aircraft purchases are
procurement politics. Management is appointed the way permanent
secretaries are appointed and removed the way ministers are removed.
Losses are absorbed, so they carry no information.

Ethiopian Airlines was deliberately built on Model A in 1945 — an
American management contract with an explicit mandate to train
Ethiopians into every seat — and successive Ethiopian regimes of
wildly different ideologies defended that structure as too valuable
to raid.

Nigeria Airways drifted onto Model B. Not at once. Never
irreversibly. But steadily.

The economics of that drift can be read in three numbers.

## THE NUMBERS: Three measurements of a hollowing

**Number one: aircraft types per fleet.**

By 1980, Nigeria Airways operated roughly a dozen distinct aircraft
types in a fleet of about thirty.

To grasp how abnormal that is: commercial airlines pursue fleet
commonality almost religiously, because every additional type
multiplies costs that are invisible to passengers and enormous in
aggregate — separate spare parts inventories, separate simulator
arrangements, separate engineering certifications, separate crew
qualifications, separate maintenance manuals. The most profitable
airlines in the world have historically flown one or two types
across hundreds of aircraft.

A dozen types across thirty aircraft is not a fleet. It is a
collection.

Fleets look like that when acquisitions are decided as diplomacy and
finance-ministry theater — a wide-body because flags need one, a
European aircraft because Europe called — rather than as fleet
planning. Complexity was purchased at boom prices and would have to
be maintained at bust prices.

**Number two: chief executive tenure.**

Across its life, Nigeria Airways ran through more than twenty chief
executives. Several lasted months.

Consider what that does to a maintenance program. A heavy
maintenance cycle runs on multi-year planning. A spares pipeline
requires supplier relationships built over years. A training
pipeline pays back over a decade. Every one of those requires an
institution capable of intending something for longer than its
leadership survives.

Each new chief executive owed his seat to a patron. Each froze or
reversed his predecessor's arrangements. The procurement pipeline —
the dull artery through which an airline's future flows — was
severed and re-plumbed every two or three years.

Churn is what patronage looks like when you plot it on an
organization chart.

**Number three: employees per aircraft.**

The airline's workforce swelled to several times industry norms per
aircraft. It had become, in effect, a federal employment program
with a flying division.

Be careful here, because the individuals were not the problem. The
airline contained, to its last day, engineers and pilots of world
caliber — as its diaspora would shortly prove by staffing other
countries' airlines.

The problem was arithmetic. An airline that exists partly to
distribute jobs cannot shrink to its revenue. It cannot pay
competitively at the top of its technical grades, because the
payroll is consumed at the bottom. And it cannot say no.

An organization that cannot say no is accumulating risk somewhere.

In aviation, it accumulates in the hangar.

## The private subscription service

What did the drift feel like from inside a cockpit?

The senior pilots of that era describe, with a consistency that
survives every retelling, a calculation performed privately at the
head of the jetway.

The trip offered with a deferred-defect list that would have
grounded the aircraft on a neighboring registry. The roster torn up
overnight because a VIP charter had commandeered the aircraft. The
simulator session postponed for a budget season.

Which compromises would a captain carry into the air, and which
would he refuse at the cost of a summons to headquarters?

"You were not flying for the airline," one retired captain of that
generation put it. "You were flying against it. Every day, a
little."

By the late boom, the safety of Nigeria's national carrier had
become a private subscription service, operated by its professionals
against the drift of their employer.

This is a more important observation than it first appears, and it
recurs in every chapter of this book, so it is worth stating as a
principle:

*A system that runs on individual heroism is a system spending
margins it does not own.*

Heroism keeps the schedule alive. And by keeping the schedule alive,
it hides the decay from the public, from the press, and from the
treasury — until the day the heroism is one contingency short.

Nobody can say in advance which day that will be. That is the entire
problem.

## THE ARGUMENT: What a symbol is for

There is a serious philosophical question buried in the flying
elephant, and dismissing it as vanity misses what was actually at
stake.

Symbols are not decoration in political life. They are coordination
devices. A flag, an anthem, a national carrier — these give a new
and internally divided population something to point at and say
*that is us*. Nigeria in 1960 was three regions, hundreds of
languages, and a constitution three years old. The case for spending
real money on symbols was not frivolous.

The problem is not that Nigeria bought a symbol.

The problem is the *sequence*.

A symbol that crowns a working system compounds the system's worth:
it recruits talent, attracts capital, and creates a constituency
that will defend the substance in order to protect the emblem.

A symbol procured *in place of* a system becomes, in time, that
system's epitaph. Worse: it actively conceals the absence. The flag
flies whether or not anything beneath it works. A symbol that cannot
fail is the perfect roof under which everything else can.

By the 1980s the joke had turned proverbial in Nigerian markets:
elephants do not fly. The airline died narrated under elephant
metaphors of increasing cruelty.

Hold that lesson. In July 2018, at an air show in England, a
Nigerian minister would unveil a livery for a new national carrier
that did not yet exist, would not exist five years later, and would
be denounced by a parliamentary committee in the harshest available
language.

The columnists reached for the elephant within hours.

## Zaria, 1964

Fairness requires this section, because the independence generation
built one aviation institution on the right model, and its fate is
as instructive as the airline's.

In 1964, with United Nations and ICAO support, Nigeria opened the
Civil Aviation Training Centre at Zaria — a school for pilots, air
traffic controllers, and aircraft engineers.

Zaria was everything the airline was not: technically governed,
internationally supervised in its founding decade, selective, and
oriented toward a standard rather than a spectacle. It trained
students from across anglophone Africa. For a generation of African
aviation professionals, "Zaria" carried the weight that "Sandhurst"
carries in another profession, and its alumni run through every
institution in this book, usually on the creditable side of the
ledger.

Zaria matters twice.

First, it falsifies in advance the laziest explanation for
everything that follows — that Nigeria somehow could not do
aviation. Nigeria could, and did, wherever the institutional design
insulated competence from politics: an entrance examination instead
of a patron, a curriculum audited from Montreal instead of a budget
negotiated in Lagos.

Second, its later history proves the theorem's other half. Decades
of underfunding. Obsolete training aircraft. Instructors lost to
airline pay and then to the Gulf. Periodic rescue, renewed decay.

Founding design protects an institution for a while. Only a
political constituency protects it forever.

The airline had a constituency — everyone who could extract from it.
The training college had none. Where the extraction was, the money
went.

A country's budget is its actual aviation policy, whatever its
gazette says.

## Kano, January 22, 1973

The Hajj is the largest annually recurring movement of human beings
on earth, and by the early 1970s Nigeria was among its largest
sources.

Hajj transport was aviation's strangest market: a colossal demand
spike, a few weeks long, in one direction at a time, to a single
destination, composed of passengers many of whom had never flown and
would never fly again. No airline could keep a fleet sized for it.

The universal solution, then and now, was the charter — hire
aircraft and crews from wherever capacity could be found, and surge
the pilgrims across the Red Sea and home.

On January 22, 1973, a Boeing 707 chartered from the Jordanian
carrier Alia for the Nigerian pilgrimage approached Kano at the end
of a flight from Jeddah. The winter Harmattan does to Kano what fog
does to London. The approach went wrong. The aircraft came down
hard, broke, and burned.

One hundred and seventy-six people died.

At that moment, it was the deadliest crash in the history of civil
aviation anywhere in the world.

Sit with that fact for a moment, because it is genuinely strange.

The deadliest crash the world had yet seen involved Nigerian
passengers, on Nigerian soil, in the fifth year of the Nigerian
boom — and it is barely remembered today outside the families and
the city that buried the dead in long trenches while the north
mourned through the spring.

Part of the amnesia is general. The 1970s were world aviation's
bloodiest decade, and January's record was surpassed before the year
was out.

Part of it is specific, and diagnostic.

The aircraft was Jordanian. The crew was foreign. The charter had
been arranged through the apparatus of the pilgrimage rather than
the airline proper. Investigation belonged formally to Nigeria as
the state of occurrence, with Jordan and the manufacturer's state in
attendance.

In the seams between all those jurisdictions, accountability quietly
bled out.

There was grief. There was no reckoning. No public report Nigerians
could read. No named institutional failure. No reform with a date on
it.

The state's operative conclusion, as far as the record shows, was
that a foreign airline had crashed a foreign aircraft in bad weather.

An act of God with a Jordanian registration.

## The conclusion that was available

The other conclusion was available in 1973, and it did not require
hindsight.

It was this: Nigeria had built a system in which the most dangerous
flying its citizens did — mass charters, surge operations,
unfamiliar crews, minimal continuity — was precisely the flying over
which its own institutions exercised the least control. And a state
whose citizens board aircraft it does not oversee has not avoided
responsibility. It has only deferred it.

That conclusion was not drawn.

Eighteen years later, at Jeddah, in an almost perfectly recurved
echo — a chartered foreign aircraft, a pilgrimage operation, Nigerian
passengers, institutional responsibility dissolving across three
countries — the deferred bill arrived with 261 names on it.

Institutions that do not digest their first warning are not granted
gentler second ones.

## What the oil paid for

The comparison worth making about the 1970s is one nobody at the
time knew they were running.

In Addis Ababa, Ethiopian Airlines spent the decade doing
unglamorous things. Its American management contract ended in 1971,
having produced the Ethiopianization it was designed to produce, and
the airline kept the systems, the academy, the maintenance culture.

When revolution came in 1974 and a Marxist military junta seized the
state — nationalizing everything and shooting much of the old elite
— the new rulers left the airline's management substantially alone.
They had grasped that it was one of the very few reliable
hard-currency machines the state owned.

The airline flew through the terror, the famine, and the civil war.
It made money. It trained pilots that other African carriers
borrowed.

The lesson is uncomfortable precisely because it removes every
excuse: institutional protection is a political choice, available to
poor states, available even to brutal states, the moment a sovereign
decides that an institution is worth more functioning than raided.

Nigeria ran the opposite protocol with ten times the money.

And here is the part that economists will recognize immediately. It
was not despite the oil. It was *because* of it.

A state with petroleum revenue does not need its airline to earn
foreign exchange. The recomputation in Addis Ababa always returned
the answer *protect the machine*, because the machine was one of the
few sources of dollars. The recomputation in Lagos always returned
the other answer, because the dollars came out of the ground.

Oil was Nigerian aviation's quiet curse, and it operated not through
corruption but through the arithmetic of alternatives.

The 1970s were the years in which all of this was affordable,
because oil paid for the slack. When maintenance was expensive, one
bought spares. When a route lost money, the treasury absorbed it.
When the pilgrimage needed lift, one chartered it.

Boom-era institutions are never tested by booms. The test comes when
the money stops — when the deferred maintenance, the deferred
training, the deferred regulation must all be paid at once from an
empty account.

In 1981, the oil price broke.
