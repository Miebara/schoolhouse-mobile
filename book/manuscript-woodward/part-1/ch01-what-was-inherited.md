# PART I — WHAT WAS INHERITED

# 1. The Files at Kew

## London, the National Archives, Kew

The reading room closes at five and the boxes must be back on the
trolley by four-thirty, which is how a researcher looking for the
origins of Nigerian aviation ends up with a specific and unwelcome
piece of knowledge.

The files are all there.

The monthly serviceability returns of the wireless station at Kano.
The correspondence over an emir's request for a flying
demonstration. The land acquisition disputes for every aerodrome,
with compensation schedules and the names of the men who cut the
grass. The incident reports, in which a bent undercarriage generates
a minuted inquiry, a finding, and a filed reply.

The colonial administration of Nigerian aviation between 1925 and
1960 can be reconstructed almost day by day. The Nigerian
administration of Nigerian aviation between 1980 and 1999
substantially cannot.

"You will find more about 1935 than about 1985," a retired Nigerian
aviation official said, when told what the Kew boxes contained. He
did not find this surprising. He had watched some of the 1985
records go. "Floods. Termites. Offices moving. And some of it, let
us be honest, went because somebody wanted it gone."

He paused. "A state that stops writing things down has decided not
to be audited. By anyone. Including itself."

That is the first fact of this book, and it is worth stating before
the airplanes start falling, because it explains why so much of what
follows had to be assembled from newspapers, from foreign archives,
and from the memories of men in their seventies.

## Kano, November 1, 1925

The city heard it before it saw it. A drone out of the northeast,
thin at first and then filling the sky, brought the markets out onto
the plain to watch three Royal Air Force biplanes drop out of the
Harmattan haze and settle onto a strip of cleared laterite outside
the walls.

The walls were a thousand years old. They had seen caravans from
Tripoli, armies from Sokoto, and, twenty-two years earlier, the
Maxim guns of the British conquest.

The emir received the airmen with ceremony. The colonial Resident
wrote enthusiastic dispatches. In London, a file was opened.

The airplane had arrived in Nigeria as an instrument of somebody
else's system, landing on Nigerian soil and answering to
institutions four thousand miles away. Nothing about that
arrangement was hidden or shameful at the time. It was simply how
empires worked. What matters is what it left behind.

## A route map is a confession

The flights of 1925 did not begin in Lagos, which was the commercial
capital, and they did not follow the population, which was
concentrated in the south and the middle belt. They ran laterally
across the Sahel, from Cairo to Khartoum to Maiduguri to Kano,
because that was the line the empire needed to move along.

Plot the imperial air network of British West Africa against a map
of where Nigerians actually lived and the two barely correspond.
Plot it instead against a map of where the colonial administration
needed to move men and mail, and the fit is very nearly total. Kano
was not a destination. It was a refueling stop on the way to
somewhere that mattered more.

The consequence compounds over decades. At independence in 1960
Nigeria inherited an aviation system shaped like a funnel, wide
toward London and narrow at home. The trunk international routes had
paved infrastructure, navigation aids, and trained ground staff,
while the domestic network that would eventually kill the most
people in this book had a scatter of strips, most of them unlit and
several of them unpaved.

Sixty years later the shape persists. Lagos to London remains among
the most valuable air routes in Africa by yield, and Nigerian
carriers have never held more than a minority share of the traffic
on it. Domestic Nigerian aviation, meanwhile, remained for decades
the system's neglected child. Of the mass-casualty accidents in Part
II of this book, every one except the Jeddah pilgrimage disaster of
1991 occurred on a domestic sector or a domestic charter.
Infrastructure is destiny with a very long fuse.

## Runways, not inspectors

There is a standard economic logic to colonial infrastructure, and
Nigerian aviation follows it exactly.

An extractive administration builds the assets that lower its own
cost of operation, which means ports, rail to the mines, telegraph
to the capital, and airstrips along the administrative corridor. It
does not build the assets that would raise the productive capacity
of the governed. The distinction is not a moral one. It is a return
calculation. The colonizer's planning horizon extends only to the
end of the concession, and the returns to human capital in a colony
accrue mostly to the colonized. Apply that logic to aviation and it
yields a precise prediction, which is that the capital assets will
be built and the institutional capacity will not.

The prediction holds. By 1945 Nigeria had, courtesy of the Second
World War, some of the most strategically significant runways in
Africa. Britain, needing to ferry warplanes to Egypt without sailing
past Axis airpower, had built the West African Reinforcement Route,
along which crated fighters were assembled at the coast and flown
across the continent in stages from Lagos to Kano to Sudan at a rate
that reached several hundred aircraft a month at its peak. The
Americans arrived after 1941 and enlarged everything, adding
hangars, fuel farms, and radio installations.

Thousands of Nigerians worked that route as riggers, fitters,
loaders, and drivers. A very small number flew, and Peter Thomas of
Lagos became the first Nigerian commissioned into the Royal Air
Force. When the war ended, the organizations went home and took the
system with them.

This is the distinction on which the entire book turns, so it is
worth naming plainly. Aviation's artifacts are runways, hangars,
aircraft, and terminals. They are visible, financeable, and
politically rewarding, and a ribbon can be cut in front of them.
Aviation's institutions are the airworthiness inspectorate, the
accident investigator, the licensing examination, the maintenance
records regime, and the training pipeline. They are invisible, dull,
and politically worthless. Nobody has ever won an election by
funding a records office.

The wartime system worked brilliantly because it was a closed loop
in which the Royal Air Force and Pan American owned every link,
supplying their own aircraft, their own mechanics, their own fuel
testers, their own standards, and their own discipline. Nigeria
hosted world-class aviation for four years without ever acquiring
the machinery that produced it.

A colonial engineer, asked in the 1950s why more Nigerians were not
being licensed to certify aircraft, is recorded in the
administrative correspondence giving an answer that is almost
admirable in its circularity. There was, he explained, no
established local capacity to train them to the standard. There was
no capacity because no one had built it, and no one built it because
there was no capacity.

## The corporation that worked

There is a might-have-been in this chapter, and its name was the
West African Airways Corporation.

WAAC was founded in 1946 on the model of the BBC, as a public
corporation jointly owned by four colonial governments, those of
Nigeria, the Gold Coast, Sierra Leone, and the Gambia. It was
headquartered in Lagos, run by aviation professionals, and its
airworthiness was overseen from a Britain too distant to be lobbied.

Reconstruct an ordinary WAAC morning in 1952 from its operating
culture and here is what you get. The engineer signs the daily
inspection in a bound log. The load sheet is computed and
countersigned. The magneto check fails at run-up, and the flight
cancels.

And nobody calls anybody, because there is no one to call.
Accountability to four governments means, in practice,
accountability to no single politician. The decision to cancel
belongs to the system rather than to a negotiation. The passengers
grumble at the notice board and wait for the afternoon service, and
the airplane does not crash.

Multiply that uncancelled telephone call across a decade and you
have a safety record assembled out of pure governance, achieved on
1950s technology, in exactly the skies and storms that will fill
Part II of this book with wreckage.

Then the Gold Coast moved toward independence, Kwame Nkrumah pulled
Ghana out to found a flag carrier of its own, and the shared
corporation's logic collapsed. Nigeria followed. Ghana Airways would
be banned from American skies in 2004 and liquidated soon
afterward, and Nigeria Airways would be liquidated in 2003. A
professionally run airline owned by four states, too large for any
one government to raid, was precisely the institution that West
Africa would spend the next half-century failing to rebuild. It had
existed once. It was dismantled for the flag.

## The problem of the invisible seat

Something happened in the 1950s that the historical literature
records without quite noticing what it means.

The nationalist press covered aviation with the intensity other
countries reserve for sport. The first Nigerian captain was
celebrated like a title fight. The line about how one day Nigerians
would fly these planes was a fixture of the platform speeches. When
the airline was fully bought out from its British partners in 1961,
it was front-page news.

Read those campaigns carefully and a pattern appears in what was
actually demanded. The demand was to fly the planes, to hold the
visible seats in the cockpit, in the boardroom, and on the
ownership register. Nobody, anywhere, in any speech, demanded the
airworthiness inspectorate.

This is not a Nigerian failing. It is a structural property of
decolonization, and of politics generally, and it deserves to be
stated as a general principle because it explains a great deal
beyond aviation. Sovereignty is claimed first over the functions
that are visible, and last over the functions that are load-bearing.

The arithmetic of the handover made this fatal. Decolonization ran
faster than any training plan could. A pilot took roughly a decade
to reach command, an aircraft maintenance engineer took comparably
long, and an airworthiness surveyor, who is the professional who
inspects the inspectors and who must accumulate industry experience
before the oversight training even begins, took longer still. There
was no Nigerian cohort in that pipeline in 1950, because the
pipeline had been closed to Nigerians. There could therefore be no
Nigerian cohort emerging from it by 1960, regardless of goodwill,
money, or talent.

The new state faced the standard postcolonial menu. It could retain
expatriates, hire international agencies, or promote the available
Nigerians faster than the ladders were designed to bear. It did all
three, in proportions set by budget crises rather than by strategy.
Operations Nigerianized quickly and well. Oversight did not. The gap
between those two curves, one rising fast and the other barely
rising at all, is the space in which every disaster in this book
occurred.

## The record that was not kept

One more thing traveled from the empire to the republic, and it was
not an asset.

When an aircraft crashed in colonial Nigeria, an inspector
investigated under British air navigation rules. The findings went
to London, were filed in London, and changed standards maintained in
London. The system was, for its era, genuinely good. Britain had
understood since the First World War that the purpose of accident
investigation is prevention rather than punishment.

But observe the flow. Nigeria experienced the crashes and the
metropole accumulated the learning. The feedback loop that converts
wreckage into safety, which is the entire secret of aviation's
transformation into the safest form of travel ever devised, ran
through Nigeria without ever touching down.

The habit that transferred most perfectly was the habit about
publicity. Colonial accident findings were internal correspondence,
released to a public of colonial subjects rarely and partially. The
notion that a report is owed to the public, that a state must
explain a disaster to the people it fell upon, was never part of the
inheritance. So when the independent Nigerian state later sat on its
accident reports for years, or forever, it was not innovating. It
was continuing an administrative tradition. Institutions bequeath
their reflexes, and secrecy is a reflex.

## Lagos, October 1, 1960

The flag went up. The aircraft of the new national carrier flew over
the stadium in salute. Beneath them, the inheritance was mixed in a
way nobody at the ceremony could see.

The real assets were substantial. Good runways at Kano, Lagos, and
Port Harcourt. Trained ground personnel. Membership, through
Britain, in the international system framed at Chicago in 1944,
which Nigeria would join in its own name the following year.

The hollow institutions were equally substantial and much harder to
notice. Regulation had been an imported service and was now
withdrawn. The network faced outward. The airline was three parts
symbol to one part system. And the political culture had settled on
a meaning for aviation, which was spectacle, a meaning established
by the Royal Air Force in 1925 and adopted without revision by the
men who replaced them.

None of this made disaster inevitable. Ethiopia would prove, on a
fraction of Nigeria's wealth, that the institutions of flight can be
deliberately built and deliberately sustained. But inheritances set
the defaults, and defaults govern whenever attention lapses. Nigeria
began with defaults set by an empire that had needed runways rather
than inspectors, and with a political class that needed, above all,
a flag in the sky.

The next chapter is about that flag, about what it cost, and about
what was quietly not being built beneath it while everyone watched
it wave.
