# PART I — WHAT WAS INHERITED

# 1. The Files at Kew

## London, the National Archives, Kew

The reading room closes at five, and the boxes must be back on the
trolley by four-thirty, which is how a researcher looking for the
origins of Nigerian aviation ends up with a specific and unwelcome
piece of knowledge.

The files are all there.

The monthly serviceability returns of the wireless station at Kano.
The correspondence over an emir's request for a flying demonstration.
The land acquisition disputes for every aerodrome, with compensation
schedules and the names of the men who cut the grass. The incident
reports, in which a bent undercarriage generates a minuted inquiry,
a finding, and a filed reply.

The colonial administration of Nigerian aviation, 1925 to 1960,
can be reconstructed almost day by day.

The Nigerian administration of Nigerian aviation, 1980 to 1999,
substantially cannot.

"You will find more about 1935 than about 1985," a retired Nigerian
aviation official said, when told what the Kew boxes contained. He
did not find this surprising. He had watched some of the 1985
records go. "Floods. Termites. Offices moving. And some of it,
let us be honest, went because somebody wanted it gone."

He paused.

"A state that stops writing things down has decided not to be
audited. By anyone. Including itself."

That is the first fact of this book, and it is worth stating before
the airplanes start falling, because it explains why so much of what
follows had to be assembled from newspapers, from foreign archives,
and from the memories of men in their seventies.

## Kano, November 1, 1925

The city heard it before it saw it.

A drone out of the northeast, thin at first, then filling the sky —
and the markets emptied onto the plain to watch three Royal Air
Force biplanes drop out of the Harmattan haze and settle onto a
strip of cleared laterite outside the walls.

The walls were a thousand years old. They had seen caravans from
Tripoli, armies from Sokoto, and, twenty-two years earlier, the
Maxim guns of the British conquest.

The emir received the airmen with ceremony. The colonial Resident
wrote enthusiastic dispatches. In London, a file was opened.

The airplane had arrived in Nigeria as an instrument of somebody
else's system — landing on Nigerian soil, answering to institutions
four thousand miles away.

Nothing about that arrangement was hidden or shameful at the time.
It was simply how empires worked. What matters is what it left
behind.

## THE NUMBERS: A route map is a confession

The flights of 1925 did not begin in Lagos, which was the commercial
capital, and they did not follow the population, which was in the
south and the middle belt.

They ran laterally across the Sahel: Cairo to Khartoum to Maiduguri
to Kano.

Plot the imperial air network of British West Africa against two
maps — one of where Nigerians lived, one of where the colonial
administration needed to move — and the fit with the second is
near-total. Kano was not a destination. It was a refueling stop on
the way to somewhere that mattered more.

The consequence compounds. At independence in 1960, Nigeria
inherited an aviation system shaped like a funnel: wide toward
London, narrow at home. The trunk international routes had
infrastructure, navigation aids, and trained ground staff. The
domestic network — the flying that would eventually kill the most
people in this book — had a scatter of strips.

Sixty years later the shape persists. Lagos–London remains among the
most valuable air routes in Africa. Domestic Nigerian aviation
remained, for decades, the system's neglected child, and every
mass-casualty crash in Part II of this book except one occurred on a
domestic sector or a domestic charter.

Infrastructure is destiny with a very long fuse.

## THE ECONOMICS: Why the empire built runways and not institutions

There is a standard economic logic to colonial infrastructure, and
Nigerian aviation follows it exactly.

An extractive administration builds the assets that lower *its* cost
of operation — ports, rail to the mines, telegraph to the capital,
airstrips on the administrative corridor — and does not build the
assets that would raise the productive capacity of the governed. The
distinction is not moral. It is a return calculation. The colonizer's
planning horizon extends to the end of the concession, and the
returns to human capital in the colony accrue mostly to the
colonized.

Apply that to aviation and you get a precise prediction: capital
assets yes, institutional capacity no.

The prediction holds. By 1945 Nigeria had, courtesy of the Second
World War, some of the most strategically significant runways in
Africa. Britain, needing to ferry warplanes to Egypt without sailing
past Axis airpower, had built the West African Reinforcement Route —
crated fighters assembled at the coast, flown across the continent
in stages, Lagos to Kano to Sudan, hundreds of aircraft a month at
its peak. The Americans arrived after 1941 and enlarged everything:
hangars, fuel farms, radio installations.

Thousands of Nigerians worked that route as riggers, fitters,
loaders, drivers. A very small number flew. Peter Thomas of Lagos
became the first Nigerian commissioned into the Royal Air Force.

And when the war ended, the organizations went home and took the
system with them.

This is the distinction on which the entire book turns, so it is
worth naming it plainly.

**Aviation's artifacts** are runways, hangars, aircraft, terminals.
They are visible, financeable, and politically rewarding. A ribbon
can be cut.

**Aviation's institutions** are the airworthiness inspectorate, the
accident investigator, the licensing examination, the maintenance
records regime, the training pipeline. They are invisible, dull,
and politically worthless. Nobody has ever won an election by
funding a records office.

The wartime system worked brilliantly because it was a closed loop:
the RAF and Pan American owned every link — their aircraft, their
mechanics, their fuel testers, their standards, their discipline.
Nigeria hosted world-class aviation without ever acquiring the
machinery that produced it.

A colonial engineer, asked in the 1950s why more Nigerians were not
being licensed to certify aircraft, is recorded in the
administrative correspondence giving an answer that is almost
admirable in its circularity: there was no established local
capacity to train them to the standard.

There was no capacity because no one had built it. No one built it
because there was no capacity.

## The corporation that worked

There is a might-have-been in this chapter, and its name was the
West African Airways Corporation.

WAAC was founded in 1946 on the model of the BBC: a public
corporation jointly owned by four colonial governments — Nigeria,
the Gold Coast, Sierra Leone, the Gambia — headquartered in Lagos,
run by aviation professionals, its airworthiness overseen from a
Britain too distant to be lobbied.

Reconstruct an ordinary WAAC morning in 1952 from its operating
culture, and here is what you get. The engineer signs the daily
inspection in a bound log. The load sheet is computed and
countersigned. The magneto check fails at run-up, and the flight
cancels.

And nobody calls anybody.

There is no one to call. Accountability to four governments means,
in practice, accountability to no single politician. The decision to
cancel belongs to the system, not to a negotiation. The passengers
grumble at the notice board and wait for the afternoon service.

The airplane does not crash.

Multiply that uncancelled telephone call across a decade and you
have a safety record assembled out of pure governance, on 1950s
technology, in exactly the skies and storms that will fill Part II
of this book with wreckage.

Then the Gold Coast moved toward independence, and Kwame Nkrumah
pulled Ghana out to found a flag carrier of its own, and the shared
corporation's logic collapsed. Nigeria followed. Ghana Airways would
be banned from American skies in 2004 and liquidated. Nigeria
Airways would be liquidated in 2003.

A professionally run airline owned by four states, too large for any
one government to raid, was precisely the institution West Africa
would spend the next half-century failing to rebuild.

It had existed. It was dismantled for the flag.

## THE ARGUMENT: The problem of the invisible seat

Something happened in the 1950s that the historical literature
records without quite noticing what it means.

The nationalist press covered aviation with the intensity other
countries reserve for sport. The first Nigerian captain was
celebrated like a title fight. "One day Nigerians will fly these
planes" was a stock line of the platform speeches. When the airline
was fully bought out from its British partners in 1961, it was
front-page news.

Read those campaigns carefully and a pattern appears in what was
demanded.

The demand was to *fly the planes*. To hold the visible seats: the
cockpit, the boardroom, the ownership.

Nobody, anywhere, in any speech, demanded the airworthiness
inspectorate.

This is not a Nigerian failing. It is a structural property of
decolonization, and of politics generally, and it deserves to be
stated as a general principle because it explains a great deal
beyond aviation:

*Sovereignty is claimed first over the functions that are visible,
and last over the functions that are load-bearing.*

The arithmetic of the handover made this fatal. Decolonization ran
faster than any training plan. A pilot took roughly a decade to
reach command. An aircraft maintenance engineer, comparably long.
An airworthiness surveyor — the professional who inspects the
inspectors, and who must have industry experience *before* the
oversight training begins — took longer still.

There was no Nigerian cohort in that pipeline in 1950, because the
pipeline had been closed to Nigerians. There could therefore be no
Nigerian cohort emerging from it by 1960, regardless of goodwill,
money, or talent.

The new state faced the standard postcolonial menu: retain
expatriates, hire international agencies, or promote the available
Nigerians faster than the ladders were designed to bear.

It did all three, in proportions set by budget crises rather than by
strategy.

Operations Nigerianized quickly and well. Oversight did not. And the
gap between those two curves — one rising fast, one barely rising at
all — is the space in which every disaster in this book occurred.

## The record that was not kept

One more thing traveled from the empire to the republic, and it was
not an asset.

When an aircraft crashed in colonial Nigeria, an inspector
investigated under British air navigation rules. The findings went
to London, were filed in London, and changed standards maintained in
London.

The system was, for its era, genuinely good. Britain had understood
since the First World War that the purpose of accident
investigation is prevention, not punishment.

But observe the flow. Nigeria experienced the crashes. The metropole
accumulated the learning. The feedback loop that converts wreckage
into safety — the entire secret, this book will argue, of aviation's
transformation into the safest form of travel ever devised — ran
through Nigeria without ever touching down.

And the habit that transferred most perfectly was the habit about
publicity. Colonial accident findings were internal correspondence,
released to a public of colonial subjects rarely and partially. The
notion that a report is *owed* to the public — that a state must
explain a disaster to the people it fell upon — was never part of
the inheritance.

So when the independent Nigerian state later sat on its accident
reports for years, or forever, it was not innovating.

It was continuing an administrative tradition.

Institutions bequeath their reflexes. Secrecy is a reflex.

## Lagos, October 1, 1960

The flag went up. The aircraft of the new national carrier flew over
the stadium in salute.

Beneath them, the inheritance was mixed in a way nobody at the
ceremony could see.

Real assets: good runways at Kano, Lagos, and Port Harcourt.
Trained ground personnel. Membership, through Britain, in the
international system framed at Chicago in 1944, which Nigeria would
join in its own name the following year.

Hollow institutions: regulation that had been an imported service,
now withdrawn. A network that faced outward. An airline that was
three parts symbol to one part system. And a political culture in
which aviation meant spectacle — a meaning established by the Royal
Air Force in 1925 and adopted without revision by the men who
replaced them.

None of this made disaster inevitable. Ethiopia would prove, on a
fraction of Nigeria's wealth, that the institutions of flight can be
deliberately built.

But inheritances set the defaults, and defaults govern whenever
attention lapses.

Nigeria began with defaults set by an empire that had needed
runways, not inspectors — and with a political class that needed,
above all, a flag in the sky.

The next chapter is about that flag. What it cost. And what was
quietly not being built beneath it while everyone watched it wave.
