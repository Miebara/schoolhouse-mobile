# 3. The Christmas Tree

## Ikeja maintenance base, 1988

It sat at the end of the apron, and everyone who worked there knew
what it was for.

A Boeing 747 in the green-and-white livery of Nigeria Airways, doors
closed, tires flat, one engine cowling open and empty.

The engineers called it the Christmas tree.

A hydraulic pump had gone to one sister ship. Instruments to
another. Seats, slides, valves, a generator, a windshield — the
inventory of what had been taken off it existed only in the heads of
the men who took it.

"You would go out at night with a torch and a work order that did
not exist," Emeka said. "You take what you need. You write what you
can. In the morning the schedule flies."

Was it legal?

He thought about the question longer than expected.

"Parts robbery is legal. There is a procedure for it. There are
forms." A pause. "There is a procedure for one aircraft. There is
no procedure for a way of life."

Controlled cannibalization is a legitimate practice at a well-run
airline. As a way of life, it is a family burning the furniture
through a winter: rational on each cold night, catastrophic as a
strategy, and diagnostic — always — of an income that has stopped
matching a way of living.

## THE ECONOMICS: The dollar scissors

Nigeria's income had stopped matching everything.

The oil price broke in 1981 and collapsed in 1986. Federal revenue
fell by more than half in real terms. The naira, defended for a
while and then devalued in stages, lost more than nine-tenths of its
dollar value across the decade.

For most industries this was a severe recession. For aviation it was
closer to an extinction event, and the reason is a structural
feature of the business that almost no one outside it understands.

**Aviation is a dollar industry that earns local currency.**

Consider the cost stack of any airline anywhere in the world:

- Aircraft acquisition or lease — dollars
- Spare parts — dollars
- Engine overhauls — dollars
- Heavy maintenance checks — dollars
- Hull and liability insurance — dollars
- Simulator training — dollars
- Manufacturer technical support — dollars
- Navigation databases and charts — dollars

Now consider the revenue: naira tickets, sold in a price-sensitive
domestic market, at fares that were politically supervised whenever
they became visible.

Between those two lines sat the central bank's foreign exchange
queue. Even a solvent airline holding naira waited months for an
allocation at the official rate, while its overseas creditors —
lessors, insurers, parts suppliers — aged their receivables and
priced the waiting into the next contract.

Every devaluation silently multiplied the real cost of
airworthiness.

Nothing announced this. No coup plotter ever listed the exchange
rate among his grievances. But when Part II of this book asks why
Nigerian aircraft in the 1990s were old, why the spares were scarce,
why recurrent training was deferred and the insurance was
threadbare, the answer begins here — with the least dramatic villain
in this story and one of the most lethal.

## THE NUMBERS: What a maintenance check actually is

"Deferred maintenance" is an abstraction until you see what the
money buys.

An airliner's maintenance program is a pyramid of escalating
intimacy.

At the base, the daily and transit checks: walk-arounds, fluid
levels, tire and brake condition. Hours of labor. Skipped by nobody,
because their absence is visible by lunchtime.

Above them, the lettered checks. The A-check, overnight, every few
weeks. And then the heavy checks — C and D — in which the aircraft
is withdrawn from service for weeks, opened to its bones, its
systems functionally tested, its structure inspected for the cracks
and corrosion that fatigue mathematics predicts and only eyes and
instruments confirm.

The heavy checks are where airworthiness actually lives.

Metal fatigue is invisible, progressive, and unforgiving of schedule
slippage. The inspection intervals are not administrative
conveniences. They are set by engineering analysis of exactly how
long a detectable crack takes to become a critical one.

Defer the check, and you are not skipping a formality.

You are spending the safety analysis's margin. You are flying into
the interval the engineers reserved for error, on the bet that this
particular airframe is average.

Old aircraft raise the stakes twice over. Their inspection programs
*thicken* with age — supplemental structural inspections, corrosion
control programs, the aging-aircraft directives written after a
Boeing 737's cabin roof peeled open at altitude over Hawaii in 1988
— at precisely the moment their owners' economics thin.

## The deferral calculus

Now place a maintenance decision inside the arithmetic of a
1990s Nigerian carrier, and watch how rational the wrong answer
looks.

A heavy check costs, in dollars, seven figures, plus weeks of lost
revenue on an aircraft that may represent a third of the fleet.

Competing for the same scarce dollars, in the same month:

- The lease payment, whose default means repossession — the aircraft
  is gone, and with it the company
- The insurance premium, whose lapse means immediate grounding
- The fuel bill, without which nothing moves today
- The payroll, without which the engineers leave

The check can be deferred within the letter of the regulations,
because deferral mechanisms exist legitimately everywhere in
aviation. The lease payment cannot be deferred at all.

So every incentive in the structure, quarter after quarter, points
the scarce dollar away from the hangar. And the regulator of that
era — as the next chapters will show in detail — priced the deferral
at approximately zero.

The corners were not cut by monsters. They were cut by spreadsheets,
rationally, one defensible cell at a time, inside firms whose
bankruptcy was usually arriving faster than their airframes'
fatigue.

This is the oldest finding in safety economics, and it is worth
stating in its general form because it governs far more than
aviation:

*Firms discount catastrophe at their own survival horizon.*

A company that expects to die in three years is structurally
incapable of fearing a metal problem due in seven. Not unwilling.
Incapable. The risk lies beyond the entity's expected lifespan, and
therefore beyond its decision frame.

The policy implication is uncomfortable for anyone who believes
markets discipline safety: in an industry where the product is a
maintained promise, the *barrier to entry is the product*. Cheap
entry is not consumer welfare. It is an unpriced transfer of risk
from the firm to the passenger.

## THE ARGUMENT: Rules and the negotiation

The money stopped. Politics then decided where the shortage would be
felt.

The politics of the 1980s were military rule — a coup in 1983,
another in 1985, and beneath them the progressive fusion of state
and barracks that Nigerians simply call the military era.

Military government did three specific things to aviation.

It finished off management continuity, because chief executives now
rotated on the coup cycle.

It starved the referee: civil aviation oversight remained a
directorate inside the transport ministry, its inspectors on
civil-service pay that an airline first officer tripled, its
training abroad an early casualty of the dollar famine, its
independence a legal fiction under governments in which the
airline's chairman outranked the director of civil aviation in every
register Nigerian life recognizes.

And it normalized the exemption.

Soldiers do not queue. Regimes born of emergency govern by
exception. This flight will carry whom it is told. This aircraft
will be released regardless. This inspection finding will be waived,
this time, because the general's schedule requires it.

Each exemption was small. Their sum acquired a name that
professionals of the era still use without irony.

*The waiver culture.*

Here is why that phrase deserves philosophical attention rather than
mere disapproval.

A safety rule is not primarily an instruction. It is a *commitment
device*. Its value lies almost entirely in its unconditionality —
in the fact that it will bind at the exact moment when binding is
most expensive and least convenient.

A rule that is negotiable is not a weaker rule. It is a different
kind of object altogether. It is an opening bid.

And once it becomes an opening bid, its distributional consequences
follow immediately and inescapably: the rule now binds in inverse
proportion to power. It binds fully on the powerless operator with
no telephone numbers, partially on the connected one, and not at all
on the general.

This is the mechanism by which a safety system becomes a
class system.

A rule negotiable for a general is negotiable, eventually, for
anyone with his phone number. And the passenger in seat 14A, who has
no phone numbers at all, has no way of knowing which kind of rule
was in force on the morning his aircraft was released.

The reform of 2006, when it finally arrived, was at bottom nothing
more exotic than the repricing of waivers.

## The two fleets

No feature of the era exposes its priorities more cleanly than the
one fleet that stayed perfect.

While the national carrier ate itself and the navigation aids died,
the Presidential Air Fleet — the executive aircraft of the head of
state and his court — was renewed, expanded, and maintained to
standards the civil system could only describe from memory. Its
budget lived outside meaningful scrutiny. Its hangars were the best
in the country.

By the 1990s Nigeria operated one of the larger head-of-state fleets
on earth, a distinction it retains.

Treat that fleet, throughout this book, as the control group.

It answers, definitively, the question of whether the Nigerian state
knew how to keep aircraft airworthy.

It knew.

Airworthiness followed power, not capability. Every deficiency
elsewhere in this book was a choice of allocation, not a limit of
competence.

## The license factory

In 1986, under General Ibrahim Babangida, Nigeria adopted a
Structural Adjustment Programme — devaluation, deregulation,
privatization — and its aviation consequence was, at first glance,
paradoxical.

It created the Nigerian private airline.

The logic was orthodox: the national carrier was a fiscal hemorrhage
and a monopoly; private capital would supply the capacity it no
longer could. Licenses flowed from 1988, and by the early 1990s a
swarm of carriers had risen. Okada Air, Kabo, ADC, and dozens more.

Some were serious enterprises. Okada Air — the fleet of the Benin
businessman Gabriel Igbinedion — grew into the largest private
collection of airliners in Africa, assembled at scrap prices from
the world's retiring inventories. The traveling public, offered an
alternative to an airline that canceled by habit, voted with its
feet. Domestic aviation grew even as the economy shrank.

But examine what the license actually was, because its terms
predetermined the fleet that Part II buries.

In mature systems, an air operator's certificate is among the
hardest documents in commerce to obtain. Proving out an airline's
manuals, maintenance arrangements, crew training, and financial
fitness consumes years and consultants.

The Nigerian certificate of the late 1980s was, by the accounts of
people who acquired them, substantially a political and commercial
document. Capital requirements were nominal. Financial fitness
review was cursory. The technical examination was conducted by the
starved directorate described above — a handful of inspectors
certifying dozens of applicants.

And the applicants ranged from serious aviation people through
traders diversifying out of shipping to fronts whose airlines
existed chiefly as foreign exchange vehicles. An operating
certificate was, among its other properties, a claim on dollars at
the official rate.

The register filled with entities for which the airline was not the
business but the instrument.

An instrument's maintenance budget is exactly as deep as its
usefulness.

## THE NUMBERS: The natural experiment nobody designed

America had run this experiment first, and the comparison is exact
enough to be decisive.

The United States deregulated its airlines in 1978. Entry
multiplied. Fare wars erupted. Marginal operators proliferated. The
safety establishment predicted carnage, and the press ran the
"fly-by-night airline" story for a decade.

The carnage did not come. American accident rates continued their
long decline straight through deregulation.

Nigeria deregulated in 1988. Entry multiplied. Marginal operators
proliferated.

The carnage came.

Two countries, the same policy, opposite results. What differed?

Not the policy. The *surrounding institutional field*.

In the American case, deregulation of the market was paired with —
in fact, preceded by — a mature apparatus for pricing risk back onto
firms:

- An aviation regulator whose inspectors could not be reassigned by a
  telephone call
- An independent accident investigator publishing findings within
  months
- An insurance market with actuarial teeth, whose surveyors walked
  hangars uninvited
- A tort system that priced negligence in nine figures
- A used-aircraft market so deep and well-documented that skimped
  maintenance destroyed resale value, making promise-keeping
  self-enforcing through asset prices

Nigeria ran the same market experiment with every one of those
strands missing.

This is the single most important comparative finding in the book,
and it generalizes far beyond aviation, so state it precisely:

*Deregulation is not a policy. It is a policy plus a field. Removing
a constraint transfers risk to whichever institution is left holding
it. If no institution is left holding it, the risk goes to whoever
cannot refuse it.*

In Nigerian aviation, that was the passenger. And, at Kano in 2002,
it would be people who were not even passengers.

## What flying felt like

Statistics anesthetize. Millions of Nigerians carry the texture of
that decade in their bodies.

You bought a ticket, but the ticket was the beginning of a
negotiation, not the end. Confirmation was social rather than
computational. Boarding could be a physical competition in which the
manifest was reconciled to reality afterward, if at all.

Hold that word — *manifest* — because in November 1996 and again in
October 2005, the country would discover at the worst possible
moments that knowing exactly who was aboard a Nigerian airliner was
not a routine certainty but an investigative task.

A society learns its record-keeping habits in ordinary time and pays
for them in extraordinary time.

And yet — this is the half of the truth that dignifies the era — the
system worked daily, in the sense that mattered to its passengers.
Aircraft mostly arrived. The pilots were often superb, precisely
because the environment demanded it.

Musa, a composite drawn from the accounts of several retired
Lagos-area air traffic controllers, describes the tower side of that
virtuosity: separation built from paper strips and pilots' honesty,
approaches flown by hand into airports whose landing systems had
died years before, radio calls relayed aircraft-to-aircraft across
the gaps in coverage.

"We were very good," he said. "That was the tragedy. We were so good
that nobody had to fix anything."

## The warnings that were filed

The professionals did not go quietly, and the documentary record
proves it.

The controllers' association filed memoranda on the failed radar and
the degraded radios with bureaucratic correctness, year after year.
The pilots' and engineers' unions raised the fleet's condition in
every negotiation.

Outside observers filed parallel findings. The World Bank's Nigeria
transport reviews of the 1980s read today like Part II's table of
contents drafted in advance: navigation aids below operational
standards, maintenance funding a fraction of requirements, cost
recovery nonexistent. ICAO's technical missions reported through the
decade in the polite subjunctive of United Nations documents. The
foreign airlines serving Lagos priced the deficiencies in privately,
tankering their own fuel from Europe and carrying their own ground
engineers.

Five sets of institutional eyes, across fifteen years, watching the
same decline and writing it down.

The failure of the 1980s was therefore never a failure of
information.

This matters enormously, because the standard prescription for
institutional failure in poor countries is better data — more
monitoring, more indicators, more dashboards. The Nigerian aviation
record refutes that prescription directly. The data existed. The
data was ignored.

What was missing was a mechanism to convert known facts into forced
choices.

This book will call that mechanism a *constituency*: some organized
interest whose welfare depends on the institution's rigor and which
is capable of imposing costs on those who let it decay.

Nigeria's aviation system had no constituency. It had only
professionals — who can file reports but cannot compel budgets — and
passengers, who were diffuse, unorganized, and, until 2005,
politically silent.

The eventual construction of a constituency, out of funerals, is the
story of Part II.

## 1990

Set the stage as the decade closes.

A country of roughly ninety million people. Treasury gutted.
Currency a fraction of itself. A military regime entering its
baroque late period.

A flag carrier in terminal decline, bleeding its competence into the
cockpits of the Gulf and Europe — and bleeding it selectively, in
the worst possible pattern: the training captains, the check airmen,
the senior licensed engineers, the people who reproduce competence
in others. An institution can survive losing talent. It cannot
survive losing its teachers, because that loss compounds.

A new private aviation sector, vigorous and undercapitalized, flying
the developed world's retired aircraft into airports built for the
boom and maintained by the bust.

A regulator that existed chiefly on paper, inside a ministry, under
soldiers, in a culture where every rule had its waiver.

A training college running on fumes.

And an accident investigation function that had never once, in
thirty years of independence, given the Nigerian public a full
account of why their dead were dead.

No single person intended this arrangement. That is the point of
these first three chapters, and the reason they precede the
wreckage. The system that would kill several thousand people over
the next two decades was not built by villains. It was accumulated
by deferral — each decade handing the next a slightly hollower
inheritance and a slightly more practiced vocabulary of denial.

In the image the safety literature made famous, the holes in the
layers of defense were drifting into alignment.

The system now required only traffic, time, and weather.

The 1990s would supply all three.

The first invoice arrived in July 1991, at a runway in Saudi Arabia,
in the oldest costume in Nigerian aviation: a chartered aircraft, a
foreign crew, and the pilgrims of the Hajj.
