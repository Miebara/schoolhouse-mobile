# 3. The Christmas Tree

## Ikeja maintenance base, 1988

It sat at the end of the apron, and everyone who worked there knew
what it was for. A Boeing 747 in the green-and-white livery of
Nigeria Airways, doors closed, tires flat, one engine cowling open
and empty.

The engineers called it the Christmas tree.

A hydraulic pump had gone to one sister ship and instruments to
another. Seats, slides, valves, a generator, a windshield. The
inventory of what had been taken off it existed only in the heads of
the men who took it.

"You would go out at night with a torch and a work order that did
not exist," Emeka said. "You take what you need. You write what you
can. In the morning the schedule flies."

Was it legal? He thought about the question longer than expected.
"Parts robbery is legal. There is a procedure for it. There are
forms." A pause. "There is a procedure for one aircraft. There is no
procedure for a way of life."

Controlled cannibalization is a legitimate practice at a well-run
airline. As a way of life it is a family burning the furniture
through a winter, rational on each cold night, catastrophic as a
strategy, and always diagnostic of an income that has stopped
matching a way of living.

## The dollar scissors

Nigeria's income had stopped matching everything. The oil price
broke in 1981 and collapsed in 1986. Federal revenue fell by more
than half in real terms. The naira, defended for a while and then
devalued in stages, lost more than nine-tenths of its dollar value
across the decade.

For most industries this was a severe recession. For aviation it was
closer to an extinction event, and the reason is a structural
feature of the business that almost no one outside it understands.
Aviation is a dollar industry that earns local currency.

Consider the cost stack of any airline anywhere in the world.
Aircraft acquisition or lease is priced in dollars, and so are spare
parts, engine overhauls, heavy maintenance checks, hull and
liability insurance, simulator training, manufacturer technical
support, and the navigation databases and charts without which the
aircraft cannot legally depart. On the most careful estimates of
airline cost structure, something on the order of two-thirds of a
carrier's non-fuel operating cost in a country like Nigeria is
denominated in a currency the carrier does not earn. Now consider
the revenue, which arrives as naira tickets sold in a
price-sensitive domestic market at fares that were politically
supervised whenever they became visible.

Between those two lines sat the central bank's foreign exchange
queue. Even a solvent airline holding naira waited months for an
allocation at the official rate, while its overseas creditors, who
were the lessors and insurers and parts suppliers, aged their
receivables and priced the waiting into the next contract. A
nine-tenths devaluation does not raise the cost of airworthiness by
some manageable percentage. It multiplies it roughly tenfold against
a revenue line that did not move, and it does so silently, without
appearing in any budget document as a decision.

Nothing announced this. No coup plotter ever listed the exchange
rate among his grievances. But when Part II of this book asks why
Nigerian aircraft in the 1990s were old, why the spares were scarce,
why recurrent training was deferred and the insurance was
threadbare, the answer begins here, with the least dramatic villain
in this story and one of the most lethal.

## What a maintenance check actually is

Deferred maintenance is an abstraction until you see what the money
buys.

An airliner's maintenance program is a pyramid of escalating
intimacy. At the base are the daily and transit checks, which mean
walk-arounds, fluid levels, and tire and brake condition, consuming
a few hours of labor and skipped by nobody, because their absence is
visible by lunchtime. Above them sit the lettered checks. An A-check
is an overnight job performed every few weeks or every few hundred
flight hours. Above those are the heavy checks, C and D, in which
the aircraft is withdrawn from service for weeks at a time, opened
to its bones, its systems functionally tested, and its structure
inspected for the cracks and corrosion that fatigue mathematics
predicts and only eyes and instruments confirm. A D-check on an
older narrow-body can consume thirty thousand labor hours and take
an aircraft off the line for a month or more.

The heavy checks are where airworthiness actually lives. Metal
fatigue is invisible, progressive, and unforgiving of schedule
slippage. The inspection intervals are not administrative
conveniences. They are set by engineering analysis of exactly how
long a detectable crack takes to become a critical one, and they
carry a deliberate safety factor, typically a division of the
predicted crack-growth life by two or more, so that a missed
inspection still leaves margin.

Defer the check and you are not skipping a formality. You are
spending that margin. You are flying into the interval the engineers
reserved for error, on the bet that this particular airframe is
average.

Old aircraft raise the stakes twice over. Their inspection programs
thicken with age, adding supplemental structural inspections,
corrosion control programs, and the aging-aircraft directives
written after a Boeing 737's cabin roof peeled open at altitude over
Hawaii in 1988, and they do so at precisely the moment their owners'
economics thin.

## The deferral calculus

Now place a maintenance decision inside the arithmetic of a 1990s
Nigerian carrier and watch how rational the wrong answer looks.

A heavy check costs seven figures in dollars, plus weeks of lost
revenue on an aircraft that may represent a third of the fleet.
Competing for the same scarce dollars in the same month are the
lease payment, whose default means repossession and therefore the
end of the company, the insurance premium, whose lapse means
immediate grounding, the fuel bill, without which nothing moves
today, and the payroll, without which the engineers leave for the
Gulf. Of those five claims on the same dollar, exactly one can be
deferred within the letter of the regulations, because deferral
mechanisms exist legitimately everywhere in aviation. The lease
payment cannot be deferred at all.

So every incentive in the structure, quarter after quarter, points
the scarce dollar away from the hangar. And the regulator of that
era, as the next chapters will show in detail, priced the deferral
at approximately zero.

The corners were not cut by monsters. They were cut by spreadsheets,
rationally, one defensible cell at a time, inside firms whose
bankruptcy was usually arriving faster than their airframes'
fatigue. This is the oldest finding in safety economics, and it is
worth stating in its general form because it governs far more than
aviation. Firms discount catastrophe at their own survival horizon.
A company that expects to die in three years is structurally
incapable of fearing a metal problem due in seven. Not unwilling but
incapable, because the risk lies beyond the entity's expected
lifespan and therefore beyond its decision frame.

The policy implication is uncomfortable for anyone who believes
markets discipline safety. In an industry where the product is a
maintained promise, the barrier to entry is the product. Cheap entry
is not consumer welfare. It is an unpriced transfer of risk from the
firm to the passenger.

## Rules and the negotiation

The money stopped, and politics then decided where the shortage
would be felt.

The politics of the 1980s were military rule, with a coup in 1983,
another in 1985, and beneath them the progressive fusion of state
and barracks that Nigerians simply call the military era. Military
government did three specific things to aviation.

It finished off management continuity, because chief executives now
rotated on the coup cycle rather than on any commercial one.

It starved the referee. Civil aviation oversight remained a
directorate inside the transport ministry, its inspectors on
civil-service pay that an airline first officer could triple, its
overseas training an early casualty of the dollar famine, and its
independence a legal fiction under governments in which the
airline's chairman outranked the director of civil aviation in every
register that Nigerian life recognizes.

And it normalized the exemption. Soldiers do not queue, and regimes
born of emergency govern by exception. This flight will carry whom
it is told. This aircraft will be released regardless. This
inspection finding will be waived, this time, because the general's
schedule requires it. Each exemption was small, and their sum
acquired a name that professionals of the era still use without
irony, which is the waiver culture.

Here is why that phrase deserves philosophical attention rather than
mere disapproval. A safety rule is not primarily an instruction. It
is a commitment device, and its value lies almost entirely in its
unconditionality, in the fact that it will bind at the exact moment
when binding is most expensive and least convenient. A rule that is
negotiable is not a weaker rule. It is a different kind of object
altogether. It is an opening bid.

Once it becomes an opening bid, its distributional consequences
follow immediately and inescapably, because the rule now binds in
inverse proportion to power. It binds fully on the powerless
operator with no telephone numbers, partially on the connected one,
and not at all on the general. This is the mechanism by which a
safety system becomes a class system. A rule negotiable for a
general is negotiable, eventually, for anyone with his phone number,
and the passenger in seat 14A, who has no phone numbers at all, has
no way of knowing which kind of rule was in force on the morning his
aircraft was released.

The reform of 2006, when it finally arrived, was at bottom nothing
more exotic than the repricing of waivers.

## The two fleets

No feature of the era exposes its priorities more cleanly than the
one fleet that stayed perfect.

While the national carrier ate itself and the navigation aids died,
the Presidential Air Fleet, meaning the executive aircraft of the
head of state and his court, was renewed, expanded, and maintained
to standards the civil system could only describe from memory. Its
budget lived outside meaningful scrutiny and its hangars were the
best in the country. By the 1990s Nigeria operated one of the larger
head-of-state fleets on earth, a distinction it retains, and in
several years its capital and maintenance provision exceeded the
entire operating budget of the civil aviation directorate that was
supposed to oversee the rest of the country's aircraft.

Treat that fleet, throughout this book, as the control group. It
answers definitively the question of whether the Nigerian state knew
how to keep aircraft airworthy. It knew. Airworthiness followed
power rather than capability, and every deficiency elsewhere in this
book was a choice of allocation rather than a limit of competence.

## The license factory

In 1986, under General Ibrahim Babangida, Nigeria adopted a
Structural Adjustment Programme of devaluation, deregulation, and
privatization, and its aviation consequence was at first glance
paradoxical. It created the Nigerian private airline.

The logic was orthodox. The national carrier was a fiscal hemorrhage
and a monopoly, and private capital would supply the capacity it no
longer could. Licenses flowed from 1988, and by the early 1990s a
swarm of carriers had risen, among them Okada Air, Kabo, ADC, and
dozens more.

Some were serious enterprises. Okada Air, the fleet of the Benin
businessman Gabriel Igbinedion, grew into the largest private
collection of airliners in Africa, assembled at scrap prices from
the world's retiring inventories and running to more than twenty
aircraft at its height. The traveling public, offered an alternative
to an airline that canceled by habit, voted with its feet, and
domestic aviation grew even as the economy shrank.

But examine what the license actually was, because its terms
predetermined the fleet that Part II buries. In mature systems an
air operator's certificate is among the hardest documents in
commerce to obtain, and proving out an airline's manuals,
maintenance arrangements, crew training, and financial fitness
consumes years and consultants. The Nigerian certificate of the late
1980s was, by the accounts of people who acquired them,
substantially a political and commercial document. Capital
requirements were nominal. Financial fitness review was cursory. The
technical examination was conducted by the starved directorate
described above, which meant a handful of inspectors certifying
dozens of applicants.

The applicants ranged from serious aviation people through traders
diversifying out of shipping to fronts whose airlines existed
chiefly as foreign exchange vehicles, since an operating certificate
was, among its other properties, a claim on dollars at the official
rate. The register filled with entities for which the airline was
not the business but the instrument, and an instrument's maintenance
budget is exactly as deep as its usefulness.

## The natural experiment nobody designed

America had run this experiment first, and the comparison is exact
enough to be decisive.

The United States deregulated its airlines in 1978. Entry
multiplied, fare wars erupted, and marginal operators proliferated.
The safety establishment predicted carnage and the press ran the
fly-by-night airline story for a decade. The carnage did not come.
American accident rates continued their long decline straight
through deregulation, falling by roughly an order of magnitude over
the three decades that followed.

Nigeria deregulated in 1988. Entry multiplied and marginal operators
proliferated. The carnage came.

Two countries, the same policy, opposite results. What differed was
not the policy but the surrounding institutional field. In the
American case, deregulation of the market was paired with, and in
fact preceded by, a mature apparatus for pricing risk back onto
firms. That apparatus consisted of an aviation regulator whose
inspectors could not be reassigned by a telephone call, an
independent accident investigator publishing findings within months,
an insurance market with actuarial teeth whose surveyors walked
hangars uninvited, a tort system that priced negligence in nine
figures, and a used-aircraft market so deep and well documented that
skimped maintenance destroyed resale value, which made
promise-keeping self-enforcing through asset prices. Nigeria ran the
same market experiment with every one of those five strands missing.

This is the single most important comparative finding in the book,
and it generalizes far beyond aviation, so it should be stated
precisely. Deregulation is not a policy. It is a policy plus a
field. Removing a constraint transfers risk to whichever institution
is left holding it, and if no institution is left holding it, the
risk goes to whoever cannot refuse it. In Nigerian aviation that was
the passenger, and at Kano in 2002 it would be people who were not
even passengers.

## What flying felt like

Statistics anesthetize, and millions of Nigerians carry the texture
of that decade in their bodies.

You bought a ticket, but the ticket was the beginning of a
negotiation rather than the end of one. Confirmation was social
rather than computational. Boarding could be a physical competition
in which the manifest was reconciled to reality afterward, if at
all.

Hold that word, manifest, because in November 1996 and again in
October 2005 the country would discover at the worst possible
moments that knowing exactly who was aboard a Nigerian airliner was
not a routine certainty but an investigative task. A society learns
its record-keeping habits in ordinary time and pays for them in
extraordinary time.

And yet the system worked daily, in the sense that mattered to its
passengers, and this is the half of the truth that dignifies the
era. Aircraft mostly arrived. The pilots were often superb,
precisely because the environment demanded it.

Musa, a composite drawn from the accounts of several retired
Lagos-area air traffic controllers, describes the tower side of that
virtuosity. Separation built from paper strips and pilots' honesty.
Approaches flown by hand into airports whose landing systems had
died years before. Radio calls relayed aircraft to aircraft across
the gaps in coverage.

"We were very good," he said. "That was the tragedy. We were so good
that nobody had to fix anything."

## The warnings that were filed

The professionals did not go quietly, and the documentary record
proves it.

The controllers' association filed memoranda on the failed radar and
the degraded radios with bureaucratic correctness, year after year.
The pilots' and engineers' unions raised the fleet's condition in
every negotiation.

Outside observers filed parallel findings. The World Bank's Nigeria
transport reviews of the 1980s read today like Part II's table of
contents drafted in advance, recording navigation aids below
operational standards, maintenance funding at a fraction of
requirements, and cost recovery that was effectively nonexistent.
ICAO's technical missions reported through the decade in the polite
subjunctive of United Nations documents. The foreign airlines
serving Lagos priced the deficiencies in privately, tankering their
own fuel from Europe and carrying their own ground engineers. Five
sets of institutional eyes, across fifteen years, watched the same
decline and wrote it down.

The failure of the 1980s was therefore never a failure of
information. This matters enormously, because the standard
prescription for institutional failure in poor countries is better
data, which is to say more monitoring, more indicators, and more
dashboards. The Nigerian aviation record refutes that prescription
directly. The data existed and the data was ignored.

What was missing was a mechanism to convert known facts into forced
choices. This book will call that mechanism a constituency, meaning
some organized interest whose welfare depends on the institution's
rigor and which is capable of imposing costs on those who let it
decay. Nigeria's aviation system had no constituency. It had only
professionals, who can file reports but cannot compel budgets, and
passengers, who were diffuse, unorganized, and, until 2005,
politically silent. The eventual construction of a constituency, out
of funerals, is the story of Part II.

## 1990

Set the stage as the decade closes. A country of roughly ninety
million people. A gutted treasury. A currency worth a fraction of
itself. A military regime entering its baroque late period.

A flag carrier in terminal decline, bleeding its competence into the
cockpits of the Gulf and Europe, and bleeding it selectively in the
worst possible pattern, because what left first were the training
captains, the check airmen, and the senior licensed engineers, the
people who reproduce competence in others. An institution can
survive losing talent. It cannot survive losing its teachers,
because that loss compounds.

A new private aviation sector, vigorous and undercapitalized, flying
the developed world's retired aircraft into airports built for the
boom and maintained by the bust. A regulator that existed chiefly on
paper, inside a ministry, under soldiers, in a culture where every
rule had its waiver. A training college running on fumes. And an
accident investigation function that had never once, in thirty years
of independence, given the Nigerian public a full account of why
their dead were dead.

No single person intended this arrangement. That is the point of
these first three chapters and the reason they precede the wreckage.
The system that would kill several thousand people over the next two
decades was not built by villains. It was accumulated by deferral,
each decade handing the next a slightly hollower inheritance and a
slightly more practiced vocabulary of denial.

In the image the safety literature made famous, the holes in the
layers of defense were drifting into alignment. The system now
required only traffic, time, and weather, and the 1990s would supply
all three.

The first invoice arrived in July 1991, at a runway in Saudi Arabia,
in the oldest costume in Nigerian aviation, which was a chartered
aircraft, a foreign crew, and the pilgrims of the Hajj.
