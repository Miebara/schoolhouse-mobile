# Author's Note

This book is based on interviews with participants and witnesses to
the events described — pilots, air traffic controllers, aircraft
engineers, accident investigators, regulators, airline owners,
ministers and their aides, emergency responders, and the families of
those who died.

Nearly all of the interviews were conducted on "deep background,"
meaning the information could be used but the sources would not be
identified. Many of these people are still working in Nigerian
aviation. Several signed confidentiality undertakings when they
joined the agencies they served. Some feared for their pensions.
Several feared more than that. The condition of anonymity was, in
almost every case, the condition of candor.

Where I attribute a thought, a conclusion, or a private statement to
someone, that information comes from the person, from a colleague
with direct knowledge, from contemporaneous notes, or from the
documentary record. Dialogue that appears in quotation marks was
recalled by at least one participant in the conversation, and where
possible checked against another. Recollection of speech across many
years is imperfect. I have used quotation marks only where a
participant was confident of the words. Where memory was of the
substance rather than the phrasing, the exchange appears without
quotation marks.

The documentary spine of this account is the public record: the
final reports of the accident investigators, where they exist; the
audit findings of the International Civil Aviation Organization and
the assessments of the United States Federal Aviation
Administration; the Civil Aviation Acts of 2006 and 2022; National
Assembly hearing records; and the daily coverage of the Nigerian
press, which for two decades documented what the state would not.

Some accounts could not be corroborated to my satisfaction and do
not appear here. In several important instances the official record
does not exist at all — the board of inquiry into the loss of a
Nigerian Air Force C-130 at Ejigbo in 1992 has never been published,
and after more than thirty years no Nigerian citizen can read why
158 officers died. Where the record is missing, this book says so.
The absence is part of the story.

A note to the reader on names. A small number of recurring figures
in these pages are composites, drawn from the accounts of several
people in the same position and identified as composites at first
appearance. They exist because the alternative was to leave the
people at the center of this story — the controller on the night
shift, the widow in the pension queue, the neighbor who reached the
wreckage first — without a voice. No public official is quoted in
this book saying anything he or she did not say.

# Prologue

## Abuja, Sunday, October 29, 2006

The call reached the presidential villa a little after nine in the
morning.

An aircraft was down. A Boeing 737. It had lifted off from Nnamdi
Azikiwe International Airport minutes earlier, into a wall of
thunderstorms that everyone at the field could see, and it had not
come back on the radio.

The duty officer had a partial manifest. He read it out.

The Sultan of Sokoto was on the airplane.

There was a silence on the line while that information found its
place. Muhammadu Maccido was not a passenger in the ordinary sense.
He was the Sarkin Musulmi — the commander of the faithful, the
spiritual authority for tens of millions of Nigerian Muslims,
occupant of a throne older than Nigeria itself. His son was aboard.
A senator was aboard. Ninety-six of the 105 people on ADC Airlines
Flight 053 would not survive the morning.

"That was the moment it stopped being about airplanes," one official
who was in the villa that day recalled. "It became about whether the
government could survive it."

Fourteen months earlier, a Boeing 737 belonging to Bellview Airlines
had disappeared three minutes after takeoff from Lagos, and the
Federal Republic of Nigeria had been unable to find it for twelve
hours. Ten months earlier, a DC-9 had come down short of the runway
at Port Harcourt and burned, and the airport fire trucks had arrived
without water, and sixty children of one Abuja school had died in
the flames.

Now a king.

By noon the roads into Sokoto were filling. By evening the whole
apparatus of the Nigerian state was moving north for a funeral.

And in an office in Lagos, a man with a doctorate in aeronautical
engineering from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology was
looking at the same news and doing a different calculation. Harold
Demuren had been running the Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority for
barely a year. He had inherited an agency that could not, in any
meaningful sense, regulate. Its inspectors were paid less than the
copilots they were supposed to examine. Its budget came from a
ministry that answered to politicians who owned airlines. Its
findings could be, and routinely were, reversed by telephone.

Demuren understood something about the morning that most of the
country did not yet understand.

For the first time in the history of Nigerian aviation, the people
who could fix the system had just discovered they were not safe from
it.

## What follows

This is the story of how a country's airplanes started falling out of
the sky, and how — for a period that no one predicted and few have
adequately explained — they stopped.

Between 1991 and 2012, Nigeria buried the passengers of seven
mass-casualty air disasters. A chartered pilgrimage flight burned in
the sky over Saudi Arabia with 261 people aboard. A military
transport carrying a generation of army officers went into a canal
at the edge of Lagos. Airliners came down in the lagoon, in the
forest, into a city neighborhood, into a residential street.

Then, after 2006, the crashes stopped. As this book is written, the
scheduled airlines of Nigeria have gone more than a decade without a
mass-casualty jet crash — the longest such run in the country's
history.

The reasons are not mysterious, but they have never been fully
assembled in one place. They involve a law passed in a hurry, a
funding formula, a man who would not take telephone calls, an
American audit, a group of grieving parents who would not go away,
and a political system that acted — briefly, decisively, and almost
by accident — in the public interest.

They also involve what has happened since: the reformer removed, the
reports that arrive five years late, the military aircraft that
still crash and are still never explained.

This is a reported account, assembled from the people who were in
the rooms.

It begins with the tires.
