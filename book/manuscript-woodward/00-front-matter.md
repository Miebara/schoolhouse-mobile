# Author's Note

This book is based on interviews with participants and witnesses to
the events described. They include pilots, air traffic controllers,
aircraft engineers, accident investigators, regulators, airline
owners, ministers and their aides, emergency responders, and the
families of those who died.

Nearly all of the interviews were conducted on what journalists call
deep background, meaning that the information could be used but the
sources would not be identified. Many of these people are still
working in Nigerian aviation, and several signed confidentiality
undertakings when they joined the agencies they served. Some feared
for their pensions and several feared a good deal more than that.
The condition of anonymity was, in almost every case, the condition
of candor.

Where I attribute a thought, a conclusion, or a private statement to
someone, that information comes from the person, from a colleague
with direct knowledge, from contemporaneous notes, or from the
documentary record. Dialogue that appears in quotation marks was
recalled by at least one participant in the conversation and, where
possible, checked against another. Recollection of speech across
many years is imperfect, so I have used quotation marks only where a
participant was confident of the words. Where memory was of the
substance rather than the phrasing, the exchange appears without
quotation marks.

The documentary spine of this account is the public record. It
consists of the final reports of the accident investigators where
they exist, the audit findings of the International Civil Aviation
Organization, the assessments of the United States Federal Aviation
Administration, the Civil Aviation Acts of 2006 and 2022, National
Assembly hearing records, and the daily coverage of the Nigerian
press, which for two decades documented what the state would not.

Some accounts could not be corroborated to my satisfaction and do
not appear here. In several important instances the official record
does not exist at all. The board of inquiry into the loss of a
Nigerian Air Force C-130 at Ejigbo in 1992 has never been published,
and after more than thirty years no Nigerian citizen can read why
158 officers died. Where the record is missing, this book says so,
because the absence is part of the story.

A note to the reader on names. A small number of recurring figures
in these pages are composites, drawn from the accounts of several
people in the same position and identified as composites at first
appearance. They exist because the alternative was to leave the
people at the center of this story without a voice, and those people
are the controller on the night shift, the widow in the pension
queue, and the neighbor who reached the wreckage first. No public
official is quoted in this book saying anything he or she did not
say.

# Prologue

## Abuja, Sunday, October 29, 2006

The call reached the presidential villa a little after nine in the
morning. An aircraft was down, a Boeing 737, and it had lifted off
from Nnamdi Azikiwe International Airport minutes earlier into a
wall of thunderstorms that everyone at the field could see. It had
not come back on the radio.

The duty officer had a partial manifest, and he read it out.

The Sultan of Sokoto was on the airplane.

There was a silence on the line while that information found its
place. Muhammadu Maccido was not a passenger in the ordinary sense.
He was the Sarkin Musulmi, the commander of the faithful, the
spiritual authority for tens of millions of Nigerian Muslims and the
occupant of a throne older than Nigeria itself. His son was aboard.
A senator was aboard. Ninety-six of the 105 people on ADC Airlines
Flight 053 would not survive the morning.

"That was the moment it stopped being about airplanes," one official
who was in the villa that day recalled. "It became about whether the
government could survive it."

The morning arrived at the end of a run that had already exhausted
the country's capacity for shock. Fourteen months earlier a Boeing
737 belonging to Bellview Airlines had disappeared three minutes
after takeoff from Lagos, and the Federal Republic of Nigeria had
been unable to find it for twelve hours. Ten months earlier a DC-9
had come down short of the runway at Port Harcourt and burned, the
airport fire trucks had arrived without water, and sixty children of
one Abuja school had died in the flames.

Now a king.

By noon the roads into Sokoto were filling, and by evening the whole
apparatus of the Nigerian state was moving north for a funeral.

In an office in Lagos, a man with a doctorate in aeronautical
engineering from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology was
looking at the same news and doing a different calculation. Harold
Demuren had been running the Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority for
barely a year, and he had inherited an agency that could not, in any
meaningful sense, regulate. Its inspectors were paid less than the
copilots they were supposed to examine. Its budget came from a
ministry that answered to politicians who owned airlines. Its
findings could be, and routinely were, reversed by telephone.

Demuren understood something about the morning that most of the
country did not yet understand. For the first time in the history of
Nigerian aviation, the people who could fix the system had just
discovered that they were not safe from it.

## What follows

This is the story of how a country's airplanes started falling out
of the sky, and how, for a period that no one predicted and few have
adequately explained, they stopped.

Between 1991 and 2012, Nigeria buried the passengers of seven
mass-casualty air disasters. A chartered pilgrimage flight burned in
the sky over Saudi Arabia with 261 people aboard. A military
transport carrying a generation of army officers went into a canal
at the edge of Lagos. Airliners came down in the lagoon, in the
forest, into a city neighborhood in Kano, and into a residential
street in Iju-Ishaga. In the fourteen months between October 2005
and November 2006 alone, more than three hundred people died in
Nigerian civil aviation, which is a toll that would have constituted
a national emergency in any country and constituted one here.

Then, after 2006, the crashes stopped. As this book is written, the
scheduled airlines of Nigeria have gone more than a decade without a
mass-casualty jet crash, which is the longest such run in the
country's history and one of the most under-examined public policy
successes on the African continent.

The reasons are not mysterious, but they have never been fully
assembled in one place. They involve a law passed in a hurry, a
funding formula that gave a regulator its own money, a man who would
not take telephone calls, an American audit that turned a technical
question into a political prize, a group of grieving parents who
would not go away, and a political system that acted briefly,
decisively, and almost by accident in the public interest.

They also involve what has happened since. The reformer was removed.
The reports arrive five years late. The military aircraft still
crash and are still never explained.

This is a reported account, assembled from the people who were in
the rooms.

It begins with the tires.
