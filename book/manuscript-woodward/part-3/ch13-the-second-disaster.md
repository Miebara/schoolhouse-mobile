# 13. The Second Disaster

## Five photographs

Line them up, because together they form a single image taken five
times.

At Ejigbo in 1992, residents wade into a canal with rope while the
military organizes a cordon. On the lagoon in 1996, fishermen pole
through a debris field the state has not yet located. At Lisa in
2005, a Boeing missing for twelve hours inside the Lagos commuter
belt is found by farmers walking toward smoke. At Port Harcourt in
2005, fire tenders arrive at a burning, survivable wreck without
water. At Iju-Ishaga in 2012, ten thousand volunteers arrive and no
crane does.

Five different decades' worth of aircraft, weather, and causation,
and one constant so reliable it amounts to a national signature. In
the window between impact and institutional arrival, the Nigerian
state is represented at its own disasters by whoever happens to live
nearby. The first disaster belongs to physics. The second belongs to
the state.

## Why this chapter carries so much weight

Emergency response is the purest test of state capacity that exists,
and the reason is worth stating precisely. It cannot be faked,
deferred, or performed selectively.

Every other function of a state can be simulated for an audience.
Statistics can be re-based. Buildings can be commissioned.
Legislation can be passed and never implemented. Attention can be
allocated to whatever is being measured this quarter. Emergency
response is the state's entire apparatus of communications,
logistics, command, medicine, and honesty, summoned without notice,
against a clock, before witnesses. The golden hour does not care
what the roadmap said.

## The physics of survivability

The survivability literature, built over decades from accident
forensics, establishes a fact that reorganizes how Part II should be
read, which is that a large fraction of transport aircraft accidents
are survivable, or partially survivable, at impact. Studies of
accident cohorts in the mature systems routinely find survival rates
above ninety percent across all accidents and well above half in the
serious ones.

The popular image of the air crash as instantaneous annihilation
with nothing to be done describes some events in this book
accurately, including the lagoon and Lisa, but it does not describe
the class. Runway excursions, undershoots like Sosoliso, and
post-impact fires like Kano and Iju-Ishaga distribute survival
unevenly through the cabin, and the toll is then negotiated between
fire and extinguishing agent, between trauma and the clock, and
between the trapped and whoever arrives with cutting equipment. The
negotiation is over in roughly the time it takes to read this
chapter.

The international standards are calibrated to that negotiation.
Airport firefighting rules specify response to any point of the
runway within about three minutes with rated quantities of water and
foam, because an intact cabin adjacent to a kerosene fire resists
for a survivable interval measured in low single-digit minutes.
Which produces the arithmetic that Chapter 8 stated and this chapter
generalizes. The difference between a two-minute response and a
ten-minute response is not eight minutes of delay. It is everyone
the fire reaches second.

## The multiplication problem

An emergency system is a race run in stages, comprising detection,
dispatch, access, extrication, stabilization, transport, and
definitive care, and its performance is not the sum of those stages
but their product. That distinction is the most important
operational idea in this chapter, so consider what it means. If each
of seven stages performs at eighty percent, which is a grade most
managers would accept, the system's end-to-end performance is eighty
percent multiplied seven times, which comes to twenty-one percent.

A failure at any single stage does not add to the delay. It
multiplies through everything downstream, because the later stages
cannot begin until the earlier ones complete. Which is why the
Nigerian record reads as it does. Each disaster in Part II failed at
several stages simultaneously, with twelve hours to find, no water
at the fire, no crane under the slab, and no burn beds at the end,
and multiplication of failures is not addition. It is collapse.

## The stage nobody examines

The last link in the chain is the least discussed and, in Nigeria,
the most binding. Severe burns, which are aviation's signature
injury, are among the most resource-intensive conditions in all of
medicine, requiring dedicated units, sterile environments, skin
banking, and specialist teams working around the clock for weeks or
months per patient. Nigeria's burn capacity through the crisis
decades was a handful of units concentrated in two or three cities
and chronically short of everything, amounting to a few dozen
dedicated beds for a population that passed a hundred and forty
million. A mass-burn event would have overwhelmed the national
capability even if every ambulance in the country had flown.

The medical odysseys of the Sosoliso survivors, involving years of
reconstructive care sourced abroad and funded by family means and
public appeal, were not exceptional bad luck. They were the system
stating its capacity honestly. The definitive-care stage for the
injuries that its airports were certified to produce did not exist
inside the country.

And the toll has never been counted. Burn and crush patients die in
the second week as reliably as in the first hour, through infection,
renal failure, and the complications a mature trauma system exists
to prevent, and no Nigerian study has ever enumerated those deaths.
Disaster mortality in Nigeria is counted at the scene or not at all,
which flatters every emergency response the statistics touch and
means the true cost of the second disaster in this book is
systematically understated in every number it contains.

## The archipelago

Nigeria does not lack emergency institutions. It lacks an emergency
system. The distinction organizes everything here, and the inventory
makes it visible. Take a mass-casualty aviation event and list who
responds.

There is the airport fire service, jurisdictionally confident to the
fence line and legally uncertain beyond it, which matters enormously
since most of the crashes in this book landed outside the fence.
There are the state fire services, chronically the most starved
agencies in Nigerian federalism. There is the national emergency
management agency, created in 1999 with a mandate whose breadth,
covering floods, epidemics, displacement, and crashes, has always
exceeded a budget sized for press statements. There are its state
counterparts, existing on paper in most states and in fact in few.
There is the military, which possesses the actual heavy capabilities
of engineering plant, airlift, and divers, but which is
constitutionally and culturally a guest at civilian disasters,
arriving through liaison channels that must be improvised each time.
There are the police, who control scenes. There is the Red Cross and
its volunteer kin, who frequently outperform everyone. And there are
the hospitals, federal and state and private, each admitting by its
own rules.

Every island is staffed by people who mostly try. No bridge between
the islands is load-tested before the day it must carry a disaster.
The after-action finding at the top of every mass-casualty event in
this book, had anyone written after-action reports, would have been
the same phrase, which is interface failure. The catastrophe falls
into the water between agencies.

One island has grown notably since 2005 and earns its paragraph.
Lagos State's emergency agency, built by a government wealthy and
embarrassed enough to invest, was fielding equipment and doctrine
visibly ahead of the federal layer by the time of the Dana response,
and has handled the state's grim portfolio of building collapses and
tanker fires with accumulating competence since. It matters
analytically because it demonstrates the sub-national route.
Response capacity, unlike regulatory sovereignty, is buildable at
state level, where a governor's incentives of visibility, credit,
and a defined constituency who vote can align with it.

## The offshore exception

One sector of Nigerian aviation has run, for decades, a response and
safety regime close to world standard, and its existence inside the
same country under the same state is this chapter's most clarifying
exhibit and, once understood, its most damning.

The oil and gas fields of the Niger Delta are serviced by one of the
world's denser helicopter economies, flying crew changes, logistics,
and medevac under contract to the international majors. The flying
is objectively hazardous, involving offshore platforms, salt
corrosion, and night medevacs over water. Yet the sector's practices
would be recognizable in Aberdeen or the Gulf of Mexico. Aircraft
carry flotation gear and automatically deployable emergency beacons.
Passengers, who are the roughnecks of the delta, are put through
helicopter underwater escape training as a condition of the
manifest. Flight-data monitoring runs on every airframe, years
before the scheduled airlines adopted it. And a standing
search-and-rescue capability is contractually guaranteed, with
dedicated aircraft, trained crews, and response times measured
against the survival window of a man in tropical water.

None of it was provided by the Nigerian state. It was specified,
audited, and paid for by the customers, meaning the oil majors,
whose own liability and reputational machinery, tightened after each
industry disaster worldwide, made safety performance a condition of
the contract and priced it accordingly.

Now extract the theorems, because there are three and they are all
important. The geography was never the constraint, because the same
delta skies that hosted Sosoliso's undefended passengers hosted,
simultaneously, world-standard rescue. The mechanism is the presence
of a party who is punished by the death, because the roughneck flew
safe on account of an institution that was structurally penalized
when he died, and the passenger on the domestic schedule had no such
institution standing behind him. And the capability was purchasable
at Nigerian price levels, on Nigerian soil, by organizations that
decided to want it.

That last theorem is the one that removes every excuse. The offshore
sector is a privatized preview of the public system this book's
final chapters specify. The scandal is not that it exists. The
scandal is what its existence certifies, which is that the Nigerian
state has watched, for forty years, a working demonstration of
exactly what its citizens' survival would cost, and has declined, so
far, to place the order.

## Counting what rescue is worth

Public investment lives or dies by cost-benefit arithmetic, and the
neglect of emergency response has always hidden inside a quiet
assumption that the arithmetic is unfavorable and that rescue
capacity is a rich country's luxury. The assumption collapses on
contact.

Standard policy analysis prices life-saving interventions against
each other using cost per life-year saved as the common denominator,
and on such a table aviation disaster response looks at first glance
like a poor buy, given rare events and concentrated costs. The first
glance misprices three things.

It misprices the shared nature of the capacity. The firefighting
trucks, the trauma protocols, the mass-casualty coordination, and
the family-assistance machinery are not aviation assets. They are
all-hazards assets, and the same investment answers the tanker
explosion, the market fire, the collapsed building, and the flood.
Nigeria's calendar supplies those at a rate that makes utilization
anything but rare, and aviation is merely the domain where the
standard is written down.

It misprices the trust externality. Every failed response compounds
the civic distrust that taxes every other state function, and
Chapter 10 showed that distrust arriving physically at Iju-Ishaga in
the form of a crowd that would not clear. Conversely, visible
competence pays into the same account. When a US Airways crew
ditched an aircraft in the Hudson River in 2009 and every one of the
155 people aboard was recovered alive, ferries were alongside within
four minutes, because a city had drilled the scenario, and what that
response was worth to American institutional confidence exceeded, by
an enormous margin, any budget line it consumed.

And it misprices the alternative, which is the second disaster's
casualties, the uncounted second-week dead, the decades of
litigation and grief-work, and the international reputation
repriced, with the lease rates and insurance premia that follow it.
All of these are costs. They are simply booked nowhere.

Run honestly, the package this chapter recommends prices out among
the cheapest life-years a Nigerian budget can buy, cheaper than most
health interventions and cheaper by orders of magnitude than the
terminal buildings that outrank it in every appropriation cycle. The
arithmetic was never unfavorable. It was never done. And the reason
it was never done is the one this book keeps returning to, which is
that the constituency that would have demanded it, being the future
victims of the unresponded crash, is by definition not yet
assembled. They do not know who they are.

## The volunteer nation

Every response narrative in this book has the same first responder,
who is the neighbor, and a serious national doctrine would stop
treating that fact as an embarrassment to be cordoned off and start
treating it as the system's largest unregistered asset.

The research literature converged long ago on what practitioners
call the first-responder myth. In mass-casualty events everywhere,
from Mexico City to Kobe and through the great urban catastrophes of
the record, the majority of live rescues are performed not by
professionals but by bystanders and neighbors in the first hour,
before any siren arrives. This is not a failure of professional
systems. It is an ineliminable property of geometry and time,
because the people nearest the event arrive first by virtue of being
nearest. Which means the professional system's highest-leverage move
is not to replace the crowd but to equip and interface with it.

The instruments exist and are cheap. Community emergency response
training teaches light rescue, fire suppression, and triage to
ordinary residents in a weekend course, on models that many
countries run at scale. Interface protocols let an incident
commander convert a crowd from obstruction into organized labor
through cordon assistance, litter bearing, supply chains, and
traffic control. And registration of standing community assets, such
as the fishing fleets of this book's coastal chapters, the
neighborhood associations, and the traders' unions and religious
brigades that already possess rosters, radios, and internal
discipline, turns them into auxiliary units with training days and
call-out lists.

Nigeria is, by civic structure, almost ideally suited to this
doctrine. Its neighborhoods are organized to a density most rich
countries lost generations ago, and its crowds have proven, from
Ejigbo to Iju-Ishaga, both willing and brave. What they have never
been offered is the weekend course, the interface protocol, or the
respect of being planned for. A state that trained one percent of
Lagos in light rescue would have fielded, at Dana 992, ten thousand
assets instead of ten thousand obstacles, at the cost of roughly one
ministry's annual conference line. Of all this chapter's cheap
purchases this is the cheapest, because the workforce has already
volunteered. It has been volunteering, unpaid and unthanked, since
September 1992.

## The doctrine on one page

Since this chapter keeps demanding a national response doctrine, it
should sketch one, offering the skeleton any competent drafting team
could flesh out in a month, to demonstrate how little mystery the
assignment holds.

On command, there should be one incident commander per event,
designated by pre-agreed rule, being the airport authority inside
the fence and the metropolitan emergency authority outside it, with
every other agency subordinated for the duration by statute rather
than by negotiation. Dual command is no command, and every failed
response in this book had five commanders.

On communication, there should be a national mutual-aid radio
protocol and a single emergency coordination number for responders,
with contact trees tested monthly by actual calls rather than annual
memoranda.

On capability, firefighting substance should be audited unannounced,
covering agent stocks, pump function, and access routes rather than
the certificate on the wall. Every international airport should be
paired by written, exercised agreement with its municipal fire,
ambulance, and hospital network. And regional caches of the heavy
kit, meaning cranes, cutting gear, and lighting towers, should be
mapped and contracted in advance, so that the Dana crane is never
again sourced by telephone on a Sunday afternoon.

On casualty flow, a regulating function should assign patients to
hospitals by capability and load rather than by proximity and rumor,
and a single casualty register should be opened within the hour and
staffed to answer families.

On families, there should be notification standards, a family
information center at every event, remains management under forensic
protocol, and a named liaison per family, which is the machinery the
United States legislated after its own disasters of 1996 at almost
no cost, and which changed the civic meaning of American aviation
accidents by extending the state's competence to grief itself.

And on learning, the after-action report should be mandatory,
published, and answered.

One page. Nothing novel. Every line already paid for, somewhere in
this book, by someone.

## The case against despair

There is a version of this book's argument that counsels
hopelessness. If crashes are symptoms of institutional depth, and
institutional depth takes generations, then the dying continues
until history is fixed. This chapter is the rebuttal, and it rests
on a distinction that matters enormously for anyone trying to
improve a weak state.

Some reforms are deep, requiring constitutional arrangements, tenure
protections, funding formulas, and the political coalitions to
defend them. Chapter 11's regulator is deep and Chapter 12's capital
markets are deep, and both take decades and can be reversed. The
second disaster is shallow. It is cheap, separable, and technically
undemanding. Filling a fire tender does not require an autonomous
regulator. Writing an incident command doctrine does not require
constitutional reform. Running a drill does not require a change of
political culture.

Which means a country can learn to rescue competently long before it
learns to regulate competently, and every life saved in the interval
is banked regardless of the deeper reforms' fate. That is not a
small consolation. It is a strategy. If Nigerian public life took a
single sentence from this book into its next budget cycle, it should
be this one. Fill the trucks, write the doctrine, run the drill.

The dead of Port Harcourt were failed twice, and the second failure
was the one that any state, in that year, with that budget, could
simply have declined.

## After

What a state does after its disasters extends past the scene. The
trucks answer for the first hours and the report answers for the
years. And the report, in Nigeria, has its own history of not
arriving. That silence, which is the most self-inflicted of all the
patterns in this book and the cheapest to end, is the next chapter.
