# 13. The Second Disaster

## Five photographs

Line them up, because together they form a single image taken five
times.

**Ejigbo, 1992.** Residents wading into a canal with rope, while the
military organizes a cordon.

**The lagoon, 1996.** Fishermen poling through a debris field the
state has not yet located.

**Lisa, 2005.** A Boeing missing for twelve hours inside the Lagos
commuter belt, found by farmers walking toward smoke.

**Port Harcourt, 2005.** Fire tenders arriving at a burning,
survivable wreck without water.

**Iju-Ishaga, 2012.** Ten thousand volunteers, and no crane.

Five different decades' worth of aircraft, weather, and causation.

One constant, so reliable it amounts to a national signature: in the
window between impact and institutional arrival, the Nigerian state
is represented at its own disasters by whoever happens to live
nearby.

The first disaster belongs to physics.

The second belongs to the state.

## Why this chapter carries so much weight

Emergency response is the purest test of state capacity that exists,
and the reason is worth stating precisely.

It cannot be faked, deferred, or performed selectively.

Every other function of a state can be simulated for an audience.
Statistics can be re-based. Buildings can be commissioned.
Legislation can be passed and never implemented. Attention can be
allocated to whatever is being measured this quarter.

Emergency response is the state's entire apparatus — communications,
logistics, command, medicine, honesty — summoned without notice,
against a clock, before witnesses.

The golden hour does not care what the roadmap said.

## THE NUMBERS: The physics of survivability

The survivability literature, built over decades from accident
forensics, establishes a fact that reorganizes how Part II should be
read.

A large fraction of transport aircraft accidents are survivable, or
partially survivable, at impact.

The popular image of the air crash — instantaneous annihilation,
nothing to be done — describes some events in this book accurately.
The lagoon. Lisa. But it does not describe the *class*.

Runway excursions. Undershoots like Sosoliso. Post-impact fires like
Kano and Iju-Ishaga. In these, the impact distributes survival
unevenly through the cabin, and the toll is then *negotiated*.

Between fire and extinguishing agent.

Between trauma and the clock.

Between the trapped and whoever arrives with cutting equipment.

The negotiation is over in roughly the time it takes to read this
chapter.

The international standards are calibrated to that negotiation.
Airport firefighting rules specify response to any point of the
runway within about three minutes, with rated quantities of water
and foam, because an intact cabin adjacent to a kerosene fire
resists for a survivable interval measured in low single-digit
minutes.

Which produces the arithmetic that Chapter 8 stated and this chapter
generalizes:

The difference between a two-minute response and a ten-minute
response is not eight minutes of delay.

It is everyone the fire reaches second.

## The multiplication problem

An emergency system is a race run in stages: detection, dispatch,
access, extrication, stabilization, transport, definitive care.

Its performance is not the *sum* of those stages. It is the
**product**.

That distinction is the most important operational idea in this
chapter, so consider what it means.

If each of seven stages performs at eighty percent — a grade most
managers would accept — the system's end-to-end performance is
eighty percent multiplied seven times.

Twenty-one percent.

A failure at any single stage does not add to the delay. It
multiplies through everything downstream, because the later stages
cannot begin until the earlier ones complete.

Which is why the Nigerian record reads as it does. Each disaster in
Part II failed at *several* stages simultaneously — twelve hours to
find, no water at the fire, no crane under the slab, no burn beds at
the end — and multiplication of failures is not addition.

It is collapse.

## The stage nobody examines

The last link in the chain is the least discussed and, in Nigeria,
the most binding.

Severe burns — aviation's signature injury — are among the most
resource-intensive conditions in all of medicine. They require
dedicated units, sterile environments, skin banking, and specialist
teams working around the clock for weeks or months per patient.

Nigeria's burn capacity through the crisis decades was a handful of
units, concentrated in two or three cities, chronically short of
everything.

A mass-burn event would have overwhelmed the *national* capability
even if every ambulance in the country had flown.

The medical odysseys of the Sosoliso survivors — years of
reconstructive care sourced abroad, funded by family means and
public appeal — were not exceptional bad luck.

They were the system stating its capacity honestly.

The definitive-care stage for the injuries that its airports were
certified to produce did not exist inside the country.

And the toll has never been counted. Burn and crush patients die in
the second week as reliably as in the first hour, through infection,
renal failure, and the complications a mature trauma system exists
to prevent. No Nigerian study has ever enumerated those deaths.

Disaster mortality in Nigeria is counted at the scene or not at all
— which flatters every emergency response the statistics touch, and
which means the true cost of the second disaster in this book is
systematically understated in every number it contains.

## The archipelago

Nigeria does not lack emergency institutions.

It lacks an emergency *system*.

The distinction organizes everything here, and the inventory makes
it visible. Take a mass-casualty aviation event and list who
responds:

The **airport fire service**, jurisdictionally confident to the
fence line and legally uncertain beyond it — which matters
enormously, since most of the crashes in this book landed outside
the fence.

The **state fire services**, chronically the most starved agencies
in Nigerian federalism.

The **national emergency management agency**, created in 1999 with a
mandate whose breadth — floods, epidemics, displacement, crashes —
has always exceeded a budget sized for press statements.

Its **state counterparts**, existing on paper in most states and in
fact in few.

The **military**, which possesses the actual heavy capabilities —
engineering plant, airlift, divers — but is constitutionally and
culturally a guest at civilian disasters, arriving through liaison
channels that must be improvised each time.

The **police**, who control scenes.

The **Red Cross** and its volunteer kin, who frequently outperform
everyone.

And the **hospitals** — federal, state, and private — each admitting
by its own rules.

Every island is staffed by people who mostly try.

No bridge between the islands is load-tested before the day it must
carry a disaster.

The after-action finding at the top of every mass-casualty event in
this book — had anyone written after-action reports — would have
been the same phrase.

*Interface failure.*

The catastrophe falls into the water between agencies.

One island has grown notably since 2005 and earns its paragraph.
Lagos State's emergency agency, built by a government wealthy and
embarrassed enough to invest, was fielding equipment and doctrine
visibly ahead of the federal layer by the time of the Dana response,
and has handled the state's grim portfolio of building collapses and
tanker fires with accumulating competence since.

It matters analytically because it demonstrates the sub-national
route.

Response capacity, unlike regulatory sovereignty, is buildable at
state level — where a governor's incentives (visibility, credit, a
defined constituency who vote) can align with it.

## THE ARGUMENT: The offshore exception

One sector of Nigerian aviation has run, for decades, a response and
safety regime close to world standard.

Its existence inside the same country, under the same state, is this
chapter's most clarifying exhibit — and, once understood, its most
damning.

The oil and gas fields of the Niger Delta are serviced by one of the
world's denser helicopter economies: crew changes, logistics,
medevac, flown under contract to the international majors.

The flying is objectively hazardous. Offshore platforms. Salt
corrosion. Night medevacs over water.

Yet the sector's practices would be recognizable in Aberdeen or the
Gulf of Mexico.

Aircraft with flotation gear and automatically deployable emergency
beacons. Passengers — the roughnecks of the delta — put through
helicopter underwater escape training as a *condition of the
manifest*. Flight-data monitoring on every airframe, years before
the scheduled airlines adopted it. And a standing search-and-rescue
capability, contractually guaranteed, with dedicated aircraft,
trained crews, and response times measured against the survival
window of a man in tropical water.

None of it was provided by the Nigerian state.

It was specified, audited, and paid for by the *customers* — the oil
majors, whose own liability and reputational machinery, tightened
after each industry disaster worldwide, made safety performance a
condition of the contract and priced it accordingly.

Now extract the theorems, because there are three and they are all
important.

**The geography was never the constraint.** The same delta skies
that hosted Sosoliso's undefended passengers hosted, simultaneously,
world-standard rescue.

**The mechanism is the presence of a party who is punished by the
death.** The roughneck flew safe because an institution existed —
the major's liability and reputation apparatus — that was
structurally penalized when he died. The passenger on the domestic
schedule had no such institution standing behind him.

**And the capability was purchasable at Nigerian price levels, on
Nigerian soil, by organizations that decided to want it.**

That last theorem is the one that removes every excuse.

The offshore sector is a privatized preview of the public system
this book's final chapters specify.

The scandal is not that it exists.

The scandal is what its existence certifies: that the Nigerian state
has watched, for forty years, a working demonstration of exactly
what its citizens' survival would cost.

And has declined, so far, to place the order.

## THE ECONOMICS: Counting what rescue is worth

Public investment lives or dies by cost-benefit arithmetic, and the
neglect of emergency response has always hidden inside a quiet
assumption that the arithmetic is unfavorable — that rescue capacity
is a rich country's luxury.

The assumption collapses on contact.

Standard policy analysis prices life-saving interventions against
each other, using cost per life-year saved as the common
denominator. On such a table, aviation disaster response looks, at
first glance, like a poor buy: rare events, concentrated costs.

The first glance misprices three things.

**It misprices the shared nature of the capacity.** The firefighting
trucks, the trauma protocols, the mass-casualty coordination, the
family-assistance machinery — none of these are aviation assets.
They are all-hazards assets. The same investment answers the tanker
explosion, the market fire, the collapsed building, the flood. And
Nigeria's calendar supplies those at a rate that makes utilization
anything but rare. Aviation is merely the domain where the standard
is written down.

**It misprices the trust externality.** Every failed response
compounds the civic distrust that taxes every other state function —
and Chapter 10 showed that distrust arriving physically at
Iju-Ishaga in the form of a crowd that would not clear. Conversely,
visible competence pays into the same account. When a US Airways
crew ditched an aircraft in the Hudson River in 2009 and every one
of the 155 people aboard was recovered alive, ferries were alongside
within four minutes — because a city had drilled the scenario. What
that response was worth to American institutional confidence
exceeded, by an enormous margin, any budget line it consumed.

**And it misprices the alternative.** The second disaster's
casualties. The uncounted second-week dead. The decades of
litigation and grief-work. The international reputation repriced,
and the lease rates and insurance premia that follow it. All of
these are costs. They are simply booked nowhere.

Run honestly, the package this chapter recommends prices out among
the cheapest life-years a Nigerian budget can buy — cheaper than
most health interventions, and cheaper by orders of magnitude than
the terminal buildings that outrank it in every appropriation cycle.

The arithmetic was never unfavorable.

It was never done.

And the reason it was never done is the one this book keeps
returning to: the constituency that would have demanded it — the
future victims of the unresponded crash — are, by definition, not
yet assembled.

They do not know who they are.

## THE ARGUMENT: The volunteer nation

Every response narrative in this book has the same first responder.

The neighbor.

And a serious national doctrine would stop treating that fact as an
embarrassment to be cordoned off, and start treating it as the
system's largest unregistered asset.

The research literature converged long ago on what practitioners
call the first-responder myth. In mass-casualty events everywhere —
Mexico City, Kobe, the great urban catastrophes of the record — the
*majority of live rescues* are performed not by professionals but by
bystanders and neighbors in the first hour, before any siren
arrives.

This is not a failure of professional systems. It is an ineliminable
property of geometry and time. The people nearest the event arrive
first because they are nearest.

Which means the professional system's highest-leverage move is not
to replace the crowd.

It is to equip and interface with it.

The instruments exist and are cheap:

**Community emergency response training** — light rescue, fire
suppression, and triage, taught to ordinary residents in a weekend
course, on models that many countries run at scale.

**Interface protocols** that let an incident commander convert a
crowd from obstruction into organized labor: cordon assistance,
litter bearing, supply chains, traffic control.

**Registration of standing community assets** — the fishing fleets
of this book's coastal chapters, the neighborhood associations, the
traders' unions and religious brigades that already possess rosters,
radios, and internal discipline — as auxiliary units with training
days and call-out lists.

Nigeria is, by civic structure, almost ideally suited to this
doctrine. Its neighborhoods are organized to a density most rich
countries lost generations ago. And its crowds have proven, from
Ejigbo to Iju-Ishaga, both willing and brave.

What they have never been offered is the weekend course, the
interface protocol, or the respect of being planned for.

A state that trained one percent of Lagos in light rescue would have
fielded, at Dana 992, ten thousand assets instead of ten thousand
obstacles — at the cost, roughly, of one ministry's annual
conference line.

Of all this chapter's cheap purchases, this is the cheapest.

The workforce has already volunteered.

It has been volunteering, unpaid and unthanked, since September
1992.

## The doctrine on one page

Since this chapter keeps demanding a national response doctrine, it
should sketch one — the skeleton any competent drafting team could
flesh out in a month, offered here to demonstrate how little mystery
the assignment holds.

**Command.** One incident commander per event, designated by
pre-agreed rule — the airport authority inside the fence, the
metropolitan emergency authority outside it — with every other
agency subordinated for the duration *by statute*, not by
negotiation. Dual command is no command. Every failed response in
this book had five commanders.

**Communication.** A national mutual-aid radio protocol and a single
emergency coordination number for responders. Contact trees tested
monthly by actual calls, not annual memoranda.

**Capability.** Firefighting substance audited unannounced — agent
stocks, pump function, access routes, not the certificate on the
wall. Every international airport paired by written, exercised
agreement with its municipal fire, ambulance, and hospital network.
And regional caches of the heavy kit — cranes, cutting gear,
lighting towers — mapped and contracted in advance, so that the Dana
crane is never again sourced by telephone on a Sunday afternoon.

**Casualty flow.** A regulating function that assigns patients to
hospitals by capability and load, rather than by proximity and
rumor. A single casualty register opened within the hour and staffed
to answer families.

**Families.** Notification standards. A family information center at
every event. Remains management under forensic protocol. A named
liaison per family — the machinery the United States legislated
after its own disasters of 1996, at almost no cost, which changed
the civic meaning of American aviation accidents by extending the
state's competence to grief itself.

**Learning.** The after-action report mandatory, published, and
answered.

One page. Nothing novel. Every line already paid for, somewhere in
this book, by someone.

## THE ARGUMENT: The case against despair

There is a version of this book's argument that counsels
hopelessness.

If crashes are symptoms of institutional depth, and institutional
depth takes generations, then the dying continues until history is
fixed.

This chapter is the rebuttal, and it rests on a distinction that
matters enormously for anyone trying to improve a weak state.

Some reforms are **deep**. They require constitutional arrangements,
tenure protections, funding formulas, and the political coalitions to
defend them. Chapter 11's regulator is deep. Chapter 12's capital
markets are deep. These take decades and can be reversed.

The second disaster is **shallow**.

It is cheap, separable, and technically undemanding. Filling a fire
tender does not require an autonomous regulator. Writing an incident
command doctrine does not require constitutional reform. Running a
drill does not require a change of political culture.

Which means a country can learn to *rescue* competently long before
it learns to *regulate* competently — and every life saved in the
interval is banked regardless of the deeper reforms' fate.

That is not a small consolation. It is a strategy.

If Nigerian public life took a single sentence from this book into
its next budget cycle, it should be this one:

Fill the trucks. Write the doctrine. Run the drill.

The dead of Port Harcourt were failed twice.

And the second failure was the one that any state, in that year,
with that budget, could simply have declined.

## After

What a state does after its disasters extends past the scene.

The trucks answer for the first hours.

The report answers for the years.

And the report, in Nigeria, has its own history of not arriving.
That silence — the most self-inflicted of all the patterns in this
book, and the cheapest to end — is the next chapter.
