# PART III — THE PATTERNS

# 11. The Friday Problem

## Lagos, a Friday, some year between 1988 and 2005

The year hardly matters, and that is the point of the story.

An inspector of the civil aviation authority, Zaria-trained,
seasoned abroad, twelve years in the job, walks the ramp and finds
an aircraft he cannot legally release. The specifics vary in the
tellings, sometimes a defect beyond its deferral window, sometimes a
lapsed certification, sometimes a crew out of duty time. He grounds
it. The airline's owner makes two telephone calls.

By Monday, one of three things has happened. The finding has been
overruled from above the inspector's head. Or the inspector is on
course, sent for training in a distant city with his ramp assignment
reallocated. Or, and this is the commonest outcome because systems
teach faster than they punish, nothing has happened at all, because
the inspector, knowing the first two outcomes by heart, wrote the
finding narrowly enough to be waivable in the first place.

The aircraft flies Monday evening. Nothing about that weekend
appears in any record that an international auditor will ever see.
Multiply that Friday by twenty years and you have Part II of this
book.

## The question this chapter asks

The question is not why individual Nigerians failed to stop those
weekends. Many tried, some paid for it, and their memoranda are in
the files. The question is why the institution was built so that
trying was heroic, because a regulatory system that requires brave
inspectors is already a broken system. The entire art of
institutional design is to make the safe finding the cheap one.

## Capture is universal, architecture is not

Start with the universal, because Nigerian exceptionalism is the
enemy of understanding here.

Regulatory capture, meaning the process by which an agency comes to
serve the industry it is meant to oversee, is not an African
pathology. It is the default tendency of all regulation everywhere,
and it was formalized by the economist George Stigler more than
fifty years ago. The mechanism is structural rather than moral. The
regulated party is concentrated, informed, and permanently
interested, employing people whose full-time job is to influence the
regulator, knowing the technical details better than the regulator
does because it operates the equipment, and being present at every
meeting, every rulemaking, and every hearing, forever. The public is
diffuse, technically outmatched, and interested only after
disasters, because no individual passenger has an incentive
proportionate to the cost of engagement, and the rational passenger
does not read airworthiness directives. That asymmetry never sleeps.
It operates in Tokyo and Toulouse exactly as it operates in Lagos.

If you doubt it, consider the most consequential capture scandal of
the modern era, which happened in Washington. The Boeing 737 MAX
crashes of 2018 and 2019 killed 346 people in Indonesia and
Ethiopia. The investigations that followed exposed an American
regulator that had delegated core certification judgments to Boeing
itself, under a decades-refined system of designated representatives
in which company engineers acted as the government's certifiers,
while a commercial deadline drove schedule pressure down through the
organization and internal warnings were filed and buried. Change the
nouns and Chapter 4's soft tires inflate themselves.

Capture happens everywhere. What varies, and it is the only thing
that varies and therefore this chapter's entire subject, is what
happens next. After the MAX there were congressional hearings with
subpoena power within months, internal messages extracted and
printed, independent technical review boards, a criminal settlement
in the billions of dollars, and legislation rewriting the delegation
system. The captured system audited its own capture in public and
repriced it. The Nigerian analogue of that aftermath, for two
decades, was a filing cabinet. Every country has these problems. Not
every country's problems get subpoenaed.

## Three regimes, one experiment

Nigerian oversight history is best read as a natural experiment in
the pricing of capture, run in three regimes on the same population.

The first regime was the directorate, running from 1960 to 1999,
with oversight sitting as a department inside the transport ministry.
It had no separate legal personality and no budget of its own. Its
head was a civil servant removable at pleasure and its inspectors
sat on scales that an airline first officer tripled. In that
architecture capture required no corruption, no conspiracy, and not
even a telephone call, because the regulator was simply inside the
political structure whose interests conflicted with its function.
The waiver culture was not a distortion of that design. It was the
design, expressing itself. The audit trail of the first regime is
Chapters 4 through 8.

The second regime was the authority on paper, running from 1999 to
2006. A Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority was created as a formally
distinct body, but it was funded by annual allocation, led at
pleasure, and legally toothless against the ministry. It was
autonomy as letterhead. The era's record, from Kano in 2002 to the
catastrophes of 2005, demonstrates the central finding of the
comparative literature on regulatory design, which policymakers keep
rediscovering the expensive way. Formal independence without fiscal
and tenure independence is capture with better stationery.

The third regime was the 2006 architecture, built on statutory
levies, tenured leadership, a published rulebook, and imported
witnesses. It was the same country, the same industry, the same
salary differentials, and the same generals with the same
telephones, and it produced a measurably different output within two
years.

The experiment's power lies entirely in what it held constant. No
cultural transformation occurred between 2005 and 2008. No
generation turned over. The officials were substantially the same
people. Nigeria did not become more honest, more competent, or more
modern in eighteen months. The architecture changed, and the Friday
of this chapter's opening began ending differently, because the
Monday overrule now had to survive a paper trail auditable from
Montreal, a Director-General who could not be quietly reassigned,
and a levy stream that no minister could pinch.

## The four load-bearing members

Strip the 2006 reform to its mechanism and four members carry the
structure. Each is exportable to any weak-capacity regulator
anywhere, and each is also reversible, which is why Chapter 10 ends
where it does.

The first is funding by formula, examined in Chapter 9. An agency
financed by statutory levy can be disciplined only by changing the
law in public, so capture must come out of hiding to operate, and
hiding is its entire comparative advantage.

The second is tenure at the top. An office occupiable only at
pleasure will be filled by someone whose planning horizon is that
pleasure, while tenure converts a posting into a position and
therefore makes it worth the reputational investment of a
professional with alternatives.

The third is transparency as a ratchet, and this one is subtler than
the accountability rhetoric suggests. Publication does not primarily
inform the public, most of whom will never read an operating
certificate register. It creates third-party evidence that makes
quiet reversal expensive. A waiver granted against a published rule
is a documented act, while a waiver granted against an internal
circular is a conversation.

The fourth is imported witnesses. The deliberate coupling to
external audit, through ICAO's programme, the FAA's assessment, and
the industry's own operational audit, gave the domestic regulator
something it had never possessed, which was a constituency for its
own integrity that outranked its domestic predators.

That fourth member deserves a moment, because it inverts this book's
colonial theme in a way that reformers elsewhere should study.
Chapter 1 described oversight located abroad as Nigeria's founding
defect. The 2006 reform chose foreign scrutiny, sovereignly, as
scaffolding, and the difference between being audited by an empire
and hiring an auditor is the difference between subordination and
strategy. Where internal accountability is unbuilt, external
accountability can hold the structure while it grows, but only if
some domestic actor has both the authority and the motive to invite
it in.

## The ministry problem

One actor has escaped this chapter's focus so far, and the
regulator's history is illegible without it. The Federal Ministry of
Aviation is the political principal above the whole alphabet of
agencies, and it is the channel through which every pathology in
this book has, at one time or another, flowed.

The ministry's structural situation invites its behavior, and the
concentration of its portfolio is striking. It controls, formally or
informally depending on the statutory era, appointments to the
boards of the regulator, the airports authority, the airspace
agency, the meteorological agency, and the training college. It
sponsors the sector's legislation and defends its budget. It
negotiates the bilateral air services agreements that allocate
international routes, which are the sector's single richest
discretionary prize and are transacted state to state, largely
outside public scrutiny, with enormous commercial consequences for
identifiable firms. And it owns the ceremonial surface of terminal
commissionings, national-carrier announcements, and ribbon-cuttings
from which political credit is harvested. It is, in short, a machine
for converting aviation into politics, sitting directly atop the
machine this chapter wants for converting aviation into safety.

Now the tenure data. Nigerian aviation ministers since 1999 have
averaged barely two years in office. Each arrives with a roadmap, a
slate of board nominees, and a flagship project, and each departs
before the roadmap's second budget cycle. The agencies below have
learned to metabolize the turnover the way Nigeria Airways
metabolized its chief executives, which is by treating each minister
as weather.

The reform proposals that follow from this diagnosis have converged,
for decades, on a quiet structural heresy, which is to shrink the
ministry. Fold aviation into a general transport portfolio with the
agencies as autonomous authorities, which is the pattern across much
of the developed world. Strip appointment and commercial powers back
to a policy-only mandate. Nominate professionally qualified boards
through public process with staggered terms that no single
administration can sweep. No Nigerian government has yet found a
reason to legislate away its own patronage surface, which is the
proposal's realistic prognosis in a single sentence.

But naming the apex predator accurately matters analytically. The
NCAA's deepest dilemma is not that it faces a powerful industry,
because every regulator faces that. It is that its own hierarchy is
the industry's most reliable advocate.

## The market for inspectors

Beneath the architecture sits a labor market, and no honest account
of regulatory capacity survives without it.

Consider what an airworthiness surveyor actually is. He is a
licensed aircraft maintenance engineer with a decade of hangar
seniority, retrained in regulation and audit, and fluent in the
documentation systems of two or three manufacturers. Now consider
who else wants that person. The airlines want him to run their
quality departments. The lessors want him to inspect aircraft on
delivery and redelivery. The Gulf carriers want him. The
international agencies want him. The maintenance organizations of
Europe and the Middle East want him. All of them pay in dollars, and
several offer housing and school fees. The flight operations
inspectorate has the same problem in a more acute form, because it
needs current or recently current airline captains, whose price is
set in Dubai and Doha.

Every capable authority on earth manages this squeeze with some
combination of three tools. The first is pay parity mechanisms that
peg technical salaries to the industry rather than to the civil
service scale, and the 2006 levy funding made a version of this
possible in Nigeria, whose partial erosion is among the plateau's
quietest dangers. The second is a secondment culture that borrows
industry professionals for defined tours behind conflict-of-interest
firewalls. The third is prestige, the intangible that lets the great
regulators retain people the market prices higher, because the work
confers standing that money cannot.

That third tool is the interesting one, because it is manufactured
by exactly the things this book keeps prescribing for other reasons.
Published work. Visible independence. International respect. The
institution's name meaning something in a technical conversation. A
regulator whose findings are overruled by telephone cannot pay in
prestige, and one whose Category 1 rating the whole industry cites
can. The Demuren-era recruitment from the diaspora succeeded on that
currency as much as on salary, and engineers took real pay cuts to
join an institution that had begun to mean something. Which yields a
conclusion that no budget office has ever recorded. Institutional
dignity is a compensation line, and governments that spend it down
discover the deficit in their vacancy lists five years later, when
the last inspector who remembers the standard retires.

## What it costs to say no

Enforcement is not a decision. It is a budget. Follow a single
refusal through a regulator's accounting and watch the costs
accumulate.

The inspector's finding costs his time, and defensible documentation
costs more of it, in photographs, records, statements, and a file
that will survive review. If the operator contests, the authority
pays counsel through months or years of litigation, against firms
whose lawyers bill more per week than the inspector earns per year,
and in many jurisdictions, including Nigeria's for much of this
period, the aircraft may continue flying under interim orders while
the case runs. If the operator escalates politically, the authority
spends its two scarcest currencies, which are leadership attention
and political capital that could have been spent on something else.
And if the operator simply defies, since operating while suspended
is a documented Nigerian genre, the authority discovers that its
ultimate instruments run through police, courts, and prosecutors it
does not control and must recruit case by case.

So every refusal is an expenditure, and an agency's enforcement
posture is exactly as strong as its enforcement budget. This is why
the comparative literature finds that effective regulators are
distinguished less by the quality of their rulebooks than by three
unglamorous features, which are their litigation war chests, their
summary powers to impose fines and groundings that take effect
before appeal rather than after, and their institutionalized working
relationships with prosecutors.

The 2006 reform funded the Nigerian version partially. The levy
stream paid for lawyers and the Act's summary powers survived their
first court tests. The plateau erodes it quietly, because every raid
on the war chest for overheads, every settlement that rewards
defiance, and every appeal that suspends a grounding shifts the
industry's calculated price of non-compliance downward. And operators
price regulatory risk as accurately as they price fuel, because they
have to. Which means the question of whether the NCAA is strong is
answerable at any moment by a number that no audit publishes, namely
what defying it actually costs, in naira, this year.

## What a rule is

There is a philosophical point underneath the waiver culture that
deserves to be argued rather than merely deplored, because it
explains why the reform had to take the specific form it took.

A safety rule is not primarily an instruction. It is a commitment
device, and its value lies almost entirely in its unconditionality,
in the fact that it will bind at the exact moment when binding is
most expensive and least convenient. That is the only moment at
which a safety rule does any work, because a rule that binds when
compliance is easy has achieved nothing. Which means a rule that is
negotiable is not a weaker rule. It is a categorically different
object. It is an opening bid.

Once it becomes an opening bid, its distributional consequences
follow immediately and inescapably, because the rule now binds in
inverse proportion to power. It binds fully on the operator with no
telephone numbers, partially on the connected one, and not at all on
the general. This is the mechanism by which a safety system silently
becomes a class system, and it operates without anyone intending it
and without a single corrupt payment, purely through the
differential cost of obtaining an exception.

Now add the passenger's epistemic position, which is the part that
makes it intolerable. The passenger in seat 14A cannot observe which
kind of rule was in force on the morning his aircraft was released.
He cannot audit the maintenance records. He cannot know whether the
finding was written narrowly. He is, in the strict economic sense,
purchasing a credence good, meaning a product whose quality he
cannot evaluate before consumption, after consumption, or in most
cases ever. Credence goods are the classic case where markets fail
without institutions. You cannot shop your way to safety. You can
only be governed into it.

And that is why the reform of 2006, stripped of everything else, was
at bottom the repricing of waivers. Not new rules, because the rules
had always existed, but a new price for breaking them.

## After

The men and women of the opening Friday deserve this chapter's last
word. Every system this book has anatomized was, on its worst days,
staffed by some inspector who wrote the finding anyway, knowing
exactly what Monday held. Their memoranda are in the archives and
some of them are still working.

The 2006 reform did not create Nigerian regulatory virtue. It
stopped taxing it at confiscatory rates. That is all institutional
design ever does, which is to arrange matters so that ordinary
conscientiousness rather than heroism is sufficient. And it is
enough, because the difference it makes is countable in the crashes
that stopped.

The question the plateau poses is whether a country that built such
architecture once, under compulsion, can learn to maintain it
without compulsion's return. The next three chapters examine the
economic, operational, and cultural forces that will supply the
compulsion if the answer is no.
