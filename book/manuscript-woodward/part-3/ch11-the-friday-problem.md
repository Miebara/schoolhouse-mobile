# PART III — THE PATTERNS

# 11. The Friday Problem

## Lagos, a Friday, some year between 1988 and 2005

The year hardly matters. That is the point of the story.

An inspector of the civil aviation authority — Zaria-trained,
seasoned abroad, twelve years in the job — walks the ramp and finds
an aircraft he cannot legally release. The specifics vary in the
tellings: a defect beyond its deferral window, a lapsed
certification, a crew out of duty time.

He grounds it.

The airline's owner makes two telephone calls.

By Monday, one of three things has happened.

The finding has been overruled from above the inspector's head.

Or the inspector is "on course" — sent for training in a distant
city, his ramp assignment reallocated.

Or — and this is the commonest outcome, because systems teach faster
than they punish — nothing has happened at all. Because the
inspector, knowing the first two outcomes by heart, wrote the
finding narrowly enough to be waivable in the first place.

The aircraft flies Monday evening.

Nothing about that weekend appears in any record that an
international auditor will ever see.

Multiply that Friday by twenty years and you have Part II of this
book.

## The question this chapter asks

The question is not why individual Nigerians failed to stop those
weekends. Many tried. Some paid for it. Their memoranda are in the
files.

The question is why the institution was built so that trying was
heroic.

Because a regulatory system that requires brave inspectors is
already a broken system.

The entire art of institutional design is to make the safe finding
the *cheap* one.

## THE ARGUMENT: Capture is universal. Architecture is not.

Start with the universal, because Nigerian exceptionalism is the
enemy of understanding here.

Regulatory capture — the process by which an agency comes to serve
the industry it is meant to oversee — is not an African pathology.
It is the default tendency of all regulation everywhere, and it was
formalized by the economist George Stigler more than fifty years
ago.

The mechanism is structural, not moral.

The regulated party is **concentrated, informed, and permanently
interested**. It employs people whose full-time job is to influence
the regulator. It knows the technical details better than the
regulator does, because it operates the equipment. And it is present
at every meeting, every rulemaking, every hearing, forever.

The public is **diffuse, technically outmatched, and interested only
after disasters**. No individual passenger has an incentive
proportionate to the cost of engagement. The rational passenger does
not read airworthiness directives.

That asymmetry never sleeps. It operates in Tokyo and Toulouse
exactly as it operates in Lagos.

If you doubt it, consider the most consequential capture scandal of
the modern era, which happened in Washington.

The Boeing 737 MAX crashes of 2018 and 2019 killed 346 people in
Indonesia and Ethiopia. The investigations that followed exposed an
American regulator that had delegated core certification judgments
to Boeing itself, under a decades-refined system of designated
representatives — company engineers acting as the government's
certifiers — while a commercial deadline drove schedule pressure
down through the organization, and internal warnings were filed and
buried.

Change the nouns and Chapter 4's soft tires inflate themselves.

Capture happens everywhere.

What varies — the only thing that varies, and therefore this
chapter's entire subject — is what happens *next*.

After the MAX: congressional hearings with subpoena power within
months. Internal messages extracted and printed. Independent
technical review boards. A criminal settlement in the billions.
Legislation rewriting the delegation system.

The captured system audited its own capture in public, and repriced
it.

The Nigerian analogue of that aftermath, for two decades, was a
filing cabinet.

Every country has these problems.

Not every country's problems get subpoenaed.

## THE NUMBERS: Three regimes, one experiment

Nigerian oversight history is best read as a natural experiment in
the pricing of capture, run in three regimes on the same population.

**Regime One: the directorate (1960–1999).**

Oversight as a department inside the transport ministry. No separate
legal personality. No budget of its own. Its head a civil servant
removable at pleasure. Its inspectors on scales that an airline
first officer tripled.

In this architecture, capture required no corruption, no conspiracy,
not even a telephone call. The regulator was simply *inside* the
political structure whose interests conflicted with its function.

The waiver culture was not a distortion of that design.

It was the design, expressing itself.

The audit trail of Regime One is Chapters 4 through 8.

**Regime Two: the authority on paper (1999–2006).**

A Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority created as a formally distinct
body — but funded by annual allocation, led at pleasure, and legally
toothless against the ministry.

Autonomy as letterhead.

The era's record — Kano in 2002, the catastrophes of 2005 —
demonstrates the central finding of the comparative literature on
regulatory design, which is worth stating in italics because
policymakers keep rediscovering it the expensive way:

*Formal independence without fiscal and tenure independence is
capture with better stationery.*

**Regime Three: the 2006 architecture.**

Statutory levies. Tenured leadership. A published rulebook. Imported
witnesses.

Same country. Same industry. Same salary differentials. The same
generals with the same telephones.

And a measurably different output within two years.

The experiment's power lies entirely in what it held constant.

No cultural transformation occurred between 2005 and 2008. No
generation turned over. The officials were substantially the same
people. Nigeria did not become more honest, more competent, or more
modern in eighteen months.

The architecture changed.

And the Friday of this chapter's opening began ending differently —
because the Monday overrule now had to survive a paper trail
auditable from Montreal, a Director-General who could not be quietly
reassigned, and a levy stream that no minister could pinch.

## The four load-bearing members

Strip the 2006 reform to its mechanism and four members carry the
structure. Each is exportable to any weak-capacity regulator
anywhere. Each is also reversible, which is why Chapter 10 ends
where it does.

**Funding by formula.** Examined in Chapter 9. An agency financed by
statutory levy can be disciplined only by changing the law in
public. Capture must come out of hiding to operate, and hiding is
its entire comparative advantage.

**Tenure at the top.** An office occupiable only at pleasure will be
filled by someone whose planning horizon is that pleasure. Tenure
converts a posting into a position — and therefore makes it worth
the reputational investment of a professional with alternatives.

**Transparency as a ratchet.** This one is subtler than the
accountability rhetoric suggests. Publication does not primarily
inform the public, most of whom will never read an operating
certificate register. It creates *third-party evidence* that makes
quiet reversal expensive. A waiver granted against a published rule
is a documented act. A waiver granted against an internal circular
is a conversation.

**Imported witnesses.** The deliberate coupling to external audit —
ICAO's programme, the FAA's assessment, the industry's own
operational audit — gave the domestic regulator something it had
never possessed: a constituency for its own integrity that outranked
its domestic predators.

That fourth member deserves a moment, because it inverts this book's
colonial theme in a way that reformers elsewhere should study.

Chapter 1 described oversight located abroad as Nigeria's founding
defect.

The 2006 reform *chose* foreign scrutiny, sovereignly, as
scaffolding.

The difference between being audited by an empire and hiring an
auditor is the difference between subordination and strategy.

Where internal accountability is unbuilt, external accountability
can hold the structure while it grows — but only if some domestic
actor has both the authority and the motive to invite it in.

## THE ARGUMENT: The ministry problem

One actor has escaped this chapter's focus so far, and the
regulator's history is illegible without it.

The Federal Ministry of Aviation — the political principal above the
whole alphabet of agencies, and the channel through which every
pathology in this book has, at one time or another, flowed.

The ministry's structural situation invites its behavior, and it is
worth laying out the portfolio, because the concentration is
striking.

It controls, formally or informally depending on the statutory era,
appointments to the boards of the regulator, the airports authority,
the airspace agency, the meteorological agency, and the training
college.

It sponsors the sector's legislation and defends its budget.

It negotiates the bilateral air services agreements that allocate
international routes — the sector's single richest discretionary
prize, and one that is transacted state-to-state, largely outside
public scrutiny, with enormous commercial consequences for
identifiable firms.

And it owns the ceremonial surface: the terminal commissionings, the
national-carrier announcements, the ribbon-cuttings from which
political credit is harvested.

It is, in short, a machine for converting aviation into politics —
sitting directly atop the machine this chapter wants for converting
aviation into safety.

Now the tenure data.

Nigerian aviation ministers since 1999 have averaged barely two
years in office. Each arrives with a roadmap, a slate of board
nominees, and a flagship project. Each departs before the roadmap's
second budget cycle.

The agencies below have learned to metabolize the turnover the way
Nigeria Airways metabolized its chief executives.

By treating each minister as weather.

The reform proposals that follow from this diagnosis have converged,
for decades, on a quiet structural heresy: shrink the ministry.

Fold aviation into a general transport portfolio, with the agencies
as autonomous authorities — the pattern across much of the developed
world. Strip appointment and commercial powers back to a policy-only
mandate. Nominate professionally qualified boards through public
process with staggered terms that no single administration can
sweep.

No Nigerian government has yet found a reason to legislate away its
own patronage surface, which is the proposal's realistic prognosis
in a single sentence.

But naming the apex predator accurately matters analytically.

The NCAA's deepest dilemma is not that it faces a powerful industry.
Every regulator faces that.

It is that its own hierarchy is the industry's most reliable
advocate.

## THE ECONOMICS: The market for inspectors

Beneath the architecture sits a labor market, and no honest account
of regulatory capacity survives without it.

Consider what an airworthiness surveyor actually is.

A licensed aircraft maintenance engineer with a decade of hangar
seniority, retrained in regulation and audit, fluent in the
documentation systems of two or three manufacturers.

Now consider who else wants that person.

The airlines want him, to run their quality departments. The lessors
want him, to inspect aircraft on delivery and redelivery. The Gulf
carriers want him. The international agencies want him. The
maintenance organizations of Europe and the Middle East want him.

All of them pay in dollars, and several offer housing and school
fees.

The flight operations inspectorate has the same problem in a more
acute form: it needs current or recently current airline captains,
whose price is set in Dubai and Doha.

Every capable authority on earth manages this squeeze with some
combination of three tools.

**Pay parity mechanisms** that peg technical salaries to the
industry rather than to the civil service scale. The 2006 levy
funding made a version of this possible in Nigeria, and its partial
erosion is among the plateau's quietest dangers.

**Secondment cultures** that borrow industry professionals for
defined tours behind conflict-of-interest firewalls.

And **prestige** — the intangible that lets the great regulators
retain people the market prices higher, because the work confers
standing that money cannot.

That third tool is the interesting one, because it is manufactured
by exactly the things this book keeps prescribing for other reasons.

Published work. Visible independence. International respect. The
institution's name meaning something in a technical conversation.

A regulator whose findings are overruled by telephone cannot pay in
prestige. One whose Category 1 rating the whole industry cites can.

The Demuren-era recruitment from the diaspora succeeded on that
currency as much as on salary — engineers took real pay cuts to join
an institution that had begun to mean something.

Which yields a conclusion that no budget office has ever recorded:

*Institutional dignity is a compensation line.*

Governments that spend it down discover the deficit in their vacancy
lists, five years later, when the last inspector who remembers the
standard retires.

## THE ECONOMICS: What it costs to say no

Enforcement is not a decision. It is a budget.

Follow a single "no" through a regulator's accounting and watch the
costs accumulate.

The inspector's finding costs his time. Defensible documentation
costs more of it — photographs, records, statements, a file that
will survive review.

If the operator contests, the authority pays counsel through months
or years of litigation, against firms whose lawyers bill more per
week than the inspector earns per year. And in many jurisdictions,
including Nigeria's for much of this period, the aircraft may
continue flying under interim orders while the case runs.

If the operator escalates politically, the authority spends its two
scarcest currencies: leadership attention, and political capital
that could have been spent on something else.

And if the operator simply defies — operating while suspended is a
documented Nigerian genre — the authority discovers that its
ultimate instruments run through police, courts, and prosecutors it
does not control and must recruit case by case.

So every "no" is an expenditure. And an agency's enforcement posture
is exactly as strong as its enforcement budget.

This is why the comparative literature finds that effective
regulators are distinguished less by the quality of their rulebooks
than by three unglamorous features: their litigation war chests,
their **summary powers** — fines and groundings that take effect
before appeal rather than after — and their institutionalized
working relationships with prosecutors.

The 2006 reform funded the Nigerian version partially. The levy
stream paid for lawyers. The Act's summary powers survived their
first court tests.

The plateau erodes it quietly. Every raid on the war chest for
overheads, every settlement that rewards defiance, every appeal that
suspends a grounding shifts the industry's calculated price of
non-compliance downward.

And operators price regulatory risk as accurately as they price
fuel. They have to.

Which means the question *is the NCAA strong?* is answerable at any
moment by a number that no audit publishes:

What does defying it actually cost, in naira, this year?

## THE ARGUMENT: What a rule is

There is a philosophical point underneath the waiver culture that
deserves to be argued rather than merely deplored, because it
explains why the reform had to take the specific form it took.

A safety rule is not primarily an instruction.

It is a **commitment device**.

Its value lies almost entirely in its unconditionality — in the fact
that it will bind at the exact moment when binding is most expensive
and least convenient. That is the *only* moment at which a safety
rule does any work. A rule that binds when compliance is easy has
achieved nothing.

Which means a rule that is negotiable is not a weaker rule.

It is a categorically different object. It is an opening bid.

And once it becomes an opening bid, its distributional consequences
follow immediately and inescapably.

The rule now binds in inverse proportion to power.

It binds fully on the operator with no telephone numbers.

It binds partially on the connected one.

It does not bind at all on the general.

This is the mechanism by which a safety system silently becomes a
class system — and it operates without anyone intending it, without
a single corrupt payment, purely through the differential cost of
obtaining an exception.

Now add the passenger's epistemic position, which is the part that
makes it intolerable.

The passenger in seat 14A cannot observe which kind of rule was in
force on the morning his aircraft was released. He cannot audit the
maintenance records. He cannot know whether the finding was written
narrowly. He is, in the strict economic sense, purchasing a
**credence good** — a product whose quality he cannot evaluate
before consumption, after consumption, or in most cases ever.

Credence goods are the classic case where markets fail without
institutions. You cannot shop your way to safety. You can only be
governed into it.

And that is why the reform of 2006, stripped of everything else, was
at bottom the repricing of waivers.

Not new rules. The rules had always existed.

A new price for breaking them.

## After

The men and women of the opening Friday deserve this chapter's last
word.

Every system this book has anatomized was, on its worst days,
staffed by some inspector who wrote the finding anyway, knowing
exactly what Monday held. Their memoranda are in the archives. Some
of them are still working.

The 2006 reform did not create Nigerian regulatory virtue.

It stopped taxing it at confiscatory rates.

That is all institutional design ever does — arrange matters so that
ordinary conscientiousness, rather than heroism, is sufficient.

And it is enough. The difference it makes is countable in the
crashes that stopped.

The question the plateau poses is whether a country that built such
architecture once, under compulsion, can learn to maintain it
without compulsion's return.

The next three chapters examine the forces — economic, operational,
and cultural — that will supply the compulsion if the answer is no.
