# 12. The Arithmetic of Cutting Corners

## The biography of an airframe

Follow one aircraft through its life, because the journey explains
more about Nigerian aviation than any regulation ever written.

A Boeing 727 rolls out of Renton, Washington, in 1968 and goes to
work for a major American carrier.

For fifteen years it lives the pampered life of a flagship fleet.
Maintained to the manufacturer's full program, in the airline's own
hangars, by engineers the airline trained. Its records are
immaculate. Every part is traceable. Every cycle is logged. Its
technical file is, in a real sense, worth more than its aluminum.

In the early 1980s, deregulation's fare wars and a new generation of
quieter, thriftier jets push it down the food chain.

To a charter operator. Then a second-tier scheduled carrier. Then a
third.

Each owner extracts value while investing less. The maintenance
remains legal — this is important — but it is outsourced, then
outsourced again, and the records pass from custodian to custodian
like a title deed accumulating stamps in a jurisdiction that keeps
changing.

By the early 1990s the aircraft is twenty-five years old.

It is worth less than one of its own overhauled engines. Noise
regulations have closed most of the developed world's airports to
it. Its residual value is approaching scrap.

And it is now, for the first time, precisely affordable to a
naira-earning, dollar-starved startup in Lagos.

It is ferried south.

Nothing about that transaction is illegal.

And nothing about that aircraft is safe *by default*.

At twenty-five years, an airframe is a promise that must be actively
kept — heavy checks costing millions of dollars, parts priced like
jewelry, engineering judgment costing most of all.

And it has just been sold, precisely and only, to the buyer least
equipped to keep it.

## THE ECONOMICS: The sorting machine

That last sentence is not an accident of Nigerian history. It is the
predictable output of a market operating exactly as markets operate,
and the mechanism deserves to be stated formally because it governs
far more than aircraft.

Markets sort assets to whoever values them most.

In a second-hand market for a safety-critical asset, "values most"
means "can extract the most revenue at the lowest cost of capital."

Now ask what determines an operator's cost of keeping the promise.

It is not the aircraft. The engineering requirement is identical
wherever the aircraft flies. A crack propagates at the same rate in
Lagos as in Frankfurt.

What differs is the *expected cost of not keeping it*.

In a jurisdiction with a functioning regulator, an aggressive tort
system, actuarially serious insurers, and a deep resale market that
punishes bad records, the expected cost of deferred maintenance is
high. Operators therefore bid for the aircraft on the assumption
that they will maintain it fully.

In a jurisdiction where oversight is weak, courts are slow, insurers
do not inspect, and records are unverifiable, the expected cost of
deferral approaches zero. Operators can therefore bid for the same
aircraft on the assumption that they will maintain it partially —
which means they can bid *more* for it, or accept lower revenues,
and still profit.

So the weak jurisdiction wins the auction.

Not through corruption. Through arithmetic.

*Risk flows downhill, toward whoever is least able to price it.*

The old fleet did not drift to Nigeria.

It was sorted there.

## THE NUMBERS: The graveyard

Nigerian aviation's corporate cemetery is large enough to support
statistics, and the statistics reorganize several of this book's
narratives.

Count the carriers licensed from the deregulation wave onward: Okada
Air, Kabo, Triax, Gas Air, Concord, Oriental, ADC, Bellview,
Sosoliso, EAS, Chanchangi, Albarka, Space World, Afrijet, Slok, Air
Midwest, First Nation, Discovery, Medview, Virgin Nigeria and its
successors — and onward through the 2010s.

Several dozen scheduled or substantial charter operators.

The survivors into the mid-2020s can be counted on one hand.

The median operating certificate's working life ran well under a
decade.

Now hold that number against the maintenance horizon of the
airframes those certificates flew.

A heavy structural check cycle runs on multi-year intervals. Fatigue
analysis operates on horizons of a decade and more. Corrosion
control programs assume continuous custody across the aircraft's
entire life.

The median firm's expected survival was shorter than the median
airframe's inspection cycle.

Which produces the chapter's central finding, and it is not a moral
one:

*The safety crisis and the corporate mortality crisis were the same
crisis, recorded in two different ledgers.*

Firms that cannot survive commercially cannot invest in five-year
airworthiness horizons — not because their owners are wicked, but
because the investment lies beyond the entity's expected lifespan
and therefore outside its decision frame entirely.

A company expecting to die in three years is not *unwilling* to fear
a metal problem due in seven.

It is structurally incapable of it.

## The causes of death, tabulated

Walk the cemetery rows and the causes cluster.

**Undercapitalization at birth.** Licenses issued against paid-up
capital that would not have bought a spare engine, let alone funded
a heavy check.

**The currency scissors.** Naira revenue, dollar costs, and a
central bank allocation queue between them. Chapter 3 laid out the
mechanism; every carrier in the graveyard died with a foreign
exchange story attached.

**Fleet economics.** The geriatric trap: cheap to enter, ruinous to
sustain, with maintenance costs rising as an aircraft ages precisely
when its revenue-generating reliability falls.

**Founder dependence.** The airline as personal estate, dying with
its patron's fortunes, attention, or life.

And, repeatedly, **the post-crash spiral** — in which a single hull
loss destroyed a brand, its bookings, and its insurability inside a
quarter. Bellview and Sosoliso never meaningfully outlived 2005. ADC
survived 1996 only to be finished by 2006. EAS was gone within the
year of Kano.

That last cause deserves a comment, because it looks like market
discipline and is not quite.

The market did punish killing passengers. But it punished
exclusively *after the fact* — an enforcement mechanism structurally
identical to the funerals-then-reform politics this book has
indicted throughout.

Both systems learned only in arrears.

## THE ECONOMICS: What the deferral actually buys

Return to the decision at the center of it all, and price it out
properly, because "deferred maintenance" is an abstraction until you
see the competing claims on a single scarce dollar.

A heavy check: seven figures in dollars, plus weeks of lost revenue
on an aircraft that may represent a third of the fleet.

Competing for the same dollars in the same month:

- The **lease payment**, whose default means repossession — the
  aircraft is gone, and with it the company
- The **insurance premium**, whose lapse means immediate grounding
- The **fuel bill**, without which nothing moves today
- The **payroll**, without which the engineers leave for the Gulf

The check can be deferred within the letter of the regulations.
Legitimate deferral mechanisms exist in every aviation system on
earth, because they must.

The lease payment cannot be deferred at all.

So every incentive in the structure, quarter after quarter, points
the scarce dollar away from the hangar.

And here is what makes it lethal rather than merely regrettable: the
consequence of deferral is *invisible and delayed*, while the
consequence of default is *visible and immediate*.

Metal fatigue does not send a demand letter.

The corners were not cut by monsters.

They were cut by spreadsheets, rationally, one defensible cell at a
time, inside firms whose bankruptcy was arriving faster than their
airframes' fatigue.

## THE NUMBERS: What a check is buying

It is worth understanding what the money purchases, because the
physical reality is what makes the arithmetic unforgiving.

An airliner's maintenance program is a pyramid.

At the base: daily and transit checks. Walk-arounds, fluid levels,
tires and brakes. Skipped by nobody, because their absence is
visible by lunchtime.

Above them: the lettered checks, escalating in depth and interval —
overnight inspections every few weeks, then progressively deeper
work.

At the top: the heavy checks, in which the aircraft is withdrawn
from service for weeks, opened to its structure, and inspected for
the cracks and corrosion that fatigue mathematics predicts and only
instruments confirm.

The heavy checks are where airworthiness actually lives.

And the intervals are not administrative conveniences. They are set
by damage-tolerance analysis: engineers calculate how long a crack
takes to grow from *detectable* to *critical*, and set the
inspection interval at a fraction of that time, so that the crack
will be found on at least two inspections before it matters.

Defer the check, and you are not skipping a formality.

You are spending the safety margin that the engineers reserved for
error — flying into the interval built to absorb the inspection that
missed something, on the bet that this particular airframe is
average.

Old aircraft raise the stakes twice over, because their inspection
programs *thicken* with age. Supplemental structural inspections.
Corrosion control. The aging-aircraft directives written after a
Boeing 737's cabin roof peeled open at altitude over Hawaii in 1988,
killing a flight attendant and exposing an entire generation of
assumptions about metal fatigue as inadequate.

So the requirement rises exactly as the owner's capacity to meet it
falls.

## The bazaar

Between the airline and the airframe stands the spares economy, and
the Nigerian version of it deserves attention, because it is where
this chapter's abstractions became physical objects moving through
customs.

A transport aircraft is several hundred thousand parts, each with a
paper twin: a release certificate attesting the component's
provenance, its overhaul status, and its remaining life.

Economically, the certificate *is* the part.

An engine bearing with clean paper is worth many multiples of the
identical bearing without it, and the integrity of the entire global
system rests on that paper chain surviving every sale, every shop
visit, and every border crossing.

Now run that system through the Nigeria of the crisis decades.

Dollar-starved carriers needing parts yesterday. A customs regime
that could hold an aircraft-on-ground shipment for weeks — converting
legality itself into schedule risk. And a brokered gray market —
Miami, Dubai, Lagos — perfectly willing to supply tagged components
whose documentation had been laundered somewhere along the chain
from salvage yards and part-outs of uncertain history.

The rational airline met the spares decision in exactly the form the
tire decision reached Nationair's managers at Jeddah.

The compliant route: slow and ruinously priced.

The gray route: fast, cheap, and papered well enough to pass the
inspection depth the era's regulator could bring.

Bogus and suspect parts became a global scandal in the 1990s — the
American regulator built an entire enforcement apparatus around them
— and no serious student of the period believes the Nigerian fleet's
exposure was below the world average.

Where a suspect component actually failed, nobody can say.

That is the point of laundered paper.

It forecloses the question forever, including in the accident
dockets.

## THE ECONOMICS: The insurance that did not inspect

Insurance is supposed to make all of this unnecessary. Understanding
why it did not is one of the most important pieces of analysis in
this book, because it explains why market discipline failed in a
market that appeared, on paper, to have it.

In the strong systems, underwriters function as **private
regulators**, and they are often more feared than the public ones.

Their surveyors walk hangars uninvited. Their exclusions ground
aircraft that no government inspector has seen. Their premiums
convert maintenance records directly into cash flow consequences,
which means an operator's safety investment shows up in its
quarterly accounts rather than only in its accident record.

The mechanism works because the insurer has skin in the game: it
pays the claim.

Now examine the Nigerian market of the crisis era, and watch the
mechanism invert.

Local-content requirements routed policies through domestic
underwriters, who retained a small fraction of the risk and reinsured
the overwhelming bulk abroad.

The retained sliver was too thin to fund real surveillance. So
surveillance never came.

And the reinsurers — remote, aggregating, holding the actual risk —
priced the *country* rather than the carrier. A flat Nigeria
premium.

Follow the consequence.

An operator with exemplary maintenance paid very nearly the same
rate as the operator running on brokered parts and stretched
intervals.

Which means the insurance market was **taxing virtue** — imposing a
cost on diligence that diligence could not recover, and subsidizing
recklessness with the difference.

Exactly backward from the design.

And claims performance completed the inversion. The Gwammaja ledger
of Chapter 7 showed what the fortress of process delivered to the
people it was supposed to compensate, and every slow claim taught
operators that the policy was a certification document rather than a
contingent asset.

Insurance that neither surveils nor promptly pays is not risk
transfer.

It is a stamp tax on the operating certificate.

For two decades, that is substantially what Nigerian aviation
bought.

## THE ARGUMENT: Why deregulation is not a policy

America deregulated its airlines in 1978. Entry multiplied. Fare
wars erupted. Marginal operators proliferated. The safety
establishment predicted carnage, and the press ran the fly-by-night
airline story for a decade.

The carnage did not come. American accident rates continued their
long decline straight through.

Nigeria deregulated in 1988. Entry multiplied. Marginal operators
proliferated.

The carnage came.

Two countries. The same policy. Opposite outcomes.

The variable was not the policy. It was the **surrounding
institutional field** — and it is worth listing that field
explicitly, because the omission of any single strand is survivable
and the omission of all of them is not:

- A regulator whose inspectors cannot be reassigned by telephone
- An independent investigator publishing findings within months
- An insurance market with actuarial teeth and uninvited surveyors
- A tort system that prices negligence in figures that reach the
  boardroom
- Lessors and financiers whose covenants enforce maintenance
  programs as loan conditions
- A used-aircraft market deep and well-documented enough that
  skimped maintenance destroys resale value — making promise-keeping
  self-enforcing through asset prices

That last one deserves emphasis, because it is the most elegant
mechanism in the entire system and it operates without any
government at all.

In a market with trustworthy records, an aircraft's maintenance
history is capitalized into its price. Neglect your fleet and you
destroy your own balance sheet. The discipline is automatic,
continuous, and requires no inspector.

It requires only that records be trustworthy.

Which is why the archives of Chapter 14 are not a clerical matter.
They are the foundation on which market discipline stands or does
not.

So state the general principle, because it applies to every
liberalization in every sector in every developing economy:

*Deregulation is not a policy. It is a policy plus a field. Removing
a constraint transfers risk to whichever institution is left holding
it. If no institution is left holding it, the risk goes to whoever
cannot refuse it.*

In Nigerian aviation, that was the passenger.

And at Kano in 2002, it was people who were not even passengers.

## The other biography

Balance requires the opposite story, because Nigeria now contains
it.

A new-generation Boeing 737 arrives in Lagos in the 2020s by a route
that touches nothing in the first biography.

It is leased new from an Irish lessor's order book, against a
business plan the lessor's credit committee has stress-tested.

Its lease covenants specify the maintenance program, the records
standard, the return conditions, and — the decisive innovation —
**maintenance reserves**: monthly payments into escrowed accounts,
held by the lessor, that can only be drawn to fund the checks
themselves.

The promise-keeping, prepaid.

Its insurers price the operator's audit trail rather than the
country.

And its arrival requires of the state exactly the inputs Chapter 16
will list: treaty-grade repossession rights, so the credit committee
will approve the jurisdiction at all; a registry whose records the
market trusts; a regulator whose surveillance the covenants can
cite.

The aircraft is safer than its 1968 ancestor not mainly because
engineering advanced — though it did, enormously — but because every
party in its economic chain is structurally invested in the promise
being kept, continuously, with money already set aside.

Nigerian carriers have walked that route. The market's anchor
carrier was the global launch customer for a new Western aircraft
type in 2019 — a first for the country, and a fact that would have
been unimaginable in 1995.

The narrowness of the route is the point.

Each rung on it is an institution.

## The new risk, growing inside the success

And there is a risk growing inside that success, which honesty
requires this chapter to price before it closes.

The consolidation the reform forced — capital floors,
recertification, the purge — raised the survival expectancy of the
median operator above the maintenance horizon of the median
airframe. That was the intended and beneficial effect.

It also produced a market in which a handful of carriers constitute
the domestic schedule.

Which means grounding one is no longer an administrative act. It is
a national event: routes going dark, states stranded, business
lobbies mobilized within a day, thousands of jobs deployed as
argument.

The carrier knows this. Its bankers know it.

And the knowledge is itself a balance-sheet asset — an implicit
regulatory forbearance that lowers, in exactly the too-big-to-fail
mechanism familiar from banking, the firm's effective cost of
running close to the line.

The 2010s and 2020s supplied the test cases: carriers under state
asset-management care whose systemic weight visibly complicated
every enforcement conversation, and suspension episodes in which
national-schedule arithmetic audibly shared the table with
airworthiness findings.

Banking's remedies translate almost line for line.

**Resolution planning** — the aviation equivalent of a living will:
a pre-agreed protocol for keeping the schedule alive through a
carrier's grounding, so that the safety decision is never hostage to
the continuity decision.

**Surveillance scaled to systemic weight** — the largest carriers
earning the *most* inspection, precisely because their failure is
least affordable.

And **structural encouragement of market depth**, so that no single
operating certificate is ever the difference between a country
flying and not.

None of that apparatus exists in Nigerian aviation law at this
writing.

It is the reform's next generation of risk, growing quietly inside
its greatest success — which is exactly how every previous era in
this book incubated its own.

## THE ARGUMENT: The false choice

Safety, this chapter has argued, is purchased upstream — in exchange
rate queues, treaty compliance, lease covenants, insurance
surveillance, and bankruptcy horizons — long before it is delivered,
or not, in a hangar at two in the morning.

The corollary dissolves the laziest dichotomy in the development
conversation: the supposed trade-off between safety and
affordability.

Run the Nigerian numbers honestly.

The era of cheap corner-cutting delivered an aviation sector that
was simultaneously the world's most dangerous *and* among its most
expensive per completed journey.

Add up what the passenger of 1995 actually paid: the ticket, plus
the chaos surcharge of a system without schedule integrity, plus the
insurance and country-risk loadings buried in every fare, plus the
risk premium itself — which in that decade was not a metaphor but a
measurable probability of death, borne in full by the traveler and
compensated at approximately nothing.

The reformed era's younger fleets and audited carriers coincided
with the greatest expansion of Nigerian air travel in the country's
history.

Kept promises compound.

Broken ones compound too — at rates no treasury ever publishes, in a
currency Part II counted.

What a society owes the people its systems fail is the next
chapter's subject, in its starkest form.

The minutes after the borrowing comes due, when the state's only
remaining duty is to arrive.
