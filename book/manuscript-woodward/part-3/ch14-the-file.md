# 14. The File

## Lagos, the present

She keeps it in a plastic envelope inside a larger envelope, and she
has carried it to more offices than she can now list in order.

A boarding pass stub. A laminated photograph. A death certificate
whose cause-of-death line reads, in a clerk's handwriting, *air
crash*.

And correspondence. Three decades of it — with an airline that no
longer exists, a ministry that has been renamed twice, and an
investigation bureau that has been renamed once.

All of it asking one question.

Why.

She is a composite, assembled from the documented histories of the
bereaved of Ejigbo, the lagoon, Lisa, and Kano. But the envelope is
not a literary device. Nigerian journalists who cover aviation know
that envelope. Several of them have photographed it.

Her file is the Nigerian aviation archive in miniature: grief fully
documented, causation forever pending.

What she was owed, under a treaty her country signed before she was
born, was a report.

## THE ARGUMENT: The honesty machine

Begin with how the system is supposed to work, because its design is
one of civilization's quiet masterpieces and almost nobody outside
the industry knows it exists.

Annex 13 to the Chicago Convention obliges every state to
investigate the accidents on its territory.

And it defines the purpose of that investigation in terms the treaty
takes care to make *exclusive*:

Prevention. And explicitly — the words are in the text — "not to
apportion blame or liability."

From that single sentence unfolds the entire apparatus this book has
invoked chapter by chapter.

**Independent investigation bodies**, walled off from the regulator
(which may itself be implicated) and from prosecutors (whose
presence silences witnesses).

**Protected safety information** — cockpit voice recordings, crew
statements — shielded from routine legal use, so that the system can
afford to be told the truth.

**Public findings and recommendations**, addressed to the world
rather than to a filing cabinet.

And layered above the accident machinery, the **voluntary reporting
regimes**, through which pilots, controllers, and engineers confess
hazards and near-misses by the hundred thousand, unpunished, so that
the system can see its failures *before* they reach the wreckage
stage.

The scholars of the field call the underlying bargain **just
culture**: the deal in which the frontline professional trades
candor for protection from blame, and honesty becomes the system's
sensory organ.

Every element of that design is counterintuitive to the punitive
instinct.

And every element is empirically vindicated.

The industries and countries that punish error into hiding fly
blind. The ones that pay for truth with immunity see everything, and
their accident curves fall for decades.

Which yields the proposition at the center of this chapter:

*Aviation safety is not primarily a technology. It is an
epistemology with a budget.*

A set of institutions for making failure speakable — fast, in
public, and protected.

And it follows that the state of a country's accident reports is not
a bureaucratic detail.

It is an X-ray of the entire safety system.

## THE NUMBERS: The Nigerian ledger

Compile it, and the record sorts into three strata.

**The unwritten and the buried.**

The colonial habit — investigation as internal correspondence —
passed seamlessly to the successor state. The 1973 Kano disaster
produced no public Nigerian account. The 1969 loss of the national
carrier's flagship produced none. And the military line, from Ejigbo
in 1992 through the twin crashes of 2021, has never published at
all.

For roughly the first four decades of independence, the default
disposition of a Nigerian accident investigation was the file, not
the press.

**The late.**

The investigation bureau era brought real investigations — and a
tempo that defeated their purpose. The report on ADC 086 took years.
Bellview's inconclusive findings took most of a decade. Dana 992's
final report took five years, for an accident whose essential
mechanics were understood within months.

Recall what a report is *for*: grounding fleets, rewriting
procedures, informing the identical aircraft still flying tonight.

Then the arithmetic of delay becomes a safety deficit in its own
right.

A five-year report protects nobody for five years.

**The reformed and partial present.**

The current bureau shows the curve bending. Reports appear, faster,
in public, on a website that works.

What has bent less is everything *around* the reports:
recommendations issued but never publicly tracked to closure; no
national voluntary reporting scheme with credible protections at
scale; and a legal environment in which the just-culture bargain has
never been struck by statute — leaving every frontline confession
theoretically discoverable, and therefore, in practice, unspoken.

## THE NUMBERS: What silence costs, itemized

Because "transparency" tends to float free as a virtue word, pin the
costs of its absence to cases already in evidence.

**The operational cost is measured in repetitions.**

Sosoliso in December 2005 and ADC 053 in October 2006 were, at the
level of decision architecture, the *same accident*: a crew
continuing into convective weather that forecasts and eyes had
flagged, under schedule and prestige pressure.

Ten months separated them.

In a system with a live reporting culture and rapid interim
publication, December's lesson — its dispatch-pressure findings, its
go-around arithmetic — would have been circulating in every crew
room, regulator bulletin, and simulator syllabus in the country by
the following rainy season.

The interval was filled instead with mourning and process.

And the lesson arrived, ten months later, in a funeral procession to
Sokoto.

**The commercial cost is priced into everything.**

Insurance loading: the flat country premium of Chapter 12 is, in
substantial part, an *opacity premium*. Underwriters charge for what
they cannot read. Lease terms. Bilateral negotiations, where audit
and investigative performance weigh directly.

And passenger demand itself — which after every unexplained event
swung toward road transport, whose fatality rate per kilometer
exceeds aviation's by orders of magnitude.

That last effect deserves its own sentence, because it inverts the
intuition entirely: opacity, by displacing travelers onto the
highways, kills statistically even in years when the fleet flies
clean.

**And the civic cost compounds longest.**

Where official causation is absent or late, explanation is supplied
by the market: sabotage theories, spiritual attribution, elite
conspiracy.

None of this is Nigerian credulity. It is rational inference under
informational famine. Where the state explains nothing, the
explanation market clears at whatever price rumor sets.

Each unexplained disaster deposits another stratum of the reflexive
distrust that Nigerian institutions now meet everywhere — including
on the occasions when they are telling the truth.

The woman with the file does not believe the government's account of
anything.

Why would she?

On the one subject she knows better than any official alive, it
never gave her one.

## THE ARGUMENT: The blame reflex

Why is silence so stable?

The economical answer — secrecy protects the guilty — is true and
insufficient, because it does not explain why silence persists after
the guilty have retired, died, or been replaced by people with no
stake in the concealment.

The deeper mechanism is that the alternative to the honesty machine
is not neutrality.

It is an older, more intuitive system: **blame**.

In the blame system, an accident is a crime scene. The organizing
question is not *what failed* but *who pays*. Information is
ammunition, so every actor — pilot, controller, airline, ministry —
rationally armors up and says nothing. And the inquiry's real
function is the allocation of punishment and the protection of
hierarchy.

Most of human history has run on the blame system. Most institutions
revert to it under stress. It is the default setting of the human
response to catastrophe, and it feels like justice.

And a state built on patronage runs on it *structurally* — because
in a patronage system, responsibility is the currency, and its
assignment is far too valuable to delegate to engineers.

Read that way, Nigeria's silences were not lapses of administration.

They were the blame system defending itself. Every unpublished
report was a favor owed, a faction shielded, a hierarchy preserved.

The tension is universal, and it is worth noting that even strong
systems fight it continuously. France's investigators have collided
for decades with its examining magistrates over exactly this line —
the safety inquiry seeking candor, the criminal inquiry seeking
culpability, both with a claim on the same wreckage.

The difference is that the French collision happens in public,
between strong institutions, and the safety findings emerge
regardless.

Where the blame system meets no institutional counterparty, it
simply wins.

And the file stays closed.

The American public docket — hearings on camera, raw evidence posted
online, findings barred by statute from use as proof of liability —
is best understood as the blame reflex's engineered defeat. It is
built on a wager: that a democracy can be trusted to watch its
failures being understood.

The wager has paid for eight decades.

And it is the cheapest reassurance technology ever invented, because
the state that explains publicly does not have to ask to be
believed.

## THE ECONOMICS: The whistle-blower's arithmetic

Between the frontline's protected report and the public's published
record stands a third figure this chapter has not yet given his due.

The insider who sees the systemic rot — not an error but a practice,
not a lapse but a policy — and must decide what speaking costs.

Run his arithmetic as the Nigerian system of the crisis decades set
it.

**Speak inside the organization**: the hazard report filed into the
void — at best ignored, at worst traced. The controllers' pre-1996
radar memoranda are the documented case, and their authors watched
them accomplish nothing for a decade.

**Speak to the regulator**: an agency that might be the operator's
patron, in a system where the paper trail led back to its author.

**Speak to the press**: effective — the era's journalism was the
functioning accountability channel — and personally catastrophic, in
an industry with three employers and one social network.

**Speak to no one**: free.

The arithmetic solved itself.

And the system's information starvation was the sum of ten thousand
such rational solutions, each one individually reasonable, arriving
collectively at blindness.

The mature systems rebuilt the equation deliberately: statutory
whistle-blower protection with real remedies; regulator hotlines
whose confidentiality survives subpoena; and — the deepest fix — a
voluntary reporting culture that makes the dramatic whistle-blow
largely unnecessary, because the undramatic report is answered.

Nigeria's whistle-blower framework, adopted with fanfare for
financial crimes, has never been seriously extended to
safety-critical industry.

The aviation professional of the 2020s still runs a milder version
of the old equation.

And every silence this book has priced was purchased at that
exchange rate — which is a statute away from repeal.

## The statute on one page

The just-culture law this chapter keeps invoking can be sketched in
its essentials, because the models are public and the drafting is a
month's work for a competent chamber.

**Section one** defines protected safety information: voluntary
hazard reports, flight-data monitoring records, cockpit voice
recordings, and statements given to safety investigators.

**Section two** erects the shield: protected information is
inadmissible in criminal, civil, disciplinary, and administrative
proceedings against the person who provided it.

With the carve-outs that make the bargain honest, and that every
serious version of this statute contains: willful misconduct, gross
negligence strictly defined, sabotage, and intoxication remain fully
prosecutable — on independently gathered evidence.

**Section three** obliges the institutions: every operator above a
size threshold must run a confidential reporting system meeting
defined standards, and the regulator must publish annual
de-identified analyses so the system's yield is visible.

**Section four** governs the interface with justice: a standing
protocol between the safety investigator and the prosecution
service, sequencing access to wreckage and records so that neither
inquiry contaminates the other.

**Section five** protects the protectors: retaliation against a
reporter becomes itself an offense.

Nothing on that page prevents Nigeria from prosecuting the genuinely
criminal.

Everything on it prevents the state from taxing candor at rates that
return the system to silence.

What the page requires is a formal admission — that the state would
rather know than punish.

Which is the entire philosophy of modern aviation safety compressed
into a single clause, and perhaps the reason it has waited so long
in Abuja.

It is the clause by which a state declares what kind of state it
intends to be.

## THE ARGUMENT: The archive problem

Beneath the unpublished report lies a deeper stratum, and a book
built substantially on documents owes the reader an account of what
the Nigerian aviation archive actually is.

It is, first, dispersed to the point of fiction.

The registries of the directorate era passed through ministerial
reorganizations, office moves, and agency splits that treated
records as furniture. Veterans describe archives lost to floods, to
termites, to the simple entropy of unfunded storage — and, at
sensitive junctures, to convenience.

The Nigeria Airways liquidation scattered the airline's technical
and corporate memory among receivers, warehouses, and skips.

The colonial strata survive better in London than in Lagos. Chapter
1 made the observation and it bears repeating here as a finding
rather than an irony: it is easier today to reconstruct the aviation
administration of 1935 than that of 1985.

There is no national aviation archive. There is no systematic oral
history program capturing the generation — now in its seventies and
eighties — that holds the only surviving record of entire decades.

And the consequence runs well beyond historians' inconvenience.

An institution's capacity to learn is bounded by its capacity to
remember. The memory infrastructure — registries, archives,
databases, librarians — is the learning system's substrate.

The regulator that re-certified the industry in 2006 had first to
*reconstruct*, aircraft by aircraft, records that should have been a
query away.

Every reform cycle in this history spent its first year rebuilding a
factual picture its predecessor had already built and lost.

Amnesia is expensive on a schedule. The state has paid for its
aviation memory several times and has owned it never.

And there is a second consequence, which Chapter 12 identified and
which deserves restating here because it connects the archive
directly to the market.

Trustworthy records are what allow an aircraft's maintenance history
to be capitalized into its resale price — which is the mechanism by
which markets discipline maintenance without any inspector at all.

Destroy the records and you destroy that mechanism.

The archive is not clerical.

It is load-bearing.

## The other publishers

Where the state declined to publish, others published, and the
culture of silence is incompletely described without its
adversaries.

The Nigerian press has been, across this book's entire span, the de
facto national accident archive.

From the reporters working Ejigbo's canal bank against a military
censor. Through the aviation desks of the 2000s, whose cumulative
coverage built the public record the investigation bureau was not
building. To the digital-era outlets whose freedom-of-information
suits and leaked-report stories forced documents into daylight after
the 2011 Act gave the tradition a legal instrument.

The professional associations published in their fashion —
communiqués and strike threats saying in public what filed reports
had said in vain.

And the diaspora published, from beyond the reach of domestic
consequences.

This counter-archive achieved real things. The political rupture of
2005 ran on it.

But its existence also defines the pathology's exact shape.

A society can know its disasters thoroughly — Nigerians never lacked
information about their crashes, in the newspaper sense — while the
*institutional* circuit stays broken.

The circuit is: finding, recommendation, verified change.

Journalism generates salience. It does not generate closure. It can
force the question; it cannot sign off the corrective action. It can
report that the fire tenders were empty; it cannot make the pumps be
tested next March.

The press is the smoke alarm of the system.

Nigeria's has been loud, brave, and — measured against what smoke
alarms can do — effective.

The building needed sprinklers.

## After

Of all the patterns in Part III, this one costs least to break and
resists longest.

Because it is not a budget line. It is a reflex, and reflexes are
governance's deepest layer.

Yet the record offers its usual Nigerian consolation: the reflex has
been broken before, in this very country, in this very industry.

The 2006 settlement that separated the investigator from the
regulator, and the 2022 statute that deepened it, were exactly such
breaks — the blame system conceding territory to the honesty
machine, under funerary compulsion, one law at a time.

What remains is the last mile.

The just-culture shield that makes candor safe. The recommendation
register that makes findings binding in fact rather than in
rhetoric. The publication tempo that lets truth arrive while it can
still protect someone.

None of it requires a naira the system does not already have.

It requires only what the woman with the envelope has been
requesting for thirty years.

That the state address its citizens the way an adult addresses
adults.

With an account of what happened.

## The anatomy, complete

Part III set out to convert seven disasters into four patterns, and
the conversion is now done.

A **regulator** whose strength turned out to be architectural rather
than personal — and therefore both buildable and reversible.

An **economics** that sorts risk toward whoever is least able to
price it — and that can be re-sorted, upstream, in capital markets
and treaty compliance and insurance surveillance.

A **response deficit** that is shallow, cheap, and separable from
every deeper reform — the single highest-yield purchase available to
the Nigerian state.

And a **silence** that costs nothing to end but pride.

Four patterns.

None of them fate.

What a country might actually do with that knowledge — and what
several other countries have already done with it — is Part IV.
