# 14. The File

## Lagos, the present

She keeps it in a plastic envelope inside a larger envelope, and she
has carried it to more offices than she can now list in order. A
boarding pass stub. A laminated photograph. A death certificate
whose cause-of-death line reads, in a clerk's handwriting, air
crash. And correspondence, three decades of it, with an airline that
no longer exists, a ministry that has been renamed twice, and an
investigation bureau that has been renamed once. All of it asking
one question. Why.

She is a composite, assembled from the documented histories of the
bereaved of Ejigbo, the lagoon, Lisa, and Kano. But the envelope is
not a literary device. Nigerian journalists who cover aviation know
that envelope and several of them have photographed it. Her file is
the Nigerian aviation archive in miniature, in which grief is fully
documented and causation is forever pending. What she was owed,
under a treaty her country signed before she was born, was a report.

## The honesty machine

Begin with how the system is supposed to work, because its design is
one of civilization's quiet masterpieces and almost nobody outside
the industry knows it exists.

Annex 13 to the Chicago Convention obliges every state to
investigate the accidents on its territory, and it defines the
purpose of that investigation in terms the treaty takes care to make
exclusive. The purpose is prevention, and explicitly, in words that
appear in the text, not to apportion blame or liability.

From that single sentence unfolds the entire apparatus this book has
invoked chapter by chapter. There are independent investigation
bodies, walled off from the regulator, which may itself be
implicated, and from prosecutors, whose presence silences witnesses.
There is protected safety information, meaning cockpit voice
recordings and crew statements, shielded from routine legal use so
that the system can afford to be told the truth. There are public
findings and recommendations, addressed to the world rather than to
a filing cabinet. And layered above the accident machinery are the
voluntary reporting regimes, through which pilots, controllers, and
engineers confess hazards and near-misses by the hundred thousand,
unpunished, so that the system can see its failures before they
reach the wreckage stage. The American voluntary system alone
receives on the order of a hundred thousand reports a year, which is
a volume of self-incrimination no punitive system could ever
extract.

The scholars of the field call the underlying bargain just culture,
meaning the deal in which the frontline professional trades candor
for protection from blame and honesty becomes the system's sensory
organ. Every element of that design is counterintuitive to the
punitive instinct, and every element is empirically vindicated. The
industries and countries that punish error into hiding fly blind,
and the ones that pay for truth with immunity see everything and
watch their accident curves fall for decades.

Which yields the proposition at the center of this chapter. Aviation
safety is not primarily a technology. It is an epistemology with a
budget, which is to say a set of institutions for making failure
speakable, fast, in public, and protected. And it follows that the
state of a country's accident reports is not a bureaucratic detail.
It is an X-ray of the entire safety system.

## The Nigerian ledger

Compile it, and the record sorts into three strata.

The first is the unwritten and the buried. The colonial habit of
investigation as internal correspondence passed seamlessly to the
successor state. The 1973 Kano disaster produced no public Nigerian
account. The 1969 loss of the national carrier's flagship produced
none. And the military line, from Ejigbo in 1992 through the twin
crashes of 2021, has never published at all. For roughly the first
four decades of independence, the default disposition of a Nigerian
accident investigation was the file rather than the press.

The second is the late. The investigation bureau era brought real
investigations and a tempo that defeated their purpose. The report
on ADC 086 took years. Bellview's inconclusive findings took most of
a decade. Dana 992's final report took five years, for an accident
whose essential mechanics were understood within months. Recall what
a report is for, which is grounding fleets, rewriting procedures,
and informing the identical aircraft still flying tonight. Then the
arithmetic of delay becomes a safety deficit in its own right,
because a five-year report protects nobody for five years.

The third is the reformed and partial present. The current bureau
shows the curve bending, with reports appearing faster, in public,
on a website that works. What has bent less is everything around the
reports, since recommendations are issued but never publicly tracked
to closure, there is no national voluntary reporting scheme with
credible protections at scale, and the legal environment has never
struck the just-culture bargain by statute, which leaves every
frontline confession theoretically discoverable and therefore, in
practice, unspoken.

## What silence costs, itemized

Because transparency tends to float free as a virtue word, pin the
costs of its absence to cases already in evidence.

The operational cost is measured in repetitions. Sosoliso in
December 2005 and ADC 053 in October 2006 were, at the level of
decision architecture, the same accident, involving a crew
continuing into convective weather that forecasts and eyes had
flagged, under schedule and prestige pressure. Ten months separated
them. In a system with a live reporting culture and rapid interim
publication, December's lesson, with its dispatch-pressure findings
and its go-around arithmetic, would have been circulating in every
crew room, regulator bulletin, and simulator syllabus in the country
by the following rainy season. The interval was filled instead with
mourning and process, and the lesson arrived ten months later in a
funeral procession to Sokoto.

The commercial cost is priced into everything. The flat country
insurance premium of Chapter 12 is, in substantial part, an opacity
premium, because underwriters charge for what they cannot read.
Lease terms carry it. Bilateral negotiations carry it, since audit
and investigative performance weigh directly there. And passenger
demand itself carries it, because after every unexplained event
demand swung toward road transport, whose fatality rate per
kilometer exceeds aviation's by orders of magnitude. That last
effect deserves its own sentence, because it inverts the intuition
entirely. Opacity, by displacing travelers onto the highways, kills
statistically even in years when the fleet flies clean.

And the civic cost compounds longest. Where official causation is
absent or late, explanation is supplied by the market, in sabotage
theories, spiritual attribution, and elite conspiracy. None of this
is Nigerian credulity. It is rational inference under informational
famine, because where the state explains nothing, the explanation
market clears at whatever price rumor sets. Each unexplained
disaster deposits another stratum of the reflexive distrust that
Nigerian institutions now meet everywhere, including on the
occasions when they are telling the truth. The woman with the file
does not believe the government's account of anything, and why would
she, when on the one subject she knows better than any official
alive it never gave her one.

## The blame reflex

Why is silence so stable? The economical answer, that secrecy
protects the guilty, is true and insufficient, because it does not
explain why silence persists after the guilty have retired, died, or
been replaced by people with no stake in the concealment.

The deeper mechanism is that the alternative to the honesty machine
is not neutrality. It is an older and more intuitive system, which
is blame. In the blame system an accident is a crime scene, and the
organizing question is not what failed but who pays. Information
becomes ammunition, so every actor, whether pilot, controller,
airline, or ministry, rationally armors up and says nothing, and the
inquiry's real function becomes the allocation of punishment and the
protection of hierarchy.

Most of human history has run on the blame system. Most institutions
revert to it under stress. It is the default setting of the human
response to catastrophe and it feels like justice. And a state built
on patronage runs on it structurally, because in a patronage system
responsibility is the currency and its assignment is far too
valuable to delegate to engineers. Read that way, Nigeria's silences
were not lapses of administration. They were the blame system
defending itself, and every unpublished report was a favor owed, a
faction shielded, and a hierarchy preserved.

The tension is universal, and it is worth noting that even strong
systems fight it continuously. France's investigators have collided
for decades with its examining magistrates over exactly this line,
with the safety inquiry seeking candor and the criminal inquiry
seeking culpability, both holding a claim on the same wreckage. The
difference is that the French collision happens in public, between
strong institutions, and the safety findings emerge regardless.
Where the blame system meets no institutional counterparty, it
simply wins, and the file stays closed.

The American public docket, with hearings on camera, raw evidence
posted online, and findings barred by statute from use as proof of
liability, is best understood as the blame reflex's engineered
defeat. It is built on a wager, which is that a democracy can be
trusted to watch its failures being understood. The wager has paid
for eight decades, and it is the cheapest reassurance technology
ever invented, because the state that explains publicly does not
have to ask to be believed.

## The whistle-blower's arithmetic

Between the frontline's protected report and the public's published
record stands a third figure this chapter has not yet given his due,
who is the insider who sees the systemic rot, meaning not an error
but a practice and not a lapse but a policy, and who must decide
what speaking costs.

Run his arithmetic as the Nigerian system of the crisis decades set
it. If he speaks inside the organization, the hazard report is filed
into the void, at best ignored and at worst traced, and the
controllers' pre-1996 radar memoranda are the documented case, whose
authors watched them accomplish nothing for a decade. If he speaks
to the regulator, he is addressing an agency that might be the
operator's patron, in a system where the paper trail led back to its
author. If he speaks to the press, he is effective, since the era's
journalism was the functioning accountability channel, and
personally catastrophic, in an industry with three employers and one
social network. If he speaks to no one, it is free.

The arithmetic solved itself, and the system's information
starvation was the sum of ten thousand such rational solutions, each
one individually reasonable, arriving collectively at blindness.

The mature systems rebuilt the equation deliberately, with statutory
whistle-blower protection carrying real remedies, regulator hotlines
whose confidentiality survives subpoena, and, in the deepest fix, a
voluntary reporting culture that makes the dramatic whistle-blow
largely unnecessary because the undramatic report is answered.
Nigeria's whistle-blower framework, adopted with fanfare for
financial crimes, has never been seriously extended to
safety-critical industry, and the aviation professional of the 2020s
still runs a milder version of the old equation. Every silence this
book has priced was purchased at that exchange rate, which is a
statute away from repeal.

## The statute on one page

The just-culture law this chapter keeps invoking can be sketched in
its essentials, because the models are public and the drafting is a
month's work for a competent chamber.

The first section defines protected safety information as voluntary
hazard reports, flight-data monitoring records, cockpit voice
recordings, and statements given to safety investigators.

The second erects the shield, making protected information
inadmissible in criminal, civil, disciplinary, and administrative
proceedings against the person who provided it, with the carve-outs
that make the bargain honest and that every serious version of this
statute contains, so that willful misconduct, gross negligence
strictly defined, sabotage, and intoxication remain fully
prosecutable on independently gathered evidence.

The third obliges the institutions, requiring every operator above a
size threshold to run a confidential reporting system meeting
defined standards and requiring the regulator to publish annual
de-identified analyses so the system's yield is visible.

The fourth governs the interface with justice through a standing
protocol between the safety investigator and the prosecution
service, sequencing access to wreckage and records so that neither
inquiry contaminates the other.

The fifth protects the protectors, making retaliation against a
reporter an offense in itself.

Nothing on that page prevents Nigeria from prosecuting the genuinely
criminal. Everything on it prevents the state from taxing candor at
rates that return the system to silence. What the page requires is a
formal admission, which is that the state would rather know than
punish. That is the entire philosophy of modern aviation safety
compressed into a single clause, and perhaps the reason it has
waited so long in Abuja, because it is the clause by which a state
declares what kind of state it intends to be.

## The archive problem

Beneath the unpublished report lies a deeper stratum, and a book
built substantially on documents owes the reader an account of what
the Nigerian aviation archive actually is. It is, first, dispersed
to the point of fiction.

The registries of the directorate era passed through ministerial
reorganizations, office moves, and agency splits that treated
records as furniture. Veterans describe archives lost to floods, to
termites, to the simple entropy of unfunded storage, and, at
sensitive junctures, to convenience. The Nigeria Airways liquidation
scattered the airline's technical and corporate memory among
receivers, warehouses, and skips. The colonial strata survive better
in London than in Lagos, and Chapter 1 made the observation, which
bears repeating here as a finding rather than an irony. It is easier
today to reconstruct the aviation administration of 1935 than that
of 1985. There is no national aviation archive, and there is no
systematic oral history program capturing the generation, now in its
seventies and eighties, that holds the only surviving record of
entire decades.

The consequence runs well beyond historians' inconvenience. An
institution's capacity to learn is bounded by its capacity to
remember, and the memory infrastructure of registries, archives,
databases, and librarians is the learning system's substrate. The
regulator that re-certified the industry in 2006 had first to
reconstruct, aircraft by aircraft, records that should have been a
query away. Every reform cycle in this history spent its first year
rebuilding a factual picture its predecessor had already built and
lost. Amnesia is expensive on a schedule, and the state has paid for
its aviation memory several times and has owned it never.

There is a second consequence, which Chapter 12 identified and which
deserves restating here because it connects the archive directly to
the market. Trustworthy records are what allow an aircraft's
maintenance history to be capitalized into its resale price, which
is the mechanism by which markets discipline maintenance without any
inspector at all. Destroy the records and you destroy that
mechanism. The archive is not clerical. It is load-bearing.

## The other publishers

Where the state declined to publish, others published, and the
culture of silence is incompletely described without its
adversaries. The Nigerian press has been, across this book's entire
span, the de facto national accident archive.

It runs from the reporters working Ejigbo's canal bank against a
military censor, through the aviation desks of the 2000s whose
cumulative coverage built the public record the investigation bureau
was not building, to the digital-era outlets whose
freedom-of-information suits and leaked-report stories forced
documents into daylight after the 2011 Act gave the tradition a
legal instrument. The professional associations published in their
fashion, issuing communiqués and strike threats that said in public
what filed reports had said in vain. And the diaspora published,
from beyond the reach of domestic consequences.

This counter-archive achieved real things, and the political rupture
of 2005 ran on it. But its existence also defines the pathology's
exact shape. A society can know its disasters thoroughly, and
Nigerians never lacked information about their crashes in the
newspaper sense, while the institutional circuit stays broken. The
circuit runs from finding to recommendation to verified change.
Journalism generates salience and it does not generate closure. It
can force the question and it cannot sign off the corrective action.
It can report that the fire tenders were empty and it cannot make
the pumps be tested next March. The press is the smoke alarm of the
system, and Nigeria's has been loud, brave, and, measured against
what smoke alarms can do, effective. The building needed sprinklers.

## After

Of all the patterns in Part III, this one costs least to break and
resists longest, because it is not a budget line. It is a reflex,
and reflexes are governance's deepest layer.

Yet the record offers its usual Nigerian consolation, which is that
the reflex has been broken before, in this very country, in this
very industry. The 2006 settlement that separated the investigator
from the regulator, and the 2022 statute that deepened it, were
exactly such breaks, with the blame system conceding territory to
the honesty machine, under funerary compulsion, one law at a time.

What remains is the last mile. The just-culture shield that makes
candor safe. The recommendation register that makes findings binding
in fact rather than in rhetoric. The publication tempo that lets
truth arrive while it can still protect someone. None of it requires
a naira the system does not already have. It requires only what the
woman with the envelope has been requesting for thirty years, which
is that the state address its citizens the way an adult addresses
adults, with an account of what happened.

## The anatomy, complete

Part III set out to convert seven disasters into four patterns, and
the conversion is now done.

There is a regulator whose strength turned out to be architectural
rather than personal, and therefore both buildable and reversible.
There is an economics that sorts risk toward whoever is least able
to price it, and that can be re-sorted upstream in capital markets,
treaty compliance, and insurance surveillance. There is a response
deficit that is shallow, cheap, and separable from every deeper
reform, making it the single highest-yield purchase available to the
Nigerian state. And there is a silence that costs nothing to end but
pride.

Four patterns, and none of them fate. What a country might actually
do with that knowledge, and what several other countries have
already done with it, is Part IV.
