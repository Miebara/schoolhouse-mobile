# 6. The Picture

## Lagos Area Control, sometime in the mid-1990s

Musa — the composite retired controller introduced in Chapter 3 —
describes the room the way a man describes a house he lived in for
twenty years.

Paper strips in metal holders, one per aircraft, arranged on a
board. A clock. A pencil. Radios that faded in rain.

And a radar display that did not work.

"People think we were guessing," he said. "We were not guessing. We
were calculating."

He drew it out on a table, using a phone and a cup and his hands.

An aircraft reports overhead a beacon at a time. It reports its
level. It gives an estimate for the next fix. The controller writes
the estimate on the strip. If two aircraft will be over the same
point within the separation minimum, one of them is moved — a level,
a delay, a hold.

"You are building a picture," he said. "Every airplane in your sky,
you are holding where it is and where it will be. In your head. For
your whole shift."

He tapped the table.

"The picture is only as true as what they tell you."

## THE NUMBERS: The physics of not seeing

Procedural control — separation by position report, timing, and
altitude, without radar — is a legitimate discipline. The North
Atlantic was run on it for decades.

Its arithmetic is unforgiving.

Radar separation in a terminal area is typically measured in units
of three to five nautical miles. Procedural separation, because it
must absorb every error in reporting and timing, is measured in
*minutes* — ten, fifteen, sometimes more, which at jet speeds
translates into fifty, eighty, a hundred nautical miles.

That is the trade. A radar controller can safely handle a large
number of aircraft in a small volume. A procedural controller can
handle a small number in a large volume.

Now hold that against what was happening in Nigerian airspace.

Deregulation had multiplied movements several-fold in a decade. New
carriers, new frequencies, new city pairs. The demand curve rose
steeply through the early 1990s.

The supply curve — the airspace's carrying capacity, which is a
function of surveillance, communications, staffing, and procedures —
was flat. It had been flat since the radar failed.

Two curves diverging, in a system where the gap between them is
absorbed by human beings holding a picture in their heads.

There is a point at which the arithmetic stops working. Nobody
publishes that point. It is discovered.

## The warnings

The controllers' association had been filing memoranda for years.

Radar unserviceable. Radios degraded. Staffing below establishment.
Overtime chronic.

The documents were filed with bureaucratic correctness, through
channels, to the responsible authorities.

"We were not ignored," Musa said. "It was more insulting than that.
We were acknowledged."

Acknowledged?

"Received. Noted. Under consideration." He made a small gesture with
his fingers, filing something away. "Every year."

Understand what a controller actually produces, because it clarifies
where responsibility for that room properly lay.

A controller produces exactly one thing: separation.

And he produces it entirely from inputs that other people fund:
surveillance, communications, navigation aids the aircraft can
trust, airspace design, and enough colleagues that vigilance does
not degrade across a shift.

Strip those inputs away and the controller does not become lazy.

He becomes a virtuoso of the workaround.

Which is what the Lagos room had been for years by the evening of
November 7, 1996.

## Approaching Lagos, November 7, 1996, last light

ADC Airlines Flight 086 was inbound from Port Harcourt with 144
people aboard.

ADC was one of the good ones. A serious company from the first wave
of post-deregulation carriers: publicly listed, run by airline
professionals, its crews largely alumni of Nigeria Airways and
Zaria, its Boeing 727s old — everyone's were — but maintained with
more conviction than the era's average.

If you were a Lagos businessman in 1996 choosing an airline on
reputation, there was a fair chance you chose ADC.

Ngozi — a composite drawn from the documented histories of the ADC
families — had seen her husband off at Port Harcourt that afternoon.
The choice of carrier was itself a form of care. It was the safer
airline. That was why he was on it.

Descending toward Lagos, the crew of 086 received traffic
information: another scheduled jet, climbing out of Lagos on a
reciprocal track, in conflict with their descent.

The controllers, working without radar, had built their instructions
on reported positions.

The geometry those reports described was wrong.

And the error surfaced in the worst form available to aviation:
visually, at a closure speed approaching a thousand miles an hour,
in the haze of a West African evening — traffic where no traffic was
supposed to be.

## Fifty-nine seconds

What happened next took less than a minute, and it is the crux of
the accident.

The captain, responding to what appeared to be an imminent
collision, threw the 727 into an evasive maneuver of extreme
aggression — a steep, rolling descent that within seconds carried
the aircraft beyond its structural and aerodynamic envelope.

Night was falling. The horizon over the lagoon is a rumor at dusk.

In the turn, the crew lost the sky.

The aircraft entered a spiral dive, transonic in its final seconds,
and struck shallow water near Ejirin with an energy that fragmented
it totally.

All 144 aboard died instantly.

The opposing aircraft landed without incident, its crew initially
unaware how close the evening had come to a mid-air collision over
Africa's largest city.

## THE ARGUMENT: Why the maneuver killed

"Pilot error" is where the lazy account of this accident stops, and
where an honest one begins.

Human spatial orientation is built for walking. It integrates three
instruments — the balance organs of the inner ear, vision, and the
pressure senses of the body — and flight systematically deceives all
three.

The semicircular canals of the inner ear detect only *changes* in
rotation. Hold a smooth, sustained bank and within seconds the
sensation of turning fades entirely; the body reports level flight.
Execute a rapid roll and pull, and the system is flooded with
sensations that outrun any human's capacity to integrate them.

Now remove the visual horizon — dusk, haze, black water below,
indistinguishable from sky.

At that point the balance system does not report uncertainty. It
does not say *I don't know*.

It reports wrong answers with total confidence.

The classic outcome has a name that predates the jet age: the
graveyard spiral. A descending turn feels level. The instinctive
pull toward "level" tightens the spiral. Airspeed builds toward
structural failure while every sensation in the pilot's body swears
the wings are straight.

Recovery requires the single act that aviation trains hardest and
human beings do worst: disbelieving your own inner ear in favor of
instruments — within seconds, under terror, at night.

The upset-recovery training that today drills airline crews on
exactly this scenario became a global standard only in the following
two decades, after loss of control in flight was recognized as
commercial aviation's largest killer.

And the aircraft itself belonged to an earlier world. The Boeing 727
was engineered to the philosophy of the early 1960s: three crew,
cable-and-pulley flight controls, and none of the protective layers
that later generations made universal. No collision avoidance system
to paint the conflicting traffic that the controllers' paper picture
had lost. No enhanced ground proximity warning. Flight instruments a
generation before the displays that make an unusual attitude legible
at a glance.

Each absence was an era, not a defect. The 727 was certified decades
before those technologies existed. The wealthy fleets retrofitted or
retired against them. The sorted-downward fleets flew them as
standard equipment.

So assign the weight of this accident correctly.

The crew of Flight 086 met the hardest known trap in human
performance, without the training the industry had not yet invented,
in an aircraft a generation behind the protections, inside an
airspace failure their state had not repaired.

Three layers. None of them is carried by the two men who died
disbelieving their instruments.

## The fishermen

The lagoon villages were first, as the neighbors always are in this
book.

There was nothing for them to do.

The debris field defied the instincts of rescue. No fuselage. No
seats. No recognizable machine — an aircraft reduced to fragments
across water and swamp.

Recovery of remains ran for days and was never complete.
Identification was partial. The burial of the unidentified was
collective.

Ngozi's file — the composite of what the ADC families actually
carried — begins with a radio bulletin that could not confirm her
particular grief, and a manifest reconciled to reality days late.

And then it continues into something the era's law had never
contemplated.

## THE ECONOMICS: The paperwork of a suddenly absent person

With remains fragmentary and unrecoverable, the families of Flight
086 collided with a problem that has nothing to do with aviation and
everything to do with the machinery of a state.

How is a person certified dead when there is no body?

Everything downstream requires that certificate. Probate. Insurance.
Access to a joint bank account. Guardianship of children.
Remarriage. The transfer of a lease. The continuation of a
business.

Nigerian law in 1996 offered presumption-of-death procedures
designed, in their origins, for sailors lost at sea in the
nineteenth century — slow, evidentiary, and utterly unsuited to a
mass-casualty event with a known cause and a known passenger list.

Estates froze for years. Widows lost houses they legally owned
because they could not prove they were widows. Businesses that had
depended on a single signature simply stopped.

The crash generated, over time, a body of case law and
administrative practice — accelerated certification pathways after
registered disasters — that later tragedies would inherit.

Note where the learning happened. Not in the radar budget. In
probate practice.

The state's institutional learning, when it occurred at all in this
period, occurred in the least visible registers.

And note the general principle, because it belongs in every
disaster-response doctrine and is almost never there:

*The administrative aftermath of a disaster is itself a response
system.* It is testable in advance. It is cheap to fix. And it is
encountered by every bereaved family at the exact moment of their
least capacity to fight it.

## The report that blamed the commons

The Accident Investigation Bureau's report on Flight 086 — completed
years after the accident, in the unhurried tradition Chapter 14 will
quantify — is, on its technical merits, a competent document. Its
account of causation has held up.

It distributed responsibility across the system with unusual candor
for its era: the loss of separation, built on erroneous position
reporting and the fragility of procedural control; the crew's
evasive maneuver, correct in impulse and fatal in execution, flown
without the situational picture that radar or a collision-avoidance
system would have supplied; and behind both, stated plainly enough
that no reader could miss it, the infrastructural dereliction that
had left one of the continent's busiest terminal areas running on
stopwatch and trust for years.

Read today, the report's most striking feature is how little of it
is about ADC Airlines.

The aircraft was airworthy. The crew was qualified. The dispatch was
orderly.

Flight 086 was killed by the commons.

## THE ARGUMENT: The third role of the state

This deserves its own frame, because it identifies a category of
state failure distinct from the two the previous chapters described.

A state has three roles in aviation, and they fail in different
ways.

**As regulator**, it sets and enforces standards on private parties.
Chapter 4 showed that role vacant at Jeddah.

**As self-regulator**, it disciplines its own operations. Chapter 5
showed that role eaten from within at Ejigbo.

**As operator of the commons**, it supplies the shared goods that no
individual firm can buy for itself: the airspace, the surveillance,
the navigation aids, the meteorological service, the published
procedures.

Flight 086 is the failure of the third role, and it is the least
escapable of the three.

A private airline can, at cost, exceed its regulator. It can buy
better aircraft than the rules require, train beyond the minimum,
hire the best crews available.

No airline can exceed the airspace it flies in.

The economics here are elementary and worth stating because they are
so frequently ignored in the Nigerian aviation debate: air traffic
infrastructure is a textbook public good. It is non-rival — one
airline's use of the radar picture does not diminish another's — and
non-excludable — you cannot sell separation to one aircraft in a
sector and withhold it from the next.

Public goods are systematically underprovided by markets. That is
not an ideological claim; it is the definitional property that makes
them public goods.

Which means the *only* possible provider is the state, and the
consequence of the state's failure falls not on the party that
failed but on whoever happens to be in the sky.

There is a particular cruelty in dying of the commons. Many of the
passengers on Flight 086 had made careful private choices — the good
airline, the experienced crew, the respectable fare — and every
private choice was overridden by a public failure that no passenger
could see, price, or avoid.

## THE ARGUMENT: The secession

That cruelty explains a social pattern that shadows the rest of this
book, and it is one of the most important political mechanisms in
the entire story.

Confronted with a failing public system, those who can afford to
exit, exit.

In Nigerian aviation, the exit had a specific form: the political
and business elite moved progressively into charter flights, private
jets, and — for shorter journeys — road convoys with escorts. The
airborne equivalent of the private generator and the household
borehole.

Each individual decision was rational. Collectively they were
catastrophic, because of what exit does to voice.

The economist Albert Hirschman formalized this half a century ago:
in any declining organization, the members most sensitive to quality
are the first to leave — and they are precisely the members whose
complaints would have been most effective in arresting the decline.

Exit drains the constituency for repair.

Apply it here. Every decision-maker who stopped flying commercially
took with him a fraction of the political demand for fixing
commercial aviation. By the early 2000s, the people with the power
to fund the radar mostly did not fly in the traffic it would have
watched.

The radar was commissioned in 2010.

It was commissioned four years after an aircraft carrying the Sultan
of Sokoto fell out of the sky — which is to say, four years after
the exit stopped working.

Hold that sequence. It is the mechanism of this entire book.

## THE NUMBERS: Fourteen years

The radar's own history is a core sample of the Nigerian state,
drilled through three regimes.

After the 1996 crash, the Abacha government announced
modernization. A contract materialized in the era's characteristic
opacity, and dissolved with the regime in 1998.

The restored democracy re-scoped the project. Years passed in the
committee purgatory that Nigerian capital projects inhabit — budget
cycles missed, counterpart funding unreleased, contractors changed,
specifications relitigated.

The eventual program — total radar coverage of Nigeria — was
contracted in the mid-2000s and driven to completion inside the
protected weather of the post-2006 reform.

In 2010, the screens lit.

Some of the controllers who switched them on were the same men and
women who had worked the paper picture on the evening of November 7,
1996.

Veterans describe the commissioning without joy.

"It showed what was always there," Musa said. "That is all it did.
It showed us what had always been there."

Three observations convert the anecdote into evidence.

**The technology was never the constraint.** Radar of the required
class was a catalogue purchase in 1996, as it had been in 1986. The
fourteen-year gap was purely institutional — the time required for
the Nigerian procurement metabolism, unassisted by crisis, to
convert an acknowledged lethal deficiency into installed equipment.

**The project completed only when it found shelter.** Protected
budget lines, a leadership measured on it, external auditors
watching. Infrastructure advances in Nigeria when, and only when,
someone powerful is scored on its completion.

**The dead of 086 are inside that radar** the way the dead of Jeddah
are inside the world's tire-pressure monitors.

The improvements of a safety system are cemeteries, reorganized as
capability.

## THE NUMBERS: The darkest month

Five days after Flight 086 went into the lagoon, on November 12,
1996, a Saudi Boeing 747 departing Delhi and a Kazakh freighter
inbound collided at altitude over Charkhi Dadri, India, killing 349
— the deadliest mid-air collision in history.

The same autumn had already seen a Peruvian airliner fly into the
Pacific with its static ports taped over, and a hijacked Ethiopian
767 ditch off the Comoros in front of tourists' cameras.

November 1996 remains, in the trade's memory, the nadir of the
decade.

The cluster was not coincidence. Charkhi Dadri came out of exactly
the seam this chapter has been mining: procedural airspace under
explosive traffic growth, a single congested corridor, aircraft
without collision-avoidance systems, controllers stacking opposing
streams on one airway.

It was the arithmetic of Lagos, at Indian volume.

And the responses diverged in a way that produces the single most
useful metric in this book.

India, humiliated before the world, mandated collision-avoidance
systems and accelerated its airspace modernization on a timetable
measured in years.

The United States had faced the same seam in June 1956, when two
airliners collided over the Grand Canyon killing 128. Within two
years it had passed the Federal Aviation Act, created an independent
agency with unified authority over the airspace, and funded a
continental radar program that followed with dispatch.

Crash to law to agency to infrastructure: roughly four years.

Nigeria's sequence ran: crash in 1996, law in 2006, infrastructure
in 2010.

Fourteen years, with two further mass-casualty disasters inside the
gap.

Here is the metric. Rank the world's aviation systems by a single
number — *years elapsed from demonstrated lethal deficiency to
installed remedy* — and they sort into very nearly the exact order
their accident records show.

That number is not a measure of wealth. India in 1996 was poorer
per capita than Nigeria. America in 1956 was rich, but the
constraint on its response was legislative attention, not money.

It is a measure of institutional drivetrain: an investigator that
converts wreckage into findings in months, a legislature that owns
the findings, a press that prices delay in political careers, and a
budget process capable of multi-year commitment.

Each of those links existed weakly or not at all in the Nigeria of
1996.

## The airline that tried

ADC's own arc completes the accounting, and it frames the era's
central injustice — which is also its central incentive problem.

The crash destroyed the meaning of the brand. Bookings collapsed.
Insurance re-rated. The airline spiraled through retrenchment toward
suspension, and clawed back years later as a diminished thing.

Its end belongs to Chapter 9. ADC Flight 053, Abuja, October 2006 —
the good airline of 1996, by then struggling under every pressure
this Part has catalogued, making the departure decision that killed
a Sultan.

The company did not survive the second wound.

Read the two ADC disasters as a pair and the lesson is uncomfortable
for anyone who believes markets discipline safety.

The system's costs were assessed on whoever the lightning found,
almost without reference to desert. The 1996 crash punished ADC for
the state's radar. Meanwhile the era's genuine cowboy operators
exited with their capital intact when their certificates died of
paperwork rather than catastrophe.

A market that cannot distinguish its good actors from its bad ones
cannot reward diligence.

And an industry that cannot reward diligence will, generation by
generation, attract less of it.

The serious operators understood this precisely. It is why, after
2006, the good airlines became the regulator's loudest constituency
for *publishing* audit results.

Markets, like publics, can only reward what they can see.

## After

The lagoon gave back what it gave back and kept the rest.

The scene now moves north, to Kano — where the price of the era's
other great deferral, the geriatric fleet, was about to be paid not
by passengers at all.

But by a neighborhood that had never bought a ticket.
