# 9. The Sultan

## Abuja, Sunday, October 29, 2006, morning

You could see it from the terminal windows. A mature squall line was
working across the capital, the wall of thunderstorms that West
African pilots learn to respect in their first season and that the
region's meteorology delivers on schedule at the turning of the
seasons. Other operations at the field that morning treated it
accordingly and waited.

ADC Airlines Flight 053 boarded for Sokoto. Among the passengers was
Muhammadu Maccido, the Sultan of Sokoto, Sarkin Musulmi, commander
of the faithful, spiritual authority for tens of millions of
Nigerian Muslims, occupant of a throne older than Nigeria itself,
and by common consent among the most beloved holders of that office
in living memory. His son was aboard. A senator was aboard. Others
of the Sokoto establishment were aboard, going home after the
Ramadan festivities.

The crew had the weather information and it was unambiguous. Flight
053 took off into it. Within roughly a minute, at low altitude, in
the heart of the squall's shear, the Boeing 737 lost the fight for
climb, came down in scrub and farmland minutes from the runway, and
burned. Ninety-six of the 105 people aboard died. Nine survived,
seated by the chance geometry of the breakup.

## The invisible wall

The West African squall line is not a storm in the temperate sense.
It is a moving wall. Organized lines of thunderstorms, sometimes
hundreds of kilometers long, build along the seasonal contest
between the dry northeasterly air of the Sahara and the moist
monsoon flow off the Atlantic, and sweep east to west across the
country with the reliability of a timetable and the violence of a
front line.

Inside and beneath them live the phenomena that aviation meteorology
ranks among its most lethal. The downburst is a column of
rain-cooled air collapsing from the storm's core and splashing
outward along the ground, and its concentrated form, the microburst,
is the specific killer. A mature microburst can produce a total
airspeed change of sixty or eighty knots across a span of a mile or
two, and it can do it in under a minute.

Here is why it is so effective a trap, and the sequence deserves to
be understood, because it is the same sequence that killed Sosoliso
Flight 1145 ten months earlier. An aircraft penetrating a microburst
at low altitude meets first the outflow headwind, so airspeed
increases, performance improves, and the aircraft climbs or floats
above the intended path. Every instrument tells the crew that things
are going well, and the trap is baited. Seconds later the aircraft
passes into the core downdraft, a column of air descending at speeds
that can exceed an aircraft's best climb rate, and then out the far
side into the tailwind shear, where the wind that was opposing the
aircraft is now pushing it. Airspeed collapses and lift collapses
with it, and because lift varies with the square of airspeed the
loss is not linear but quadratic, so a thirty percent loss of
airspeed removes roughly half the available lift. The engines cannot
restore the energy in time, because turbine engines spool up over
several seconds and the encounter is measured in seconds.

An aircraft at cruise altitude has room to trade height for speed.
An aircraft at four hundred feet on departure has nothing to trade.
The escape maneuver, which is maximum thrust, a precise pitch
attitude, and acceptance of the stall warning, is trained and it
works, within an envelope that is narrow.

## The answer was already written, in someone else's blood

The permanent answer to windshear is not heroic piloting. It is
avoidance, and the world had learned this in a documented, dated,
public way twenty-one years before Flight 053.

On August 2, 1985, Delta Air Lines Flight 191 flew into a microburst
on approach to Dallas–Fort Worth and crashed, killing 137 people.
The American response is the model this book keeps holding up,
because of what it produced and how fast. The investigation
established the mechanism definitively, and then the system built
the countermeasures. Terminal Doppler weather radar was installed at
major airports, being ground radar specifically designed to detect
the wind field of a microburst before an aircraft enters it.
Low-level windshear alert systems were fielded, consisting of
networks of anemometers around an airfield that compare wind vectors
and alarm automatically when the pattern indicates shear. Predictive
windshear radar went aboard aircraft. Training standardized the
escape maneuver and drilled it in every simulator. And, most
importantly, a dispatch culture hardened that treats an active
convective cell over the field as a closed door rather than a
judgment call.

By 2006 that apparatus had been mature for two decades in the
systems that built it. Abuja that morning had the forecast, and the
eyes of its pilots. It had none of the alerting infrastructure.

This is the sentence that should be read twice. The atmosphere of
the Gulf of Guinea is not more hostile than the atmosphere over
Texas. It is identically hostile, on a more regular schedule, to a
system that had priced its warnings at zero.

## The findings

The investigation established that the crew had received the
aerodrome weather, including thunderstorm activity, gusting winds,
and the squall essentially overhead, and had elected to depart. The
takeoff was normal until, seconds into the climb, the aircraft
entered the shear. The 737, heavy and low and slow, sank beyond
arrest, struck the ground substantially wings-level but
unrecoverable, and broke up.

The aircraft was airworthy and the engines were delivering power.
The windshear was detectable and forecast. The operative causes were
the decision to depart into known conditions and the handling of the
encounter, with contributing factors reaching into dispatch
practice, crew pairing and training, and the absence at the field of
the alerting technology that would have converted a forecast into a
prohibition.

Behind the findings sat the economics. ADC had returned from
suspension into a market where every canceled rotation bled cash it
did not have. The passenger list carried the kind of eminence that
makes delay socially expensive. And the industry's decision culture,
which was the waiver culture of Chapter 3 now privatized, still
priced a storm's risk against a schedule's certainty in the old way.

Flight 053 was, in its mechanics, the most conventional accident in
this book. No mystery, no missing recorder, no exotic failure mode.
A trained crew flew a working aircraft into weather everyone could
see, under pressures everyone understood, past a regulatory system
that had not yet grown the teeth to forbid it. Its very
conventionality is what made it, politically, the last straw. There
was nothing left to blame but the system itself.

## What the funeral did

The death of a Sultan of Sokoto is an event in Nigerian public life
without close parallel. The throne is the deepest institution in the
north, and its occupant's authority is religious rather than
constitutional, which is to say that it does not depend on any
government and cannot be conferred by one. The funeral drew the
entire apparatus of the Nigerian state to Sokoto within a day.

And it did something subtler, which the politicians present
understood immediately. For a decade the Nigerian elite had answered
the aviation system's decay by exiting it, into charters, private
jets, and road convoys with escorts, and Chapter 6 described the
mechanism and its consequence, which is that exit drains the
constituency for repair. The Sultan's death announced that the exit
had a limit. The system's reach included kings. Every powerful man
at that funeral had boarded a Flight 053 of his own within the year,
and knew it.

## The succession that worked

There is one more institutional scene from that week, and it belongs
in this book because it delivers a masterclass in the subject at the
victim's own funeral.

The throne fell vacant without warning, at the center of the
religious peace of half the federation. The Sokoto establishment's
succession machinery, meaning the kingmakers, the consultation, and
the state's formal instruments, produced within days and without
public crisis the selection of a new Sultan, a serving army officer
who was then abroad. The transition drew national comment precisely
because the surrounding month was a portrait of systems failing.

Institutions that expect to exist in a century maintain succession
machinery, documented custom, and reserves of legitimacy against
exactly the unscheduled catastrophe. Institutions that live
appointment to appointment meet the same catastrophe as an
existential surprise. The oldest continuously functioning
institution in Nigeria demonstrated the book's central proposition
during the funeral of a man killed by its absence elsewhere. Depth
was never a foreign import. It was available locally, on display,
that very week.

## Aso Rock, November 2006

President Olusegun Obasanjo was an engineer by training, in the
final year of his tenure, and already carrying the wounds of 2005.
What happened next compressed into months what previous disasters
had failed to produce in decades.

An official involved in that period described the atmosphere as a
kind of clearing. "For about a year," he said, "everybody stopped
defending themselves. Because there was nothing left to defend."

The reform had four components. Each is worth understanding
separately, because each is exportable, and because, as Chapter 10
records, each has eroded at a different rate since.

## Component one, the law

The Civil Aviation Act 2006, already in passage after the 2005
disasters, was completed and signed within weeks of the crash. Its
core provision answered the oldest defect in this book. The Nigerian
Civil Aviation Authority became legally autonomous, its
Director-General was protected by tenure provisions, and, in the
decisive clause, its funding was drawn from statutory levies on
tickets and cargo rather than from ministerial allocation. The Act
also established the Accident Investigation Bureau as a distinct
body, separating for the first time in Nigerian history the
investigation of accidents from the regulation of the industry that
has them.

That levy provision looks like accounting. It is the most important
sentence in the modern history of Nigerian aviation, and the reason
is a straightforward principal-agent argument. An agency funded by
annual allocation is disciplined by whoever controls the allocation,
and the discipline does not need to be explicit. Nobody has to
threaten anybody. The agency simply learns, over a few budget
cycles, which kinds of diligence are followed by adequate funding
and which are followed by austerity attributed to general fiscal
pressure. The starvation is always deniable, and that is its
efficiency.

An agency funded by formula can be disciplined only by changing the
law, which requires a public act, in a legislature, on a record,
with journalists present. Capture's entire comparative advantage is
that it operates in the dark. A funding formula does not eliminate
capture. It forces it into daylight, where its price rises by an
order of magnitude.

The same logic explains the tenure clause. An office occupiable only
at pleasure will be occupied by someone whose planning horizon is
the pleasure of the appointing power, while an office with protected
tenure can be occupied by someone planning beyond the next
reshuffle, which crucially makes it worth the reputational
investment of a serious professional who has alternatives. Which
brings us to the man they hired.

## Component two, the engineer

Harold Demuren arrived at the head of the NCAA with a résumé
constructed almost provocatively outside the system he was to
reform. A doctorate in aeronautical engineering from the
Massachusetts Institute of Technology, by way of rigorous Soviet-era
aviation academies. Decades in airworthiness and operations. And
standing in the international technical community, in the rooms in
Montreal where Nigeria's reputation was at that point a liability to
be managed.

He was, by the accounts of colleagues and adversaries alike across
the era's public record, difficult in precisely the dimension the
job required, being a man for whom a regulation was a technical fact
rather than an opening position. The stories from the
re-certification years share a common structure, and people who
lived through them tell them with a certain relish. The owner
arrives with the customary escort of influence, meaning the
senator's call, the governor's letter, and the directive from above,
and he leaves with a checklist. The same checklist. Everybody's
checklist.

His methods repay study because they were institutional rather than
heroic in design, and that distinction is the difference between a
reform that survives its author and one that does not. He recruited
from the diaspora, bringing home Nigerian inspectors holding
American and European credentials at real salaries funded by the new
levies, men and women who were unimpeachable in exactly the
technical conversations where the old regulator had been
condescended to. He imported witnesses, bringing ICAO and FAA
technical teams inside the building not as consultants delivering
reports but as observers whose presence changed behavior. He
published the operating certificate register, the suspensions, and
the directives, on the theory that a documented decision is a
defensible one and a reversible decision is one that was never
written down. And he aimed the entire apparatus at a single external
target.

## Component three, the American audit

The FAA's International Aviation Safety Assessment programme
deserves demystifying, because the phrase Category 1 carried
enormous symbolic weight in Nigeria and almost nobody outside the
industry knew what it measured. It does not audit airlines and it
does not audit outcomes. It audits the state, and the question is
narrow and brutal, which is whether this country's civil aviation
authority can actually perform the oversight it claims to perform.

The examiners work through ICAO's eight critical elements, which are
the legislative framework, the operating regulations, the
organization, the technical personnel and their qualifications, the
guidance material, the licensing and certification machinery, the
surveillance obligations, and the resolution of identified
deficiencies. And they do it by reading files, meaning inspector
qualification records, surveillance schedules measured against
completions, and enforcement actions measured against their
dispositions.

The genius of the instrument is that it cannot be performed for. A
minister can stage a terminal opening in a fortnight. Nobody can
stage five years of inspector training records over a weekend.

When the FAA certified Nigeria as Category 1 in August 2010, after
several assessment cycles, findings, and closures, the real content
of that certification was archival. The paper trail of a functioning
regulator existed, was internally consistent, and had been
maintained under scrutiny through two changes of government.

Here is why the target was chosen so shrewdly. Category 1 unlocked
direct flights to the United States by Nigerian carriers, with
everything that implied for the diaspora corridor, for cargo, and
for national prestige, and it repriced Nigerian risk for lessors and
insurers at the margin. Which meant that every subsequent act of
political interference in the regulator now carried a legible price
tag, denominated in something the political class valued. The reform
had, in effect, taken the country's aviation reputation hostage
against its own political class, and it is the single most
sophisticated institutional maneuver in this book.

## Component four, the purge

Through 2006 and 2007 the NCAA re-certified the entire industry from
zero. Every airline's operating certificate was re-examined against
the regulations as written, with the waiver culture suspended.

For a serious operator the process was brutal and clarifying in
equal measure. Every manual was reopened, covering operations,
maintenance, training, and security. Every aircraft's records were
audited back through its import history, an archaeology that some
airframes could not survive. Every pilot's licenses and recurrent
checks were re-verified. And the airline's financial fitness, the
item the old regime had never seriously examined, was modeled
against its schedule commitments.

Operators describe the period's signature experience with a phrase
that recurs almost verbatim across accounts, which is the inspector
who could not be made to go away. Not by delay, not by hospitality,
and not by the customary calls, because his checklist was now also
Montreal's and Washington's, and his Director-General answered the
calls with a checklist of his own.

Roughly half the operating fleet, and a swath of carriers, did not
survive the audit, and famous names among the fallen made the point
that fame was no longer collateral. The lobbying counterattack ran
through 2007, with legislators enlisted, injunctions sought, and the
press seeded with stories of jobs destroyed and monopolies looming,
and it broke against the same wall the inspectors had become. A
regulator whose decisions are documented, published, and
internationally witnessed cannot be quietly reversed, and reversing
it loudly means owning the next crash.

The economic critique of the re-certification was immediate and is
still made, which is that it reduced competition, raised fares, and
destroyed jobs. All three are true, and the analysis is incomplete
in a way that matters. Recall the graveyard. Of the dozens of
carriers licensed after deregulation, the overwhelming majority died
within a decade, and the median Nigerian air operator's certificate
had a working life shorter than a single heavy maintenance cycle.
Now recall the deferral calculus of Chapter 3. A firm expecting to
die in three years is structurally incapable of fearing a metal
problem due in seven, and its rational maintenance horizon is
shorter than its airframes' fatigue horizon. Which means that in an
industry whose product is a maintained promise, an entry regime that
admits firms with three-year survival expectations is not producing
competition. It is producing an unpriced transfer of risk from firms
to passengers, sold at a discount that looks like a cheap fare. The
barrier to entry, in this specific industry, is the product.

What the purge did was raise the survival expectancy of the median
operator above the maintenance horizon of the median airframe.
Consolidation raised load factors, it stabilized fares, and it made
the surviving carriers occasionally financeable for the first time
in the industry's history, which meant they could access lease
markets for younger aircraft, which meant the sorting machine of
Chapter 7 began, at the margin, to run in reverse. The purge was not
a trade of safety against commerce. Below a certain floor, they are
the same variable.

## The other October aircraft

Six weeks before Flight 053, in September 2006, a Nigerian Air Force
transport crashed in the hills of Benue State carrying a delegation
of senior army officers, and the dead included a concentration of
generals that made the loss, inside the military, an Ejigbo in
miniature. The event is nearly absent from civilian memory, having
been overwhelmed within weeks by the Sultan, and its absence is
exactly why it belongs here.

Consider the timing. The civil aviation system was, that autumn, in
the most intense reform ferment of its history, with the Act in
passage, the purge designed, and the regulator at maximum energy.
The military system, six weeks before that reform's crowning
catastrophe, suffered its own decapitating crash and processed it
through the unmodified 1992 protocol. Board of inquiry convened.
Condolences issued. Findings sealed. No provision of the new Act
reached it. No ICAO audit scored it. No Category 1 assessment would
ever ask about it.

The two Octobers of 2006 ran this book's controlled experiment
inside a single season, in a single country, under a single
government. One aviation system entered the strongest accountability
field ever constructed around it. The other remained in the zero
field it had always inhabited. The following fifteen years scored
the experiment. The civil accident curve bent to the floor. The
military curve, through the twin disasters of 2021, did not bend at
all. No finding in this book is better controlled.

## Holding through the handover

Reforms in presidential systems die most often at the succession,
and this one's survival of the 2007 transfer of power is the most
transferable political engineering in the story.

Obasanjo left office in May 2007, months into the purge, with the
reform's enemies waiting precisely for that exit. The grounded
operators' injunctions were live, the legislative sympathy was warm,
and the incoming administration owed nothing to its predecessor's
aviation legacy. Every precedent in this book predicted reversal.

It did not come, and the three reasons are the exportable part. The
reform had been legislated rather than decreed, so unwinding
statutory funding and tenure required public parliamentary action
with the graves of 2005 still fresh. The external witnesses were
mid-examination, because the ICAO audit cycle and the Category 1
campaign were underway and the new government inherited them as
national projects whose abandonment would have been internationally
legible failure. And the reform was staffed by people whose
reputations were their own capital, so they did not require the new
president's sponsorship and required only his protection, which is
far cheaper to grant. President Umaru Yar'Adua, a cautious
institutionalist by temperament, granted it, and Category 1 arrived
under his successor, Goodluck Jonathan.

But state the lesson precisely, because it contains the seed of
Chapter 10. Reforms survive successions on borrowed motives, meaning
legal friction, international examination, and reputational
hostages. Borrowed motives buy time. They do not buy permanence. And
time is what they buy for, which is the maturation of a domestic
constituency that makes the reform self-enforcing. The Nigerian
reform used its borrowed decade to build superb machinery and almost
no constituency.

## What actually changed

The skies answered, and the answer is the strongest evidence in this
book. The mass-casualty drumbeat that had structured Nigerian life
since 1991 stopped.

Before the reform, Nigeria averaged a mass-casualty airline disaster
roughly every two to three years, with the clustering that Part II
has documented across 1991, 1992, 1996, 2002, twice in 2005, and
2006, a run of seven catastrophes in fifteen years. After the reform
there was a single scheduled-airline catastrophe, in 2012, and then
nothing. As this book is written, Nigerian scheduled aviation has
passed a decade without a mass-casualty jet crash, which is the
longest such run in the country's history, achieved across a
doubling of traffic.

Correlation earns caution, and Chapter 10 stress-tests this
conclusion hard, because the plateau has real debits. But no honest
reading of the sequence, in which reform was followed by quiet in a
system whose every prior decade had supplied a disaster, escapes the
inference. The machine had been fixable all along.

## After

The Sultan's last flight lasted less than two minutes, and its
consequences have outlasted most of the institutions this book has
buried.

That is the paradox to carry out of Part II's darkest stretch.
Flight 053 was simultaneously the system's most damning failure,
being banal, foreseeable, and foretold that very morning by the
weather service the state had starved, and the proximate cause of
its redemption.

Nigerians are told, and tell themselves, that nothing changes. The
years 2006 to 2010 are the empirical answer. Everything changed,
measurably and quickly and by design, when, and only when, the cost
of the status quo landed on those with the power to alter it.

Aviation safety reform, on the evidence of this book and every
comparative case in it, is purchased with concentrated elite grief.
America's 1958 act was bought at the Grand Canyon. Indonesia's
reform was bought with a European ban. Nigeria's was bought with
fourteen months of funerals and a royal coffin as the gavel. The
standing question of Part IV is whether any state can learn to buy
the reform before the funeral, and the record says almost never.

What the reform had not yet faced was time, meaning the turnover of
its champions, the fading of its funerals, and the reversion
pressure of the system it had interrupted. Reforms are not events.
They are maintenance schedules.

The next chapter is a Sunday afternoon in June 2012, a neighborhood
in Lagos, and an airliner with both engines failing.
