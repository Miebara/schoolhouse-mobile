# PART II — THE CRASHES

# 4. The Tires

## Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, July 11, 1991, 08:29 local

They boarded in white.

Two hundred and forty-seven Nigerian pilgrims, most of them from
Sokoto and the country's far north, in the seamless cloth of ihram —
the garment of consecration, worn for the pilgrimage, and, in Muslim
burial practice, the color of the shroud.

Most were farmers and traders. For nearly all of them, the Hajj was
the journey of a lifetime, financed by decades of saving, often by
the sale of land or cattle. Village savings cohorts traveled
together. When they came home they would be addressed differently
for the rest of their lives.

The aircraft that would carry them wore three flags at once.

It was a Douglas DC-8, twenty-three years old, built in California.
It was owned and operated by Nationair, a charter company based in
Montreal. It was painted, for the season, in the green and white of
Nigeria Airways.

The arrangement is called a wet lease: an aircraft rented together
with its crew, its maintenance, and its insurance, so that the
machine flies under one company's brand while answering, in every
operational particular, to another's.

Nigeria Airways, by 1991 unable to mount the pilgrimage surge from
its own decaying fleet, had contracted the lift.

Nationair, whose aggressive growth had outrun its cash, needed the
revenue.

Two institutional desperations had met in a marketplace.

The flight was designated Nigeria Airways 2120. From pushback to
impact it would last twenty-nine minutes.

The evidence had begun accumulating days earlier.

## What was known before they boarded

The Saudi investigators would eventually fix the origin of the
disaster not in the sky but at a desk.

In the days before the accident, tire pressure checks on the DC-8's
main landing gear had found two tires significantly below limits.

The maintenance personnel flagged it. This is not in dispute. It is
in the report.

The nitrogen servicing equipment needed to correct it was
unserviceable.

Procuring proper servicing meant delay.

And here the file becomes a study in how commercial pressure travels
down through an organization until it arrives, finally, at a piece
of rubber.

## THE ECONOMICS: How a schedule becomes a physical force

Understand what a Hajj contract is, because its structure explains
everything that followed.

Pilgrimage lift is boom economics compressed into six weeks. An
operator commits aircraft, crews, and insurance to a base thousands
of miles from home. Revenue arrives per rotation flown. Fixed costs
run whether the aircraft moves or not.

So every day an aircraft sits unserviceable, three things happen at
once:

The costs continue. The revenue stops. And thousands of pilgrims
with visas keyed to the pilgrimage calendar back up in terminals on
two continents, where the Saudi authorities enforce the calendar
mercilessly, because the alternative is chaos at the holy places.

The client — here Nigeria Airways, and behind it the Nigerian
state's pilgrimage apparatus — measures its contractor in departures
achieved.

Now write out the incentive structure formally, because it is
almost diagrammatic in its perversity:

- Every actor in the chain is rewarded for departures
- No actor in the chain is rewarded for refusals
- The cost of a refusal is immediate, visible, and lands on the
  person who refuses
- The cost of *not* refusing is probabilistic, delayed, and lands
  on someone else

An economist would say the externality is uninternalized. A pilot
would put it differently: everybody who could say stop had a reason
not to, and the one thing that could have overruled all of them was
missing.

Aviation has a name for the force that is supposed to countervail
this. **Airworthiness.** The flat, unconditional rule that the
machine meets specification or it does not move.

Airworthiness is precisely the kind of rule — boring, expensive,
painful at exactly the wrong moment — that survives only inside
institutions built to enforce it against money.

On the Jeddah ramp in July 1991, no such institution was effectively
present.

Canada licensed the operator, but the operation ran nine time zones
away, under a contract Canada had no reason to examine.

Saudi Arabia owned the runway, but held over a foreign-registered
transient only the blunt instrument of a ramp inspection.

And Nigeria — which owned the passengers, the brand on the fin, and
the contract — had persuaded itself that the entire purpose of
chartering was to buy capacity and shed accountability in a single
transaction. It wrote no meaningful technical conditions into the
charter. It sent no technical representative. It stationed nobody
with the authority to ground an aircraft.

The rule met the money alone.

The rule lost.

## THE NUMBERS: Why a tire is not a minor component

Ask a hundred passengers to rank the components of an airliner by
how much they should worry about them, and the tires will finish
last.

This intuition is wrong, and the physics of why is worth
understanding, because the same physics recurs in Chapter 7 at Kano.

An aircraft tire at takeoff weight is carrying loads that have no
equivalent in ordinary experience. A fully loaded DC-8 is
distributing more than a hundred tons across its main gear. The
tires are inflated to pressures several times those of a car — the
pressure is what carries the load.

Underinflate that tire, and it does not simply risk going flat.

It *flexes* beyond its design envelope on every rotation. And
flexing rubber generates heat with extraordinary efficiency —
mechanical energy converting directly into thermal energy inside a
material that is an excellent insulator and therefore cannot shed
it.

On a long takeoff roll, at high weight, in July heat, on a runway in
Saudi Arabia, this becomes an exponential process. Heat softens
rubber. Softer rubber flexes more. More flexing generates more heat.

The failure mode is not a puncture. It is thermal runaway.

Two other numbers complete the picture.

An underinflated tire transfers its share of the load to its
neighbor on the same axle. So the failure is not independent — the
first tire's degradation actively causes the second's.

And the DC-8, a design of the late 1950s, had no fire detection or
extinguishing capability in its main wheel wells. None. The
certification standards of its era did not require it, because the
scenario had not yet been imagined vividly enough.

## 08:29 to 08:58

The aircraft taxied out near maximum takeoff weight — 247 passengers
and their compressed belongings, fuel for the long sector, and the
July heat, which thins the air and lengthens every takeoff roll.

The roll was long.

During it, the number one tire — carrying load its underinflated
neighbor could not — failed. The adjacent tire followed. Rubber shed
in strips. The wheel rims dropped onto the runway, grinding, throwing
a plume of friction sparks the length of the pavement.

The crew felt vibration. Their engine instruments showed nothing
abnormal, because nothing was abnormal about the engines.

They flew the aircraft off the runway.

And then, following the standard procedure of the age, they
retracted the landing gear.

They pulled wheels that were on fire up into unprotected wells,
packed among hydraulic lines, directly beneath the cabin floor.

The first unambiguous warnings arrived not as fire alarms — there
were none — but as a cascade of secondary failures. Hydraulic
systems dying. Pressurization faltering.

Then smoke in the cabin.

The crew declared an emergency and turned back toward Jeddah.

Behind them, the fire was burning through the cabin floor.

In the aircraft's final minutes, as it descended toward the runway
it had left less than half an hour earlier, the floor structure
failed progressively. Witnesses on the ground, and the distribution
of evidence along the approach path afterward, established that
people and debris fell from the burning aircraft over the last miles
of its flight.

Control was lost short of the runway.

There were no survivors. Two hundred and forty-seven passengers.
Fourteen crew.

It remains the deadliest accident in the history of Canadian
aviation, the deadliest ever involving a DC-8, and the deadliest
single disaster in the history of Nigerian civil aviation.

It is commemorated in almost no public place in any of the three
countries whose flags the aircraft wore.

## Sokoto, the weeks after

No bodies came home.

The returning flights of that season landed into a city where nearly
every extended family held a claim on the manifest. The scholars
resolved the impossible situation as scholars had resolved it for
centuries of pilgrims lost at sea: *salat al-gha'ib*, the funeral
prayer in absentia, performed for the collective dead in the great
mosque and repeated through the ward mosques for a week.

A federal delegation came, condoled, and left.

What settled afterward in the region's memory was a specific
residue, and men of that generation still describe it the same way.

The pilgrimage flight became an act of faith in both senses.
Undertaken in trust of God — because trust in the system would have
been eccentric.

## THE ARGUMENT: The accountability triangle

Wet leasing is not a scandal. It is a tool, and world aviation could
not function without it. Airlines lease capacity for seasons,
surges, and emergencies everywhere on earth, every day.

The scandal is a tool deployed without a jurisdiction to own its
risks.

Flight 2120 is the purest specimen in this book of what might be
called the accountability triangle, and its geometry is worth
setting out because the same shape recurs, in different costumes, in
every subsequent chapter.

Three states each held a corner.

Canada held the certificate. Saudi Arabia held the runway. Nigeria
held the passengers, the brand, and the contract.

Each corner could point, entirely honestly, at the other two.

The center — where 261 people sat — was sovereign to nobody.

There is a general principle here that reaches well beyond aviation
into every domain where responsibility is contracted across borders:
supply chains, offshore manufacturing, financial intermediation,
content moderation, arms transfers.

*A state that is too weak to perform a function does not escape that
function's risks by contracting it out. It re-imports them —
unregulated, marked up, and denominated in someone else's
institutional quality.*

Nigeria in 1991 was buying safety oversight the way it bought
aircraft: second-hand, sight unseen, from whoever would extend
credit.

Flight 2120 was the price discovery.

## The index card

It is fair to ask what a competent state would have done
differently, and the answer is neither exotic nor expensive.

Every element of it existed somewhere in the world in 1991:

- Technical conditions written into the charter contract, with audit
  rights, and the rights exercised
- A two-person technical team stationed with the operation for the
  six-week season, with the authority to ground an aircraft
- Approval of the operator by the state's *regulator*, not by the
  airline's commercial department — the distinction between a
  customer's due diligence and a sovereign's, which is the whole
  difference between price and safety as the binding constraint
- A manifest system that knows who boarded
- A family assistance protocol
- A standing arrangement to participate in any foreign investigation
  touching the state's citizens

Total cost of that apparatus, per pilgrimage season, at 1991 prices:
less than the catering bill.

India, running a pilgrim airlift an order of magnitude larger
through the same decades, built approximately this machinery —
imperfectly, with its own scandals, but with the state present
*inside* the operation rather than outside it.

The index card was not expensive.

It was institutionally impossible — because every line on it
presumes an agency with the authority to impose costs on politically
connected commerce, and Chapter 3 has described exactly what stood
in that place in 1991.

The difference between the disaster that happened and the routine
season that could have happened was not money, technology, or
expertise.

It was the existence of one institution able to say no and make it
stick.

That is the cheapest and rarest component in the entire machinery of
flight.

## THE ARGUMENT: Two countries read one report

The Saudi report was unusually direct for the genre. The aircraft
had been dispatched with tires known to be below limits.
Serviceable equipment had not been available. Management, under
commercial pressure, had permitted continued operation rather than
ground the aircraft.

The technical chain was established in forensic detail:
underinflation, overdeflection, thermal failure, fire carried
retracted into an unprotected wheel well, hydraulic and floor
burn-through.

One document was produced. Two countries read it.

In Canada, the disaster produced press investigation, regulatory
soul-searching about the economics of aggressive charter operators,
civil litigation, and a public record — books, documentaries,
journalism — in which the dead of Jeddah, though foreigners, entered
the national aviation conscience. The operator was destroyed by the
exposure; Nationair collapsed into bankruptcy in 1993. Ask a
Canadian aircraft maintenance engineer about that airline a
generation later and the answer is a short course on dispatch
pressure and the duty to refuse.

In Nigeria — the state of the victims — the report went
substantially unread. It was not translated into policy, testimony,
curriculum, or memorial. No Nigerian public inquiry examined how the
national carrier had come to place a quarter-thousand citizens on an
aircraft whose operator's strain was visible from Montreal. No
reform of charter oversight followed. The pilgrimage airlift resumed
under successor arrangements of varying quality, on essentially the
same architecture, for another fifteen years.

The difference between those two readings is very nearly the whole
subject of this book.

Aviation safety is usually described as a technology or a budget.

It is more fundamentally a *reading practice*: the institutional
habit of treating every report as though it were addressed to
oneself.

Canada, which lost fourteen citizens, read the report as an
indictment of Canada.

Nigeria, which lost two hundred and forty-seven, read it as news
about a Canadian airline.

## THE ECONOMICS: What a life was worth

Money is the aftermath's coldest chapter and its most revealing, and
the settlement geometry followed the accountability geometry
exactly.

The fourteen Canadian crew members' families proceeded through
Canadian channels against an airline sliding toward bankruptcy —
which converted their claims into positions in a creditors' queue,
but left a documented trail.

The 247 pilgrim families entered a different universe.

A lattice of the operator's insurers, the charterer's own troubled
underwriting, the political promises of the pilgrimage apparatus,
and — the decisive element — the liability regime then in force.

The Warsaw Convention of 1929 capped an international air carrier's
liability per passenger at a figure denominated in gold francs.
Translated into early-1990s money, the cap valued a human life at a
sum that reads today as obscene, and it applied regardless of the
victim's earnings, dependants, or age.

Families navigated this from villages without lawyers, against
counterparties with every incentive to wait. Partial disbursements
arrived years late and diminished. Other claims exhausted into
silence.

No Nigerian official document states, anywhere, what the families of
Flight 2120 were finally paid.

Now the general point, which is the one that matters for everything
that follows.

Compensation is not an epilogue to safety. It is part of safety's
*price system*.

Economists working on regulatory policy use a concept called the
value of a statistical life — the amount a society implicitly pays
to avoid one expected death, revealed by what it spends on safety
measures. It is a deliberately unsentimental instrument, and it does
real work: it tells you which safety investments a system will make
and which it will decline.

Where the deaths of passengers cost carriers and states dearly, that
cost flows upstream into maintenance and dispatch decisions. The
tire gets serviced because the alternative is ruinous.

Where deaths are cheap, cheapness flows upstream too.

The families of Flight 2120 were failed three times: by a
maintenance economy, by an oversight vacuum, and by a settlement
system that priced both failures, for their authors, at
approximately nothing.

The Montreal Convention of 1999 eventually rebuilt the
international liability regime, raising and effectively unlimiting
carrier liability — and Nigeria is a party. But treaty ceilings only
bind where domestic courts can enforce them at tolerable speed,
which returns the matter, as every chapter in this Part does, to
institutions.

## What the dead eventually bought

The learning came — a generation late, by the indirect route, and
unsigned.

The accident became a foundation case in maintenance human-factors
training worldwide. It supplied the canonical answer to a question
every maintenance organization must settle in advance: what happens
in our company when the correct action is unavailable and the
schedule is waiting?

Tire pressure monitoring migrated from luxury to standard on
subsequent designs. Wheel-well fire protection became an
airworthiness talking point for the older generation. Dispatch
governance tightened around landing gear defects.

International law narrowed the wet-lease gap. An amendment to the
Chicago Convention created machinery for formally transferring
oversight duties between states in lease arrangements, and the
serious jurisdictions built foreign-carrier assessment programs —
ramp inspection regimes, published ban lists — that treat "not our
registry" as the beginning of oversight rather than its end.

And Nigeria's own pilgrimage operation was eventually rebuilt: after
2006 the reformed regulator brought Hajj charters inside real
certification discipline, the Saudis tightened admission standards
for aircraft entering the airlift, and a permanent National Hajj
Commission finally gave the operation an institutional owner. The
airlift of recent decades — flown substantially by Nigerian
carriers, on modern aircraft — is among the reform era's least
celebrated successes, precisely because its success consists of the
absence of news.

So the dead of Flight 2120 did purchase their reforms.

What they never received was the acknowledgment — the public
connection of the change to the event that taught it.

That pattern will recur at every crash in this book. The system
learns eventually, obliquely, once external pressure and internal
reform happen to align, and the learning arrives unsigned, never
traced publicly to the event, never credited to the people who paid
its tuition.

A country that cannot connect its reforms to its catastrophes cannot
teach its own history to its successors.

Which is how a nation finds itself, in 2018, buying a livery at an
air show.

## After

Every July the anniversary passes with little notice in the country
the pilgrims came from.

They died of arrangements designed so that no one had the duty to
notice: flags borrowed, oversight assumed away, responsibility
priced out of the contract line by line.

Flight 2120 stands first among this book's seven disasters because
it shows the pattern naked, before weather or piloting can blur the
sightline. A known deficiency. A commercial pressure. A decision to
defer. An institutional vacuum where the refusal should have come
from.

And then physics, which negotiates with no one.

Everything that follows will be more Nigerian in its mechanics:
Nigerian airlines, Nigerian runways, Nigerian regulators.

Nothing that follows will be different in kind.

Fourteen months later, the pattern repeated in a different costume —
not a leased airliner but a military transport, not pilgrims but the
officer corps of the Nigerian armed forces, and not a foreign report
unread but a domestic one that was never written for the public at
all.
