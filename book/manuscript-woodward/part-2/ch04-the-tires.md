# PART II — THE CRASHES

# 4. The Tires

## Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, July 11, 1991, 08:29 local

They boarded in white. Two hundred and forty-seven Nigerian
pilgrims, most of them from Sokoto and the country's far north, in
the seamless cloth of ihram, which is the garment of consecration
worn for the pilgrimage and, in Muslim burial practice, the color of
the shroud.

Most were farmers and traders. For nearly all of them the Hajj was
the journey of a lifetime, financed by decades of saving and often
by the sale of land or cattle. Village savings cohorts traveled
together. When they came home they would be addressed differently
for the rest of their lives.

The aircraft that would carry them wore three flags at once. It was
a Douglas DC-8, twenty-three years old, built in California. It was
owned and operated by Nationair, a charter company based in
Montreal. It was painted, for the season, in the green and white of
Nigeria Airways.

The arrangement is called a wet lease, which means an aircraft
rented together with its crew, its maintenance, and its insurance,
so that the machine flies under one company's brand while answering,
in every operational particular, to another's. Nigeria Airways, by
1991 unable to mount the pilgrimage surge from its own decaying
fleet, had contracted the lift. Nationair, whose aggressive growth
had outrun its cash, needed the revenue. Two institutional
desperations had met in a marketplace.

The flight was designated Nigeria Airways 2120. From pushback to
impact it would last twenty-nine minutes. The evidence had begun
accumulating days earlier.

## What was known before they boarded

The Saudi investigators would eventually fix the origin of the
disaster not in the sky but at a desk. In the days before the
accident, tire pressure checks on the DC-8's main landing gear had
found two tires significantly below limits. The maintenance
personnel flagged it, which is not in dispute and appears in the
report. The nitrogen servicing equipment needed to correct it was
unserviceable, and procuring proper servicing meant delay.

Here the file becomes a study in how commercial pressure travels
down through an organization until it arrives, finally, at a piece
of rubber.

## How a schedule becomes a physical force

Understand what a Hajj contract is, because its structure explains
everything that followed.

Pilgrimage lift is boom economics compressed into six weeks. An
operator commits aircraft, crews, and insurance to a base thousands
of miles from home. Revenue arrives per rotation flown, while fixed
costs run whether the aircraft moves or not. So every day an
aircraft sits unserviceable, three things happen at once. The costs
continue, the revenue stops, and thousands of pilgrims holding visas
keyed to the pilgrimage calendar back up in terminals on two
continents, where the Saudi authorities enforce that calendar
mercilessly because the alternative is chaos at the holy places. The
client, meaning Nigeria Airways and behind it the Nigerian state's
pilgrimage apparatus, measures its contractor in departures
achieved.

The incentive structure is almost diagrammatic in its perversity.
Every actor in the chain is rewarded for departures and no actor in
the chain is rewarded for refusals. The cost of a refusal is
immediate, visible, and lands on the person who refuses, while the
cost of not refusing is probabilistic, delayed, and lands on someone
else. An economist would say the externality is uninternalized. A
pilot would put it differently, which is that everybody who could
say stop had a reason not to, and the one thing that could have
overruled all of them was missing.

Aviation has a name for the force that is supposed to countervail
this, and the name is airworthiness. It is the flat, unconditional
rule that the machine meets specification or it does not move.
Airworthiness is precisely the kind of rule that is boring,
expensive, and painful at exactly the wrong moment, and rules of
that kind survive only inside institutions built to enforce them
against money.

On the Jeddah ramp in July 1991 no such institution was effectively
present. Canada licensed the operator, but the operation ran nine
time zones away under a contract Canada had no reason to examine.
Saudi Arabia owned the runway, but held over a foreign-registered
transient only the blunt instrument of a ramp inspection. And
Nigeria, which owned the passengers, the brand on the fin, and the
contract, had persuaded itself that the entire purpose of chartering
was to buy capacity and shed accountability in a single transaction.
It wrote no meaningful technical conditions into the charter. It
sent no technical representative. It stationed nobody with the
authority to ground an aircraft.

The rule met the money alone, and the rule lost.

## Why a tire is not a minor component

Ask a hundred passengers to rank the components of an airliner by
how much they should worry about them and the tires will finish
last. This intuition is wrong, and the physics of why is worth
understanding, because the same physics recurs in Chapter 7 at Kano.

An aircraft tire at takeoff weight is carrying loads that have no
equivalent in ordinary experience. A fully loaded DC-8 distributes
more than a hundred tons across its main gear, and its tires are
inflated to something on the order of two hundred pounds per square
inch, which is roughly six times the pressure in a car tire. That
pressure is what carries the load. Underinflate the tire and it does
not simply risk going flat. It flexes beyond its design envelope on
every rotation, and flexing rubber generates heat with extraordinary
efficiency, converting mechanical energy directly into thermal
energy inside a material that is an excellent insulator and
therefore cannot shed it.

On a long takeoff roll, at high weight, in July heat, on a runway in
Saudi Arabia, this becomes an exponential process. Heat softens
rubber, softer rubber flexes more, and more flexing generates more
heat. The failure mode is not a puncture. It is thermal runaway.

Two other numbers complete the picture. An underinflated tire
transfers its share of the load to its neighbor on the same axle, so
the failures are not independent events and the first tire's
degradation actively causes the second's. And the DC-8, a design of
the late 1950s, had no fire detection or extinguishing capability in
its main wheel wells at all, because the certification standards of
its era did not require it, the scenario not having yet been
imagined vividly enough.

## 08:29 to 08:58

The aircraft taxied out near maximum takeoff weight, carrying 247
passengers and their compressed belongings, fuel for the long
sector, and the July heat, which thins the air and lengthens every
takeoff roll.

The roll was long. During it, the number one tire, carrying load its
underinflated neighbor could not, failed, and the adjacent tire
followed. Rubber shed in strips. The wheel rims dropped onto the
runway, grinding, throwing a plume of friction sparks the length of
the pavement.

The crew felt vibration. Their engine instruments showed nothing
abnormal, because nothing was abnormal about the engines. They flew
the aircraft off the runway, and then, following the standard
procedure of the age, they retracted the landing gear. They pulled
wheels that were on fire up into unprotected wells, packed among
hydraulic lines, directly beneath the cabin floor.

The first unambiguous warnings arrived not as fire alarms, because
there were none, but as a cascade of secondary failures. Hydraulic
systems dying. Pressurization faltering. Then smoke in the cabin.

The crew declared an emergency and turned back toward Jeddah. Behind
them the fire was burning through the cabin floor. In the aircraft's
final minutes, as it descended toward the runway it had left less
than half an hour earlier, the floor structure failed
progressively. Witnesses on the ground, and the distribution of
evidence along the approach path afterward, established that people
and debris fell from the burning aircraft over the last miles of its
flight.

Control was lost short of the runway. There were no survivors. Two
hundred and forty-seven passengers and fourteen crew.

It remains the deadliest accident in the history of Canadian
aviation, the deadliest ever involving a DC-8, and the deadliest
single disaster in the history of Nigerian civil aviation. It is
commemorated in almost no public place in any of the three countries
whose flags the aircraft wore.

## Sokoto, the weeks after

No bodies came home.

The returning flights of that season landed into a city where nearly
every extended family held a claim on the manifest. The scholars
resolved the impossible situation as scholars had resolved it for
centuries of pilgrims lost at sea, with salat al-gha'ib, the funeral
prayer in absentia, performed for the collective dead in the great
mosque and repeated through the ward mosques for a week. A federal
delegation came, condoled, and left.

What settled afterward in the region's memory was a specific
residue, and men of that generation still describe it the same way.
The pilgrimage flight became an act of faith in both senses,
undertaken in trust of God, because trust in the system would have
been eccentric.

## The accountability triangle

Wet leasing is not a scandal. It is a tool, and world aviation could
not function without it. Airlines lease capacity for seasons,
surges, and emergencies everywhere on earth, every day. The scandal
is a tool deployed without a jurisdiction to own its risks.

Flight 2120 is the purest specimen in this book of what might be
called the accountability triangle, and its geometry is worth
setting out because the same shape recurs, in different costumes, in
every subsequent chapter. Three states each held a corner. Canada
held the certificate, Saudi Arabia held the runway, and Nigeria held
the passengers, the brand, and the contract. Each corner could
point, entirely honestly, at the other two. The center, where 261
people sat, was sovereign to nobody.

There is a general principle here that reaches well beyond aviation
into every domain where responsibility is contracted across borders,
including supply chains, offshore manufacturing, financial
intermediation, content moderation, and arms transfers. A state that
is too weak to perform a function does not escape that function's
risks by contracting it out. It re-imports them, unregulated, marked
up, and denominated in someone else's institutional quality. Nigeria
in 1991 was buying safety oversight the way it bought aircraft,
second-hand and sight unseen from whoever would extend credit, and
Flight 2120 was the price discovery.

## The index card

It is fair to ask what a competent state would have done
differently, and the answer is neither exotic nor expensive. Every
element of it existed somewhere in the world in 1991.

Technical conditions written into the charter contract, with audit
rights, and the rights actually exercised. A two-person technical
team stationed with the operation for the six-week season, holding
the authority to ground an aircraft. Approval of the operator by the
state's regulator rather than by the airline's commercial
department, which is the distinction between a customer's due
diligence and a sovereign's and therefore the whole difference
between price and safety as the binding constraint. A manifest
system that knows who boarded. A family assistance protocol. A
standing arrangement to participate in any foreign investigation
touching the state's citizens.

The total cost of that apparatus per pilgrimage season, at 1991
prices, was less than the catering bill. India, running a pilgrim
airlift an order of magnitude larger through the same decades, built
approximately this machinery, imperfectly and with its own scandals,
but with the state present inside the operation rather than outside
it.

The index card was not expensive. It was institutionally
impossible, because every line on it presumes an agency with the
authority to impose costs on politically connected commerce, and
Chapter 3 has described exactly what stood in that place in 1991.
The difference between the disaster that happened and the routine
season that could have happened was not money, technology, or
expertise. It was the existence of one institution able to say no
and make it stick, which is the cheapest and rarest component in the
entire machinery of flight.

## Two countries read one report

The Saudi report was unusually direct for the genre. The aircraft
had been dispatched with tires known to be below limits. Serviceable
equipment had not been available. Management, under commercial
pressure, had permitted continued operation rather than ground the
aircraft. The technical chain was established in forensic detail,
running from underinflation to overdeflection to thermal failure to
fire carried retracted into an unprotected wheel well and then to
hydraulic and floor burn-through.

One document was produced. Two countries read it.

In Canada the disaster produced press investigation, regulatory
soul-searching about the economics of aggressive charter operators,
civil litigation, and a public record of books, documentaries, and
journalism in which the dead of Jeddah, though foreigners, entered
the national aviation conscience. The operator was destroyed by the
exposure and Nationair collapsed into bankruptcy in 1993. Ask a
Canadian aircraft maintenance engineer about that airline a
generation later and the answer is a short course on dispatch
pressure and the duty to refuse.

In Nigeria, the state of the victims, the report went substantially
unread. It was not translated into policy, testimony, curriculum, or
memorial. No Nigerian public inquiry examined how the national
carrier had come to place a quarter-thousand citizens on an aircraft
whose operator's strain was visible from Montreal. No reform of
charter oversight followed. The pilgrimage airlift resumed under
successor arrangements of varying quality, on essentially the same
architecture, for another fifteen years.

The difference between those two readings is very nearly the whole
subject of this book. Aviation safety is usually described as a
technology or a budget. It is more fundamentally a reading practice,
meaning the institutional habit of treating every report as though
it were addressed to oneself. Canada, which lost fourteen citizens,
read the report as an indictment of Canada. Nigeria, which lost two
hundred and forty-seven, read it as news about a Canadian airline.

## What a life was worth

Money is the aftermath's coldest chapter and its most revealing, and
the settlement geometry followed the accountability geometry
exactly.

The fourteen Canadian crew members' families proceeded through
Canadian channels against an airline sliding toward bankruptcy,
which converted their claims into positions in a creditors' queue
but left a documented trail. The 247 pilgrim families entered a
different universe, consisting of a lattice of the operator's
insurers, the charterer's own troubled underwriting, the political
promises of the pilgrimage apparatus, and, decisively, the liability
regime then in force.

The Warsaw Convention of 1929 capped an international air carrier's
liability per passenger at a figure denominated in gold francs.
Translated into early-1990s money the cap valued a human life at
something in the order of twenty thousand dollars, a sum that reads
today as obscene, and it applied regardless of the victim's
earnings, dependants, or age. Families navigated this from villages
without lawyers, against counterparties with every incentive to
wait. Partial disbursements arrived years late and diminished. Other
claims exhausted into silence. No Nigerian official document states,
anywhere, what the families of Flight 2120 were finally paid.

Now the general point, which is the one that matters for everything
that follows. Compensation is not an epilogue to safety. It is part
of safety's price system. Economists working on regulatory policy
use a concept called the value of a statistical life, meaning the
amount a society implicitly pays to avoid one expected death as
revealed by what it spends on safety measures. It is a deliberately
unsentimental instrument and it does real work, because it tells you
which safety investments a system will make and which it will
decline. Where the deaths of passengers cost carriers and states
dearly, that cost flows upstream into maintenance and dispatch
decisions, and the tire gets serviced because the alternative is
ruinous. Where deaths are cheap, cheapness flows upstream too.

The families of Flight 2120 were failed three times, by a
maintenance economy, by an oversight vacuum, and by a settlement
system that priced both failures, for their authors, at
approximately nothing. The Montreal Convention of 1999 eventually
rebuilt the international liability regime, raising and effectively
unlimiting carrier liability, and Nigeria is a party to it. But
treaty ceilings only bind where domestic courts can enforce them at
tolerable speed, which returns the matter, as every chapter in this
Part does, to institutions.

## What the dead eventually bought

The learning came a generation late, by the indirect route, and
unsigned.

The accident became a foundation case in maintenance human-factors
training worldwide, and it supplied the canonical answer to a
question every maintenance organization must settle in advance,
which is what happens in this company when the correct action is
unavailable and the schedule is waiting. Tire pressure monitoring
migrated from luxury to standard on subsequent designs. Wheel-well
fire protection became an airworthiness talking point for the older
generation. Dispatch governance tightened around landing gear
defects.

International law narrowed the wet-lease gap. An amendment to the
Chicago Convention created machinery for formally transferring
oversight duties between states in lease arrangements, and the
serious jurisdictions built foreign-carrier assessment programs,
including ramp inspection regimes and published ban lists, that
treat the phrase not our registry as the beginning of oversight
rather than its end.

Nigeria's own pilgrimage operation was eventually rebuilt. After
2006 the reformed regulator brought Hajj charters inside real
certification discipline, the Saudis tightened admission standards
for aircraft entering the airlift, and a permanent National Hajj
Commission finally gave the operation an institutional owner. The
airlift of recent decades, flown substantially by Nigerian carriers
on modern aircraft, is among the reform era's least celebrated
successes, precisely because its success consists of the absence of
news.

So the dead of Flight 2120 did purchase their reforms. What they
never received was the acknowledgment, meaning the public connection
of the change to the event that taught it. That pattern will recur
at every crash in this book. The system learns eventually and
obliquely, once external pressure and internal reform happen to
align, and the learning arrives unsigned, never traced publicly to
the event and never credited to the people who paid its tuition. A
country that cannot connect its reforms to its catastrophes cannot
teach its own history to its successors, which is how a nation finds
itself, in 2018, buying a livery at an air show.

## After

Every July the anniversary passes with little notice in the country
the pilgrims came from. They died of arrangements designed so that
no one had the duty to notice, with flags borrowed, oversight
assumed away, and responsibility priced out of the contract line by
line.

Flight 2120 stands first among this book's seven disasters because
it shows the pattern naked, before weather or piloting can blur the
sightline. A known deficiency. A commercial pressure. A decision to
defer. An institutional vacuum where the refusal should have come
from. And then physics, which negotiates with no one.

Everything that follows will be more Nigerian in its mechanics,
involving Nigerian airlines, Nigerian runways, and Nigerian
regulators. Nothing that follows will be different in kind.

Fourteen months later the pattern repeated in a different costume,
not a leased airliner but a military transport, not pilgrims but the
officer corps of the Nigerian armed forces, and not a foreign report
left unread but a domestic one that was never written for the public
at all.
