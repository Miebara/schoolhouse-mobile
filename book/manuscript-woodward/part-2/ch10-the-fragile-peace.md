# 10. The Fragile Peace

## Iju-Ishaga, Lagos, Sunday, June 3, 2012, 15:45

The videos are still online.

A Sunday afternoon in a dense residential quarter northwest of the
airport. The smoke column. And the crowd — thousands deep within
minutes, filling the narrow streets, passing water in basins,
pulling at hot aluminum with bare hands and generator extension bars
while the fire found the fuel.

It was the first fully documented Nigerian air disaster. Streamed,
time-stamped, uploaded by hundreds of phones that had not existed in
2005.

The information monopoly that had sealed 1992 and managed 1996 was
structurally gone.

Whatever the institutions failed to learn from Dana Air Flight 992,
the public would own its own archive.

## The airline that was doing everything right

This is what made it frightening.

Dana Air was one of the reformed market's successes. An
Indian-owned Nigerian carrier, audited to the international
operational standard, flying a young-for-Nigeria fleet of MD-83s on
the trunk routes, with a punctuality reputation that had made it a
business-class favorite.

Flight 992 was the mid-afternoon Abuja–Lagos service — the same city
pair as Bellview 210, flown in reverse — in a country approaching
six years without a scheduled-airline catastrophe.

The paperwork was in order.

What the paperwork did not show was that the aircraft descending
toward Lagos was dying.

## The first engine

Well before arrival, the number one engine had begun losing thrust,
starved by degrees through a fuel-supply fault.

The crew registered the anomaly.

And continued toward Lagos.

No emergency was declared. No diversion was made to the nearer field
behind them.

On final approach, the second engine — drawing on the same fuel
system — collapsed.

An airliner without engines is a glider with a brutal glide ratio,
and the arithmetic came up about two miles short. The MD-83 struck a
two-story building in Iju-Ishaga and burned.

All 153 people aboard died, along with at least six on the ground.
As at Kano, the true ground toll was never established to anyone's
confidence.

## THE ARGUMENT: The most expensive minutes in aviation

The decision the crew of Flight 992 made is one of the most studied
categories in aviation psychology, and it deserves to be examined
without hindsight bias, because the reasoning that led to it was not
stupid.

A twin-engine airliner is certified around a premise that is both
brutal and liberating: any single engine may fail at any moment, and
the aircraft must be able to fly, climb, and land on the survivor.

That premise converts an engine failure from an emergency into a
*procedure*.

But the premise has a hidden condition, and the condition is what
killed 153 people.

It assumes the failure is the *opening* of an emergency window, not
a condition to be managed indefinitely.

The crew's election to continue had real arguments behind it. The
engine had not failed outright — it was degrading. Lagos was close.
Lagos was the maintenance base, where the aircraft could be
repaired. And the diversion airports available in the Nigerian
network of 2012 offered their own uncertainties: fuel availability,
ground handling, onward passage for 150 passengers.

Each of those considerations was reasonable in isolation.

Together they produced a decision to spend the aircraft's remaining
redundancy on convenience.

And then the second anomaly arrived and spent it for them.

The industry's summary doctrine, hard-won across many accidents on
every continent, is now taught with Flight 992 among its case
studies:

*The first anomaly buys you options. Spend them immediately, because
the second anomaly will spend them for you.*

This is plan continuation bias again — the same cognitive structure
that killed Sosoliso 1145 on approach — but wearing engineering
clothes rather than meteorological ones.

And it carries the same institutional lesson. The countermeasure is
never exhortation. It is procedure that removes the judgment call
from the moment when judgment is least available: mandatory
diversion criteria, triggered by defined parameters, briefed in
advance, non-negotiable in the air.

## The afternoon, hour by hour

The response of 2012 was the *reformed* system's response, and both
its differences from 2005 and its samenesses are findings.

It was faster. Lagos State's emergency agency — built out of the
state government's own post-2005 investments — had crews moving
inside the hour. The federal agencies followed.

And it was still defeated by the two variables no siren solves.

Access: the crowds that had beaten the responders to the site now
stood between them and it, and Lagos Sunday traffic did the rest.

Equipment: the decisive deficit was lift. The collapsed building
needed heavy cranes, and the first crane equal to the slab arrived
after dark — hours into a fire fought with water tenders shuttling
from distant hydrants.

Nobody aboard could have been saved by speed; the recorders and the
medicine established that.

On the ground, under the slab, the crane's timeline was the
negotiation.

No accounting of it was ever published.

## THE ARGUMENT: Read the crowd correctly

The crowd at Iju-Ishaga was widely criticized in the days that
followed — for obstructing the responders, for looting, for turning
a rescue into a spectacle.

Some of that criticism was fair. All of it missed the more important
point.

Nigerians came to that wreck in their thousands because twenty years
had taught them a set of entirely accurate beliefs: that the state
arrives late, that help is a neighbor, and that presence is the only
oversight.

Every chapter of this book has documented the education that
produced those beliefs. Ejigbo, where the neighbors were the
response. The lagoon, where the fishermen got there first. Lisa,
where farmers found what the state could not.

The crowd impeding the rescue at Iju-Ishaga was the accumulated
residue of every earlier failed response.

It was distrust, embodied, at scale — complicating the very
operation it had assembled to compensate for.

There is a general lesson here for anyone designing emergency
systems in low-trust environments, and Chapter 13 will build on it:
you do not get to choose whether the crowd shows up. You only get to
choose whether you have trained and interfaced with it, or whether
you will spend your first hour fighting it.

## THE NUMBERS: Five years

The Accident Investigation Bureau — the reform's own child —
performed the forensic core of its task competently.

Recorders recovered and promptly read. Engines torn down to their
fuel systems. A technical narrative established that has never been
overturned.

Then the report entered the institutional atmosphere and slowed.

Interim statements came. The final report did not appear until
2017.

Five years.

For an accident whose essential mechanics the investigators
understood within months, with 153 families waiting, identical
aircraft in national service, and a suspended-and-restored airline
flying the fleet.

Put the delay in comparative terms, because the comparison is the
argument.

The international standard expects a final report within twelve
months, or a public interim statement explaining why not. Strong
systems miss the twelve-month target routinely — the work is genuinely
hard — but they bridge the gap with loud, continuous interim
publication, so the *learning* flows while the lawyers argue.

Thirteen months after Iju-Ishaga, an Asiana Airlines Boeing 777
crashed on landing at San Francisco, killing three. Within days, the
American investigator was briefing the world on recorder data —
publicly, on camera, hypothesis by labeled hypothesis. Within a
year: public hearings, a docket of thousands of pages, a final
report, and industry-wide training changes.

The entire digestive cycle from wreckage to reform, completed inside
the interval in which the Dana file was still marinating.

That velocity is not a function of American wealth. It is a function
of design: statutory independence, deadlines that bind in practice,
and above all a public-facing culture in which the investigation
*itself* is the reassurance, and its openness is the product being
delivered.

A state whose citizens can watch it learn does not need to ask them
for trust.

The Lagos crowds of June 2012 — pulling at the wreckage themselves,
trusting nothing they could not touch — were the photographic
negative of that proposition.

The distance between those two images is the remaining distance of
the Nigerian reform.

## What the report said, and what happened to it

When the final report emerged in 2017, its substance rewarded the
wait even as its tempo indicted it.

The findings assembled a textbook dual-failure narrative: the fuel
supply degradation of the first engine, traceable through component
histories; the crew's decision to continue rather than divert; the
collapse of the second engine drawing on the same system.

And beneath the sequence, the organizational layers — the
maintenance decision chain for the sick engine in the preceding
days, the dispatch and pairing questions, the fatigue ecology of a
punishing shuttle schedule.

One recommendation carried this book's entire argument inside it:

*Measures to ensure that commercial pressure could not override
maintenance decision-making.*

Each finding went to a named addressee.

Then the transmission question — which is the question this book
keeps asking of every document it examines.

What closed?

The narrow, type-specific technical items: substantially, yes. And
the aircraft type aged out of Nigerian service within the decade
regardless.

The systemic items entered a recommendations register, and there the
public trail thins to nothing. There is no published follow-up in
which a citizen, a bereaved parent, or an underwriter could look up
whether the commercial-pressure safeguards now exist in auditable
form.

So the dead of Iju-Ishaga purchased, verifiably, some narrow
technical protections.

And unverifiably, everything else.

That ratio — the verifiable narrow to the unverifiable broad — is
the signature of the era this chapter names.

Improving it requires no new catastrophe, no new statute, and no new
money.

Only the publication, on a schedule, of a register the investigator
already keeps.

## THE NUMBERS: The decade the reform aged

Four dated episodes give the plateau its texture, and together they
constitute the debit column against the credit column of Chapter 9.

**2013: the reformer removed.**

Weeks after a charter aircraft crashed on takeoff at Lagos —
carrying, in a detail of almost unbearable symbolic economy, the
body of a former state governor to his own funeral — the government
removed Harold Demuren from the NCAA, months from the end of his
tenure.

It was reflexive accountability theater, applied to the agency whose
decade had made the crash exceptional in the first place.

The signal traveled the system immediately: tenure protection holds
only until it is tested by embarrassment.

The office has turned over with governments since.

And the charter crash itself marked the reformed perimeter's edge —
an operator whose condition the audits had not caught, in the sector
where Nigerian aviation's oldest risks have always lived.

**2016 to 2017: the currency test.**

The naira collapsed. Ticket revenue trapped in a falling currency;
lease, spares, and insurance obligations firm in dollars; foreign
carriers' earnings marooned in Nigerian accounts by the hundreds of
millions. The state's bad-asset manager took the two largest
carriers into care.

Here the ledger must be honest in both directions.

Airlines died. Their passengers did not.

No return of the geriatric fleet. No visible collapse of maintenance
discipline. No accident cluster.

The floors built in 2006 — fleet-age limits, financial surveillance,
audited operators — held under precisely the economic pressure that
had liquefied the system of the 1980s.

This is the most underappreciated fact in modern Nigerian aviation,
and it is worth stating as a general principle: *a regulatory
system's quality is measured not in good years but in what its
constraints refuse to yield when everything else is yielding.*

**2020 to 2021: the pandemic, and the military again.**

Aviation stopped almost entirely, then restarted into a world of
parked aircraft and rusty crews that the global safety community
warned was itself a hazard. The Nigerian restart produced incidents
and no catastrophe. A qualified pass.

And in the same season, the military bill came due again. Twice in
three months.

In February 2021, a Nigerian Air Force turboprop went down on
approach to Abuja, killing seven.

In May 2021, another went down at Kaduna in weather, killing all
aboard — including Lieutenant General Ibrahim Attahiru, the serving
Chief of Army Staff, and much of his personal staff.

Twenty-nine years after Ejigbo: an air force investigating itself,
findings unpublished, a decapitated command.

A civil reform whose writ still stopped, as it had always stopped,
at the barracks gate.

No stronger proof exists that Nigeria's recovery was a
civil-institutional achievement rather than a national
transformation.

The domain the reforms reached got safe.

The domain they were never permitted to reach kept its 1992
physics intact.

**2022: the statute renewed.**

The legislative machinery, at least, still converts lessons into
law. The Civil Aviation Act was renewed, and the Accident
Investigation Bureau was reconstituted as the Nigerian Safety
Investigation Bureau, with a multimodal mandate covering aviation,
rail, and marine, on the American model.

On paper, the most advanced investigative architecture in Africa.

Its early record suggests an institution that understands it is on
probation: final reports at a tempo its predecessor never sustained,
preliminary statements published within weeks in the international
format, first investigations into rail and marine occurrences,
cooperation agreements with foreign agencies, a recorder laboratory
offered to the region.

Whether it becomes the genuine article or merely wears the
organogram will be read, as everything in this book is read, in its
publication rate.

Of all the institutions these pages audit, it is the only one whose
current decade is its best.

## The frontier that was never closed

The 2013 charter crash marked the edge of the reformed perimeter,
and the sector beyond that edge grew through the following decade
into one of the largest business-aviation markets in Africa.

Executive jets of the banking and oil aristocracy. Political
charters in campaign seasons. Helicopter shuttles in the delta and
across Lagos traffic.

Its structural feature is the hazard sign this book has taught the
reader to recognize.

Its passengers are the most powerful people in the country — and its
oversight is therefore the most negotiated space in the system.

The recurring findings in the investigative dockets describe the
pattern: private registrations flying commercial charters outside
certificate discipline; foreign-registered jets domiciled in Nigeria
beyond the practical reach of their registries; duty and load
records of ceremonial accuracy; enforcement campaigns that crest
after each embarrassing incident and recede as the calls are
returned.

And here the analytical point seeded in Chapter 6 completes itself.

The elite's exit from the public system did not exempt the powerful
from institutional quality.

It re-exposed them to it — because private oversight is exactly as
strong as the public regulator standing behind it, and the private
sphere is where that regulator is weakest.

The powerful cannot secede from the competence of their own state.

They can only choose which of its jurisdictions — the audited or the
negotiated — to entrust their families to.

The record of their choosing is an irony this book leaves, without
further comment, to its readers among them.

## THE NUMBERS: What a plateau looks like

The honest ledger of Nigerian aviation after 2012 has two columns,
and both are real.

**The credit column** is substantial and too rarely stated. As of
this writing, Nigerian scheduled airlines have gone well over a
decade without a passenger-jet catastrophe — the longest such run in
the country's history, sustained across a doubling of traffic, a
currency collapse worse than the 1980s original, an insurgency, a
pandemic, and the failure of several carriers.

Nigerians under twenty-five have no memory of the annual mass
funeral that structured their parents' relationship to the departure
gate.

By the only measure that finally matters, the reform worked and kept
working.

**The debit column** is this chapter: five-year reports; suspension
and restoration cycles that read cumulatively as oversight by
oscillation; a charter periphery under visibly thinner scrutiny; a
military sector untouched and repeating; a training college and an
inspectorate straining to hold technical staff against Gulf
salaries.

And beneath all of it, the item that Chapter 9 predicted: the
evaporation of the political constituency that 2005 and 2006 briefly
created.

The system is no longer decaying toward 1996.

It is plateaued — maintained by professional inertia, audit
exposure, and institutional memory rather than by any active
political demand.

Plateaus are respectable.

But aviation is a domain of moving baselines. Traffic grows. Fleets
age. People churn. Surveillance is a coverage function, and coverage
requirements scale with movements.

Which produces the finding that closes this Part:

*A safety system standing still is, relative to its risk base,
walking slowly backward.*

The fragile peace is peace.

It is also fragile, and it knows it.

## After

Part II closes where it opened: with fire in a place fire should not
be.

Between Jeddah in 1991 and Iju-Ishaga in 2012 stretch seven
disasters, some three thousand dead across scheduled, charter, and
military flying, and a single demonstrated theorem.

Nothing about the dying was necessary.

The same state, the same market, the same professionals — rearranged
after 2006 into institutions with money, autonomy, and witnesses —
stopped producing catastrophe within eighteen months, and have not
resumed.

The crashes were never fate.

They were policy, in the strict sense: the predictable output of
arrangements that were chosen, renewed, and defensible at every
point by someone whose interests they served.

What remains, before this book can argue about the future, is to
understand those arrangements themselves — not crash by crash but
pattern by pattern.

The regulator's political economy. The economics that made cutting
corners rational. The help that never came. And the silence that
made every lesson single-use.

That anatomy is Part III.

Its shortest summary can be given in advance.

Institutions are how a society remembers.

Nigerian aviation history is what forgetting costs.
