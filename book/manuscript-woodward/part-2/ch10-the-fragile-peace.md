# 10. The Fragile Peace

## Iju-Ishaga, Lagos, Sunday, June 3, 2012, 15:45

The videos are still online. A Sunday afternoon in a dense
residential quarter northwest of the airport. The smoke column. And
the crowd, thousands deep within minutes, filling the narrow
streets, passing water in basins, pulling at hot aluminum with bare
hands and generator extension bars while the fire found the fuel.

It was the first fully documented Nigerian air disaster, streamed,
time-stamped, and uploaded by hundreds of phones that had not
existed in 2005. The information monopoly that had sealed 1992 and
managed 1996 was structurally gone. Whatever the institutions failed
to learn from Dana Air Flight 992, the public would own its own
archive.

## The airline that was doing everything right

This is what made it frightening. Dana Air was one of the reformed
market's successes, an Indian-owned Nigerian carrier audited to the
international operational standard, flying a young-for-Nigeria fleet
of MD-83s on the trunk routes, with a punctuality reputation that
had made it a business-class favorite. Flight 992 was the
mid-afternoon Abuja to Lagos service, the same city pair as Bellview
210 flown in reverse, in a country approaching six years without a
scheduled-airline catastrophe.

The paperwork was in order. What the paperwork did not show was that
the aircraft descending toward Lagos was dying.

## The first engine

Well before arrival the number one engine had begun losing thrust,
starved by degrees through a fuel-supply fault. The crew registered
the anomaly, and continued toward Lagos. No emergency was declared
and no diversion was made to the nearer field behind them. On final
approach the second engine, drawing on the same fuel system,
collapsed.

An airliner without engines is a glider with a brutal glide ratio,
and the arithmetic came up about two miles short. The MD-83 struck a
two-story building in Iju-Ishaga and burned. All 153 people aboard
died, along with at least six on the ground, and as at Kano the true
ground toll was never established to anyone's confidence.

## The most expensive minutes in aviation

The decision the crew of Flight 992 made is one of the most studied
categories in aviation psychology, and it deserves to be examined
without hindsight bias, because the reasoning that led to it was not
stupid.

A twin-engine airliner is certified around a premise that is both
brutal and liberating, which is that any single engine may fail at
any moment and the aircraft must be able to fly, climb, and land on
the survivor. That premise converts an engine failure from an
emergency into a procedure. But the premise has a hidden condition,
and the condition is what killed 153 people, because it assumes the
failure is the opening of an emergency window rather than a
condition to be managed indefinitely.

The crew's election to continue had real arguments behind it. The
engine had not failed outright but was degrading. Lagos was close.
Lagos was the maintenance base, where the aircraft could be
repaired. And the diversion airports available in the Nigerian
network of 2012 offered their own uncertainties around fuel
availability, ground handling, and onward passage for 150
passengers. Each of those considerations was reasonable in
isolation, and together they produced a decision to spend the
aircraft's remaining redundancy on convenience. Then the second
anomaly arrived and spent it for them.

The industry's summary doctrine, hard-won across many accidents on
every continent, is now taught with Flight 992 among its case
studies. The first anomaly buys you options, and you should spend
them immediately, because the second anomaly will spend them for
you. This is plan continuation bias again, the same cognitive
structure that killed Sosoliso 1145 on approach, but wearing
engineering clothes rather than meteorological ones. And it carries
the same institutional lesson. The countermeasure is never
exhortation. It is procedure that removes the judgment call from the
moment when judgment is least available, through mandatory diversion
criteria, triggered by defined parameters, briefed in advance, and
non-negotiable in the air.

## The afternoon, hour by hour

The response of 2012 was the reformed system's response, and both
its differences from 2005 and its samenesses are findings.

It was faster. Lagos State's emergency agency, built out of the
state government's own post-2005 investments, had crews moving
inside the hour, and the federal agencies followed. And it was still
defeated by the two variables no siren solves. The first was access,
because the crowds that had beaten the responders to the site now
stood between them and it, and Lagos Sunday traffic did the rest.
The second was equipment, and the decisive deficit was lift, because
the collapsed building needed heavy cranes and the first crane equal
to the slab arrived after dark, hours into a fire fought with water
tenders shuttling from distant hydrants.

Nobody aboard could have been saved by speed, and the recorders and
the medicine established that. On the ground, under the slab, the
crane's timeline was the negotiation. No accounting of it was ever
published.

## Read the crowd correctly

The crowd at Iju-Ishaga was widely criticized in the days that
followed for obstructing the responders, for looting, and for
turning a rescue into a spectacle. Some of that criticism was fair
and all of it missed the more important point.

Nigerians came to that wreck in their thousands because twenty years
had taught them a set of entirely accurate beliefs, which are that
the state arrives late, that help is a neighbor, and that presence
is the only oversight. Every chapter of this book has documented the
education that produced those beliefs. Ejigbo, where the neighbors
were the response. The lagoon, where the fishermen got there first.
Lisa, where farmers found what the state could not. The crowd
impeding the rescue at Iju-Ishaga was the accumulated residue of
every earlier failed response. It was distrust, embodied, at scale,
complicating the very operation it had assembled to compensate for.

There is a general lesson here for anyone designing emergency
systems in low-trust environments, and Chapter 13 will build on it.
You do not get to choose whether the crowd shows up. You only get to
choose whether you have trained and interfaced with it, or whether
you will spend your first hour fighting it.

## Five years

The Accident Investigation Bureau, which was the reform's own child,
performed the forensic core of its task competently. Recorders were
recovered and promptly read. Engines were torn down to their fuel
systems. A technical narrative was established that has never been
overturned. Then the report entered the institutional atmosphere and
slowed. Interim statements came, and the final report did not appear
until 2017.

Five years, for an accident whose essential mechanics the
investigators understood within months, with 153 families waiting,
identical aircraft in national service, and a suspended-and-restored
airline flying the fleet.

Put the delay in comparative terms, because the comparison is the
argument. The international standard expects a final report within
twelve months, or a public interim statement explaining why not.
Strong systems miss the twelve-month target routinely, because the
work is genuinely hard, but they bridge the gap with loud,
continuous interim publication, so the learning flows while the
lawyers argue. Thirteen months after Iju-Ishaga an Asiana Airlines
Boeing 777 crashed on landing at San Francisco, killing three, and
within days the American investigator was briefing the world on
recorder data publicly, on camera, hypothesis by labeled hypothesis.
Within a year there were public hearings, a docket of thousands of
pages, a final report, and industry-wide training changes. The
entire digestive cycle from wreckage to reform was completed inside
the interval in which the Dana file was still marinating.

That velocity is not a function of American wealth. It is a function
of design, meaning statutory independence, deadlines that bind in
practice, and above all a public-facing culture in which the
investigation itself is the reassurance and its openness is the
product being delivered. A state whose citizens can watch it learn
does not need to ask them for trust. The Lagos crowds of June 2012,
pulling at the wreckage themselves and trusting nothing they could
not touch, were the photographic negative of that proposition, and
the distance between those two images is the remaining distance of
the Nigerian reform.

## What the report said, and what happened to it

When the final report emerged in 2017 its substance rewarded the
wait even as its tempo indicted it. The findings assembled a
textbook dual-failure narrative, tracing the fuel supply degradation
of the first engine through component histories, the crew's decision
to continue rather than divert, and the collapse of the second
engine drawing on the same system. And beneath the sequence lay the
organizational layers, meaning the maintenance decision chain for
the sick engine in the preceding days, the dispatch and pairing
questions, and the fatigue ecology of a punishing shuttle schedule.

One recommendation carried this book's entire argument inside it,
calling for measures to ensure that commercial pressure could not
override maintenance decision-making. Each finding went to a named
addressee.

Then comes the transmission question, which is the question this
book keeps asking of every document it examines. What closed? The
narrow, type-specific technical items substantially did, and the
aircraft type aged out of Nigerian service within the decade
regardless. The systemic items entered a recommendations register,
and there the public trail thins to nothing. There is no published
follow-up in which a citizen, a bereaved parent, or an underwriter
could look up whether the commercial-pressure safeguards now exist
in auditable form.

So the dead of Iju-Ishaga purchased, verifiably, some narrow
technical protections, and unverifiably, everything else. That
ratio, of the verifiable narrow to the unverifiable broad, is the
signature of the era this chapter names. Improving it requires no
new catastrophe, no new statute, and no new money. Only the
publication, on a schedule, of a register the investigator already
keeps.

## The decade the reform aged

Four dated episodes give the plateau its texture, and together they
constitute the debit column against the credit column of Chapter 9.

The first is 2013, when the reformer was removed. Weeks after a
charter aircraft crashed on takeoff at Lagos, carrying, in a detail
of almost unbearable symbolic economy, the body of a former state
governor to his own funeral, the government removed Harold Demuren
from the NCAA months from the end of his tenure. It was reflexive
accountability theater, applied to the agency whose decade had made
the crash exceptional in the first place, and the signal traveled
the system immediately, which was that tenure protection holds only
until it is tested by embarrassment. The office has turned over with
governments since. And the charter crash itself marked the reformed
perimeter's edge, involving an operator whose condition the audits
had not caught, in the sector where Nigerian aviation's oldest risks
have always lived.

The second is the currency test of 2016 and 2017. The naira
collapsed. Ticket revenue was trapped in a falling currency while
lease, spares, and insurance obligations stayed firm in dollars, and
foreign carriers' earnings were marooned in Nigerian accounts by the
hundreds of millions of dollars. The state's bad-asset manager took
the two largest carriers into care. Here the ledger must be honest
in both directions. Airlines died and their passengers did not.
There was no return of the geriatric fleet, no visible collapse of
maintenance discipline, and no accident cluster. The floors built in
2006, meaning fleet-age limits, financial surveillance, and audited
operators, held under precisely the economic pressure that had
liquefied the system of the 1980s. This is the most underappreciated
fact in modern Nigerian aviation, and it is worth stating as a
general principle. A regulatory system's quality is measured not in
good years but in what its constraints refuse to yield when
everything else is yielding.

The third is 2020 and 2021, the pandemic and the military again.
Aviation stopped almost entirely and then restarted into a world of
parked aircraft and rusty crews that the global safety community
warned was itself a hazard, and the Nigerian restart produced
incidents and no catastrophe, which is a qualified pass. In the same
season the military bill came due again, twice in three months. In
February 2021 a Nigerian Air Force turboprop went down on approach
to Abuja, killing seven. In May 2021 another went down at Kaduna in
weather, killing all aboard, including Lieutenant General Ibrahim
Attahiru, the serving Chief of Army Staff, and much of his personal
staff. Twenty-nine years after Ejigbo, an air force was investigating
itself, findings went unpublished, and a command was decapitated. A
civil reform whose writ still stopped, as it had always stopped, at
the barracks gate. No stronger proof exists that Nigeria's recovery
was a civil-institutional achievement rather than a national
transformation. The domain the reforms reached got safe. The domain
they were never permitted to reach kept its 1992 physics intact.

The fourth is 2022, when the statute was renewed. The legislative
machinery, at least, still converts lessons into law. The Civil
Aviation Act was renewed and the Accident Investigation Bureau was
reconstituted as the Nigerian Safety Investigation Bureau, with a
multimodal mandate covering aviation, rail, and marine on the
American model, which is on paper the most advanced investigative
architecture in Africa. Its early record suggests an institution
that understands it is on probation, delivering final reports at a
tempo its predecessor never sustained, publishing preliminary
statements within weeks in the international format, opening first
investigations into rail and marine occurrences, signing cooperation
agreements with foreign agencies, and offering a recorder laboratory
to the region. Whether it becomes the genuine article or merely
wears the organogram will be read, as everything in this book is
read, in its publication rate. Of all the institutions these pages
audit, it is the only one whose current decade is its best.

## The frontier that was never closed

The 2013 charter crash marked the edge of the reformed perimeter,
and the sector beyond that edge grew through the following decade
into one of the largest business-aviation markets in Africa,
comprising executive jets of the banking and oil aristocracy,
political charters in campaign seasons, and helicopter shuttles in
the delta and across Lagos traffic.

Its structural feature is the hazard sign this book has taught the
reader to recognize. Its passengers are the most powerful people in
the country, and its oversight is therefore the most negotiated
space in the system. The recurring findings in the investigative
dockets describe the pattern, which includes private registrations
flying commercial charters outside certificate discipline,
foreign-registered jets domiciled in Nigeria beyond the practical
reach of their registries, duty and load records of ceremonial
accuracy, and enforcement campaigns that crest after each
embarrassing incident and recede as the calls are returned.

Here the analytical point seeded in Chapter 6 completes itself. The
elite's exit from the public system did not exempt the powerful from
institutional quality. It re-exposed them to it, because private
oversight is exactly as strong as the public regulator standing
behind it, and the private sphere is where that regulator is
weakest. The powerful cannot secede from the competence of their own
state. They can only choose which of its jurisdictions, the audited
or the negotiated, to entrust their families to. The record of their
choosing is an irony this book leaves, without further comment, to
its readers among them.

## What a plateau looks like

The honest ledger of Nigerian aviation after 2012 has two columns
and both are real.

The credit column is substantial and too rarely stated. As of this
writing, Nigerian scheduled airlines have gone well over a decade
without a passenger-jet catastrophe, which is the longest such run
in the country's history, sustained across a doubling of traffic, a
currency collapse worse than the 1980s original, an insurgency, a
pandemic, and the failure of several carriers. Nigerians under
twenty-five have no memory of the annual mass funeral that
structured their parents' relationship to the departure gate. By the
only measure that finally matters, the reform worked and kept
working.

The debit column is this chapter, consisting of five-year reports,
suspension and restoration cycles that read cumulatively as
oversight by oscillation, a charter periphery under visibly thinner
scrutiny, a military sector untouched and repeating, and a training
college and an inspectorate straining to hold technical staff
against Gulf salaries. And beneath all of it lies the item that
Chapter 9 predicted, which is the evaporation of the political
constituency that 2005 and 2006 briefly created.

The system is no longer decaying toward 1996. It is plateaued,
maintained by professional inertia, audit exposure, and
institutional memory rather than by any active political demand.
Plateaus are respectable. But aviation is a domain of moving
baselines, because traffic grows, fleets age, and people churn, and
surveillance is a coverage function whose requirements scale with
movements. Which produces the finding that closes this Part. A
safety system standing still is, relative to its risk base, walking
slowly backward. The fragile peace is peace. It is also fragile, and
it knows it.

## After

Part II closes where it opened, with fire in a place fire should not
be.

Between Jeddah in 1991 and Iju-Ishaga in 2012 stretch seven
disasters, some three thousand dead across scheduled, charter, and
military flying, and a single demonstrated theorem. Nothing about
the dying was necessary. The same state, the same market, and the
same professionals, rearranged after 2006 into institutions with
money, autonomy, and witnesses, stopped producing catastrophe within
eighteen months and have not resumed.

The crashes were never fate. They were policy, in the strict sense,
being the predictable output of arrangements that were chosen,
renewed, and defensible at every point by someone whose interests
they served.

What remains, before this book can argue about the future, is to
understand those arrangements themselves, not crash by crash but
pattern by pattern. The regulator's political economy. The economics
that made cutting corners rational. The help that never came. And
the silence that made every lesson single-use. That anatomy is Part
III, and its shortest summary can be given in advance. Institutions
are how a society remembers, and Nigerian aviation history is what
forgetting costs.
