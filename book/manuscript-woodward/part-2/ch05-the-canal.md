# 5. The Canal

## Ejigbo, Lagos, September 26, 1992, late afternoon

They heard it before they saw it.

A four-engined drone gone wrong. Too low. Laboring.

Then the shape of it crossed the rooflines — a grey military
transport, close enough that people in the street would later argue
about whether they could read the lettering — and it went into the
canal that threads the swamp at the edge of the district, and the
water closed over it.

The aircraft was NAF 911, a Lockheed C-130H Hercules of the Nigerian
Air Force. It had been airborne for about three minutes from the
military side of Murtala Muhammed Airport, bound for Kaduna and the
Command and Staff College at Jaji.

The cabin had been configured for a troop lift, and it was full.

Almost everyone aboard was an officer in the middle of a career:
captains, majors, lieutenant-colonels — the cohort selected for the
staff course, the pool from which a nation draws its future service
chiefs. There were foreign officers from allied African armies.
There were a small number of civilians.

The passenger list, in the national habit this book has already
established, would prove approximate. The figure the record
eventually settled on was about 158 dead. Some counts run higher.

It remains one of the worst military air disasters in history,
anywhere.

For the first hour — the hour in which anyone might conceivably have
been saved — the entire response of the Nigerian state consisted of
the people of Ejigbo.

## What the neighbors did

They went in with rope.

The fuselage had driven into water and deep alluvial mud, which
sealed over it. Local men waded and swam; boys dove into black water
opaque with fuel and silt; people who had been selling in the market
an hour earlier organized a feeding line for the divers that ran for
days.

The recovery took most of a week and defeated every asset the state
eventually sent. Heavy equipment could not reach the site. Bodies
came out one and two at a time and were laid along the bank under
mats while the city watched. Refrigeration failed or never arrived.
Identification proceeded by effects — a wallet, a course notebook, a
wristwatch — in the Nigeria of 1992, without DNA, with results some
families dispute to this day.

The armed forces buried some of their dead collectively.

For an institution whose entire ceremonial vocabulary presumes the
named individual grave, the collective grave was a confession no
communiqué could unsay.

And when the military cordon finally consolidated, its first
sustained operational act was to push the neighborhood back from the
water it had been working since the first afternoon.

"They came to take the place from us," one Ejigbo man told a
reporter that week, in words that the district still repeats. "Not
to help. To take it."

The uniformed state, at its own disaster, managed witnesses first
and victims second.

Trust is a ledger. The week at the canal posted a debit that the
Nigerian military's domestic reputation carries still.

## The soft crash

There is a detail about Ejigbo that circulated through the military
community in the weeks afterward and has never left it.

The crash site was soft. Water and deep mud. The airframe did not
disintegrate on impact the way an aircraft striking hard ground
disintegrates; it went under substantially intact.

Which means that for some unknowable number of the people aboard,
death was not instantaneous.

That fact — unbearable, undocumented, and impossible to confirm or
refute in the absence of a published investigation — did as much as
anything else to poison the army's trust in its own institution's
account of the event.

It also illustrates something this book will meet repeatedly. In the
absence of an authoritative report, the worst available
interpretation becomes the operative one, and stays operative
forever.

## What was never published

A board of inquiry was convened, as regulations required.

It gathered evidence. It interviewed. It reported — upward, into the
world of military intelligence and the presidency of General Ibrahim
Babangida.

And out of history.

No findings were ever published. No summary was ever gazetted. More
than thirty years later, no Nigerian citizen can read why 158
officers died in a canal at the edge of Lagos.

Where a report should stand, there are theories. Each implies a
different institutional felony and a different set of the
still-living responsible.

**Fuel contamination** from the military fuel farm — in which case
every aircraft that fueled there the following morning flew the same
roulette.

**Maintenance failure** under the spares famine that the previous
chapter described in detail — in which case every stretched engine
in the fleet kept flying its extension.

**Overloading** — the chronic sin of military airlift everywhere,
and the one most easily concealed by the absence of a load sheet.

By choosing silence, the state chose to protect all three
possibilities equally.

It also chose to leave whatever killed NAF 911 in service.

## THE ARGUMENT: What an unpublished report costs

"Publish the report" can sound like a proceduralist's piety, so it
is worth being precise about the mechanism.

An accident investigation is a machine for converting the dead into
protection for the living.

Its findings reallocate budgets. They condemn fuel farms. They
ground engine fleets. They rewrite checklists and loading
procedures. They indict supply chains. Each of those is a physical
change in the world, and each is caused by a document.

An unpublished investigation converts the dead into nothing.

Now consider what a published report would have contained, because
constructing its table of contents shows exactly what the public
was denied.

A **factual section**: the maintenance history of the aircraft, hours
since overhaul on every engine, every deferred defect, the fuel
uplift records and the fuel farm's quality-control documentation for
that week, the crew's training currency, the load manifest
reconciled against the recovery.

Merely listing those items reveals the problem. Every one of them is
a question that some institution preferred permanently unanswered to
temporarily embarrassing.

An **analysis section**: engine teardowns. This is not speculative.
The canal is not the ocean; the engines were recoverable.
Metallurgical examination of turbine and propeller witness marks
distinguishes, to a near-certainty, an engine failing under power
from one that was already dead. The laboratories capable of that
work existed in 1992 in a dozen countries and were available to
Nigeria through the mutual-assistance provisions of the Chicago
system.

The central question of Ejigbo was answerable.

It was simply not asked anywhere the public could hear.

A **recommendations section**: addressed to named institutions with
dated expectations. Certify the fuel farm. Inspect the fleet.
Discipline the loading. Staff the airworthiness review.

Chapter 10 of this book records what the Nigerian Air Force's
transport and utility fleets did over the following three decades.

Every subsequent loss occurred in the
recommendation-shaped hole where that section should have been.

## THE ARGUMENT: What secrecy actually withholds

Secrecy is usually defended as the protection of specific sensitive
facts.

Examine Ejigbo and you find that is not what it did.

The facts leaked anyway. Facts always do. Lagos knew within weeks
what the divers had found. The mess halls knew what the engines had
sounded like. The theories listed above circulated freely through
the officer corps and the press, in some cases within days.

What never existed was the *structure*.

The disciplined, cross-examined, permanent organization of knowledge
that converts an event into an inheritance.

Rumor, however accurate, cannot ground an airworthiness directive.
You cannot condemn a fuel farm on the basis of what everyone
believes. You cannot rewrite a loading procedure because of what the
mess hall says.

So state the general principle, because it applies to every
information-suppressing institution:

*States that choose secrecy are not choosing which facts circulate.
They are choosing that facts shall circulate uselessly.*

The information escapes. Only the *authority* is withheld — and
authority is the component that makes information actionable.

## THE ECONOMICS: The exemption from every market

Everything this book argues about civil aviation applies doubled to
military aviation, for a structural reason most countries discover
the hard way.

Military flying is the only major category of aviation *designed* to
escape independent oversight.

Set out the civil apparatus and then delete it, line by line, and
you can see the size of the hole:

- No civil regulator certifies an air force's maintenance
- No treaty compels publication of its investigations
- No insurer's surveyor walks its hangars uninvited, because the
  state self-insures
- No lessor's covenants specify its maintenance program, because the
  state owns the aircraft outright
- No passenger chooses another carrier, because the passengers are
  posted
- No litigation prices its negligence, because sovereign immunity
  and military justice absorb the claims

Every one of the private disciplinary mechanisms that Chapter 12
will describe as the "institutional field" is, by design, absent.

What replaces the entire apparatus is a single mechanism:
institutional self-respect, enforced internally, by professionals
against their own hierarchy.

Where the military is a strong institution inside a strong state,
that mechanism works. The military airworthiness systems of the
serious democracies are formidable.

Where the military *is* the state, the mechanism eats itself.

The hierarchy that must be held to account owns the inquiry, the
budget, the promotion board, and the censor.

Nigeria in 1992 was the limiting case. The air force investigating
NAF 911 was investigating its own maintenance economies, authorized
by a government of generals whose budget decisions had imposed them,
under a head of state who was the hierarchy's apex.

The inquiry was not suppressed by some identifiable villain.

It was digested, harmlessly, by an institution with no external
organ to deliver its findings to.

Selves do not indict themselves.

## The British mirror

The comparison that proves this is not a Nigerian condition is
British, and it should be held up against Ejigbo deliberately.

In September 2006, a Royal Air Force Nimrod exploded over
Afghanistan, killing fourteen servicemen.

The initial board of inquiry ran the familiar internal course.

And then Britain's institutions outside the military refused to let
it rest. A coroner's inquest. A furious family campaign.
Parliamentary pressure. And finally an independent review by a
senior barrister, Charles Haddon-Cave, whose 2009 report remains the
most unsparing public autopsy of a military airworthiness system
ever written.

It named names, including senior officers. It traced the disaster to
budget cuts and a "culture of complacency." It forced the creation
of a structurally independent Military Aviation Authority.

The comparison is not that the Royal Air Force was better. The whole
point of the Nimrod report was that its airworthiness system had
rotted.

The comparison is that Britain possessed external institutions —
courts, coroners, Parliament, press — with the standing to crack the
military's informational shell, and Nigeria in 1992 possessed none.

The lesson generalizes cleanly, and it is one of the most
transferable findings in this book:

*Military aviation safety is a function of civilian institutional
strength.*

Nigeria would test that proposition again in February and May of
2021, with results recorded in Chapter 10.

## THE NUMBERS: The generation, audited

The claim that Ejigbo destroyed a generation of Nigerian military
leadership is repeated so often in national commentary that it
deserves the audit this book applies to every inherited sentence.

What was verifiably aboard was a cross-section of an institution's
investment in its own future: officers of all three services in the
captain-to-colonel band, several instructors, allied students from
other African armies.

A senior staff course roll is, by construction, a forecast of the
establishment fifteen years out. Passage through the staff college
is the credential that separates officers who will administer a
military from officers who will command it.

So the loss was not a unit. It was a cohort. The nearest civilian
analogy is a country losing an entire graduating class of its best
medical school in a single afternoon.

What is *not* auditable is the counterfactual that Nigerian
conversation has built on that grave: the reforming service chiefs
who would have emerged, the coups not attempted, the professionalism
preserved.

Honesty requires the harder observation.

The officers of NAF 911 were products of the same institution whose
decay killed them. Had they lived, they would have risen through the
same politicized promotion boards, the same budget triage, the same
culture of the sealed report. Some of them would have been the
eulogists at the 2021 funerals rather than the occupants of the
caskets.

No roster of individual quality has ever, in the evidence of this
book, outperformed institutional design.

The truest thing mourned at Jaji was not the future that cohort
would have built.

It was a system that could waste them twice — once in the canal, and
once in the filing cabinet where the explanation went.

## The hinge year

Zoom out, because the crash and the country were the same story that
autumn.

Nigeria in 1992 stood at the fevered climax of the Babangida
transition: the endlessly postponed handover to civilian rule, a
newly created two-party system running its primaries, an economy in
the deepest trough of structural adjustment, and a military — which
owned both the state and the crashed aircraft — split between those
who intended to leave power and those who intended to keep it.

The dead of Ejigbo fell into that argument.

Within eighteen months, the regime annulled the freest election in
Nigerian history and collapsed into the darkness of the Abacha
years.

Historians of the period treat the sequence as compounding. An
institution already hollowed by two decades of governance duty lost
a staff college's worth of its future, and then its remaining
political legitimacy, in the space of a year.

And both events were processed identically.

Sealed.

A state that could not explain its dead officers to its own army was
never going to explain an annulled election to its own people.

The filing cabinet at Ejigbo and the one at the electoral commission
were the same piece of furniture.

## THE ARGUMENT: Who is owed an explanation

There is a question underneath this chapter that is worth making
explicit, because it recurs at every subsequent disaster and it is
genuinely philosophical rather than technical.

What, exactly, does a state owe the people its systems kill?

The minimal answer — the one most governments in this book acted on
— is: burial with honor, condolence, and such compensation as the
law prescribes. Nigeria delivered all three at Ejigbo, and delivered
the first with real magnificence.

But there is a stronger claim available, and it rests on the nature
of the relationship rather than on sentiment.

The men aboard NAF 911 did not choose that aircraft. They were
*posted* to it. They had surrendered, as soldiers do, the ordinary
civilian right to decline a risk they judged unreasonable. The state
had taken that right from them and had, in exchange, assumed
responsibility for exercising the judgment on their behalf.

When a party assumes another's power of refusal, it acquires a
corresponding duty: to explain how the judgment was exercised, and
to demonstrate that the same judgment will not kill the next cohort.

That duty is not discharged by a funeral. A funeral honors the dead.
Only an explanation protects the living.

And notice the asymmetry that makes silence so tempting. The dead
cannot demand the account. The people it would protect do not yet
know they need it. The only party with both the knowledge and the
motive to withhold it is the institution that would be indicted by
it.

This is why the duty has to be *structural* — vested in an
independent body, with statutory deadlines and mandatory publication
— rather than left to the good conscience of the implicated.

Every reform Part IV of this book recommends is, at bottom, an
attempt to build the mechanism that Ejigbo lacked: an organ capable
of delivering an explanation that the institution itself would
prefer not to give.

## The lake in the memory of the city

The state built no memorial at Ejigbo.

The city built its own. The canal was renamed by grief. The district
sustained anniversary observances for decades, unfunded. The
conviction hardened over time — from suspicion into simple civic
common sense — that the truth had been known and withheld.

When newspapers marked the round-number anniversaries with
investigative retrospectives, their sources were the memory bank the
community had maintained: survivors of the recovery, retired
officers speaking at last, families' preserved papers.

The state's archive of Ejigbo is a closed file of unknown contents.

The city's archive is live, growing, and public.

A historian of the next century will reconstruct NAF 911 from the
community's record, not the government's.

That inversion — a society whose informal memory outperforms its
official memory — may be the most Nigerian finding in this book. It
indicts the state. It honors the people the state declined to
inform.

## After

The proper mourning for the dead of NAF 911 would have been the
boring kind: a published report, a condemned fuel system, a funded
spares pipeline, an air force answerable to something outside
itself.

The country chose the eloquent kind — flags, volleys, marble at
Jaji.

The distance between those two kinds of mourning, compounded across
every disaster in this book, is a fair measure of the distance
between the state Nigeria performs and the state it needs.

Four years later, and about a mile south of the canal as the crow
flies, the failure would wear a different uniform.

Not military secrecy this time.

A radar that had not worked in years, and a report that — for once —
would actually exist.
