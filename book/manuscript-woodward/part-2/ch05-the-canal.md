# 5. The Canal

## Ejigbo, Lagos, September 26, 1992, late afternoon

They heard it before they saw it. A four-engined drone gone wrong,
too low and laboring. Then the shape of it crossed the rooflines, a
grey military transport close enough that people in the street would
later argue about whether they could read the lettering, and it went
into the canal that threads the swamp at the edge of the district,
and the water closed over it.

The aircraft was NAF 911, a Lockheed C-130H Hercules of the Nigerian
Air Force. It had been airborne for about three minutes from the
military side of Murtala Muhammed Airport, bound for Kaduna and the
Command and Staff College at Jaji. The cabin had been configured for
a troop lift and it was full.

Almost everyone aboard was an officer in the middle of a career,
which meant captains, majors, and lieutenant-colonels, the cohort
selected for the staff course and the pool from which a nation draws
its future service chiefs. There were foreign officers from allied
African armies and there were a small number of civilians. The
passenger list, in the national habit this book has already
established, would prove approximate. The figure the record
eventually settled on was about 158 dead, and some counts run
higher. It remains one of the worst military air disasters in
history, anywhere.

For the first hour, which was the hour in which anyone might
conceivably have been saved, the entire response of the Nigerian
state consisted of the people of Ejigbo.

## What the neighbors did

They went in with rope.

The fuselage had driven into water and deep alluvial mud, which
sealed over it. Local men waded and swam, boys dove into black water
opaque with fuel and silt, and people who had been selling in the
market an hour earlier organized a feeding line for the divers that
ran for days.

The recovery took most of a week and defeated every asset the state
eventually sent. Heavy equipment could not reach the site. Bodies
came out one and two at a time and were laid along the bank under
mats while the city watched. Refrigeration failed or never arrived.
Identification proceeded by effects, a wallet or a course notebook
or a wristwatch, in the Nigeria of 1992, without DNA, with results
that some families dispute to this day.

The armed forces buried some of their dead collectively. For an
institution whose entire ceremonial vocabulary presumes the named
individual grave, the collective grave was a confession no communiqué
could unsay.

And when the military cordon finally consolidated, its first
sustained operational act was to push the neighborhood back from the
water it had been working since the first afternoon.

"They came to take the place from us," one Ejigbo man told a
reporter that week, in words that the district still repeats. "Not
to help. To take it."

The uniformed state, at its own disaster, managed witnesses first
and victims second. Trust is a ledger, and the week at the canal
posted a debit that the Nigerian military's domestic reputation
carries still.

## The soft crash

There is a detail about Ejigbo that circulated through the military
community in the weeks afterward and has never left it.

The crash site was soft, being water and deep mud, and the airframe
did not disintegrate on impact the way an aircraft striking hard
ground disintegrates. It went under substantially intact, which
means that for some unknowable number of the people aboard, death
was not instantaneous.

That fact is unbearable, undocumented, and impossible to confirm or
refute in the absence of a published investigation, and it did as
much as anything else to poison the army's trust in its own
institution's account of the event. It also illustrates something
this book will meet repeatedly. In the absence of an authoritative
report, the worst available interpretation becomes the operative
one, and stays operative forever.

## What was never published

A board of inquiry was convened, as regulations required. It
gathered evidence, it interviewed, and it reported upward into the
world of military intelligence and the presidency of General Ibrahim
Babangida, and out of history.

No findings were ever published. No summary was ever gazetted. More
than thirty years later, no Nigerian citizen can read why 158
officers died in a canal at the edge of Lagos.

Where a report should stand there are theories, and each implies a
different institutional felony and a different set of the
still-living responsible. Fuel contamination from the military fuel
farm, in which case every aircraft that fueled there the following
morning flew the same roulette. Maintenance failure under the spares
famine that the previous chapter described in detail, in which case
every stretched engine in the fleet kept flying its extension.
Overloading, the chronic sin of military airlift everywhere and the
one most easily concealed by the absence of a load sheet.

By choosing silence the state chose to protect all three
possibilities equally. It also chose to leave whatever killed NAF
911 in service.

## What an unpublished report costs

The instruction to publish the report can sound like a
proceduralist's piety, so it is worth being precise about the
mechanism.

An accident investigation is a machine for converting the dead into
protection for the living. Its findings reallocate budgets, condemn
fuel farms, ground engine fleets, rewrite checklists and loading
procedures, and indict supply chains. Each of those is a physical
change in the world, and each is caused by a document. An
unpublished investigation converts the dead into nothing.

Now consider what a published report would have contained, because
constructing its table of contents shows exactly what the public was
denied. The factual section would have set out the maintenance
history of the aircraft, the hours since overhaul on every engine,
every deferred defect, the fuel uplift records and the fuel farm's
quality-control documentation for that week, the crew's training
currency, and the load manifest reconciled against the recovery.
Merely listing those items reveals the problem, because every one of
them is a question that some institution preferred permanently
unanswered to temporarily embarrassing.

The analysis section would have rested on engine teardowns, and this
is not speculative. The canal is not the ocean and the engines were
recoverable. Metallurgical examination of turbine and propeller
witness marks distinguishes, to a near-certainty, an engine failing
under power from one that was already dead. The laboratories capable
of that work existed in 1992 in a dozen countries and were available
to Nigeria through the mutual-assistance provisions of the Chicago
system. The central question of Ejigbo was answerable. It was simply
not asked anywhere the public could hear.

The recommendations section would have been addressed to named
institutions with dated expectations. Certify the fuel farm. Inspect
the fleet. Discipline the loading. Staff the airworthiness review.
Chapter 10 of this book records what the Nigerian Air Force's
transport and utility fleets did over the following three decades,
and every subsequent loss occurred in the recommendation-shaped hole
where that section should have been.

## What secrecy actually withholds

Secrecy is usually defended as the protection of specific sensitive
facts. Examine Ejigbo and you find that is not what it did.

The facts leaked anyway, because facts always do. Lagos knew within
weeks what the divers had found. The mess halls knew what the
engines had sounded like. The theories listed above circulated
freely through the officer corps and the press, in some cases within
days.

What never existed was the structure, meaning the disciplined,
cross-examined, permanent organization of knowledge that converts an
event into an inheritance. Rumor, however accurate, cannot ground an
airworthiness directive. You cannot condemn a fuel farm on the basis
of what everyone believes, and you cannot rewrite a loading
procedure because of what the mess hall says.

So state the general principle, because it applies to every
information-suppressing institution. States that choose secrecy are
not choosing which facts circulate. They are choosing that facts
shall circulate uselessly. The information escapes, and only the
authority is withheld, and authority is the component that makes
information actionable.

## The exemption from every market

Everything this book argues about civil aviation applies doubled to
military aviation, for a structural reason most countries discover
the hard way. Military flying is the only major category of aviation
designed to escape independent oversight.

Set out the civil apparatus and then delete it, line by line, and
you can see the size of the hole. No civil regulator certifies an
air force's maintenance. No treaty compels publication of its
investigations. No insurer's surveyor walks its hangars uninvited,
because the state self-insures. No lessor's covenants specify its
maintenance program, because the state owns the aircraft outright.
No passenger chooses another carrier, because the passengers are
posted. No litigation prices its negligence, because sovereign
immunity and military justice absorb the claims. Every one of the
six private disciplinary mechanisms that Chapter 12 will describe as
the institutional field is, by design, absent.

What replaces the entire apparatus is a single mechanism, which is
institutional self-respect, enforced internally by professionals
against their own hierarchy. Where the military is a strong
institution inside a strong state, that mechanism works, and the
military airworthiness systems of the serious democracies are
formidable. Where the military is the state, the mechanism eats
itself, because the hierarchy that must be held to account owns the
inquiry, the budget, the promotion board, and the censor.

Nigeria in 1992 was the limiting case. The air force investigating
NAF 911 was investigating its own maintenance economies, authorized
by a government of generals whose budget decisions had imposed them,
under a head of state who was the hierarchy's apex. The inquiry was
not suppressed by some identifiable villain. It was digested,
harmlessly, by an institution with no external organ to deliver its
findings to. Selves do not indict themselves.

## The British mirror

The comparison that proves this is not a Nigerian condition is
British, and it should be held up against Ejigbo deliberately.

In September 2006 a Royal Air Force Nimrod exploded over
Afghanistan, killing fourteen servicemen. The initial board of
inquiry ran the familiar internal course, and then Britain's
institutions outside the military refused to let it rest. There was
a coroner's inquest, a furious family campaign, and parliamentary
pressure, and finally an independent review by a senior barrister,
Charles Haddon-Cave, whose 2009 report remains the most unsparing
public autopsy of a military airworthiness system ever written. It
named names, including senior officers. It traced the disaster to
budget cuts and a culture of complacency. It forced the creation of
a structurally independent Military Aviation Authority.

The comparison is not that the Royal Air Force was better, since the
whole point of the Nimrod report was that its airworthiness system
had rotted. The comparison is that Britain possessed external
institutions in the form of courts, coroners, Parliament, and press,
all with the standing to crack the military's informational shell,
and Nigeria in 1992 possessed none of them.

The lesson generalizes cleanly and it is one of the most
transferable findings in this book. Military aviation safety is a
function of civilian institutional strength. Nigeria would test that
proposition again in February and May of 2021, with results recorded
in Chapter 10.

## The generation, audited

The claim that Ejigbo destroyed a generation of Nigerian military
leadership is repeated so often in national commentary that it
deserves the audit this book applies to every inherited sentence.

What was verifiably aboard was a cross-section of an institution's
investment in its own future, consisting of officers of all three
services in the captain-to-colonel band, several instructors, and
allied students from other African armies. A senior staff course
roll is, by construction, a forecast of the establishment fifteen
years out, because passage through the staff college is the
credential that separates officers who will administer a military
from officers who will command it. So the loss was not a unit. It
was a cohort, and the nearest civilian analogy is a country losing
an entire graduating class of its best medical school in a single
afternoon.

What is not auditable is the counterfactual that Nigerian
conversation has built on that grave, meaning the reforming service
chiefs who would have emerged, the coups not attempted, and the
professionalism preserved. Honesty requires the harder observation.
The officers of NAF 911 were products of the same institution whose
decay killed them. Had they lived, they would have risen through the
same politicized promotion boards, the same budget triage, and the
same culture of the sealed report, and some of them would have been
the eulogists at the 2021 funerals rather than the occupants of the
caskets. No roster of individual quality has ever, in the evidence
of this book, outperformed institutional design.

The truest thing mourned at Jaji was not the future that cohort
would have built. It was a system that could waste them twice, once
in the canal and once in the filing cabinet where the explanation
went.

## The hinge year

Zoom out, because the crash and the country were the same story that
autumn.

Nigeria in 1992 stood at the fevered climax of the Babangida
transition, with its endlessly postponed handover to civilian rule,
a newly created two-party system running its primaries, an economy
in the deepest trough of structural adjustment, and a military that
owned both the state and the crashed aircraft split between those
who intended to leave power and those who intended to keep it. The
dead of Ejigbo fell into that argument.

Within eighteen months the regime annulled the freest election in
Nigerian history and collapsed into the darkness of the Abacha
years. Historians of the period treat the sequence as compounding.
An institution already hollowed by two decades of governance duty
lost a staff college's worth of its future, and then its remaining
political legitimacy, in the space of a year.

Both events were processed identically. Sealed. A state that could
not explain its dead officers to its own army was never going to
explain an annulled election to its own people. The filing cabinet
at Ejigbo and the one at the electoral commission were the same
piece of furniture.

## Who is owed an explanation

There is a question underneath this chapter that is worth making
explicit, because it recurs at every subsequent disaster and it is
genuinely philosophical rather than technical. What, exactly, does a
state owe the people its systems kill?

The minimal answer, which is the one most governments in this book
acted on, is burial with honor, condolence, and such compensation as
the law prescribes. Nigeria delivered all three at Ejigbo, and
delivered the first with real magnificence.

But there is a stronger claim available, and it rests on the nature
of the relationship rather than on sentiment. The men aboard NAF 911
did not choose that aircraft. They were posted to it. They had
surrendered, as soldiers do, the ordinary civilian right to decline
a risk they judged unreasonable, and the state had taken that right
from them and had, in exchange, assumed responsibility for
exercising the judgment on their behalf. When a party assumes
another's power of refusal, it acquires a corresponding duty, which
is to explain how the judgment was exercised and to demonstrate that
the same judgment will not kill the next cohort. That duty is not
discharged by a funeral. A funeral honors the dead, and only an
explanation protects the living.

Notice the asymmetry that makes silence so tempting. The dead cannot
demand the account. The people it would protect do not yet know they
need it. The only party with both the knowledge and the motive to
withhold it is the institution that would be indicted by it. This is
why the duty has to be structural, vested in an independent body
with statutory deadlines and mandatory publication, rather than left
to the good conscience of the implicated. Every reform Part IV of
this book recommends is, at bottom, an attempt to build the
mechanism that Ejigbo lacked, which is an organ capable of
delivering an explanation that the institution itself would prefer
not to give.

## The lake in the memory of the city

The state built no memorial at Ejigbo. The city built its own. The
canal was renamed by grief. The district sustained anniversary
observances for decades, unfunded. The conviction hardened over
time, moving from suspicion into simple civic common sense, that the
truth had been known and withheld.

When newspapers marked the round-number anniversaries with
investigative retrospectives, their sources were the memory bank the
community had maintained, meaning survivors of the recovery, retired
officers speaking at last, and families' preserved papers. The
state's archive of Ejigbo is a closed file of unknown contents. The
city's archive is live, growing, and public, and a historian of the
next century will reconstruct NAF 911 from the community's record
rather than the government's.

That inversion, in which a society's informal memory outperforms its
official memory, may be the most Nigerian finding in this book. It
indicts the state and it honors the people the state declined to
inform.

## After

The proper mourning for the dead of NAF 911 would have been the
boring kind, consisting of a published report, a condemned fuel
system, a funded spares pipeline, and an air force answerable to
something outside itself. The country chose the eloquent kind, which
was flags, volleys, and marble at Jaji. The distance between those
two kinds of mourning, compounded across every disaster in this
book, is a fair measure of the distance between the state Nigeria
performs and the state it needs.

Four years later, and about a mile south of the canal as the crow
flies, the failure would wear a different uniform. Not military
secrecy this time, but a radar that had not worked in years, and a
report that for once would actually exist.
