# 8. The Year of Weeping

## Lagos, Saturday, October 22, 2005, 20:45

Bellview Airlines Flight 210 lifted off from Murtala Muhammed
International Airport for Abuja with 117 people aboard, into a line
of thunderstorms that everyone at the field could see. Three minutes
later it was gone from the radio.

And then something happened that had no precedent even in the
history recorded in this book. For twelve hours, the Federal
Republic of Nigeria could not find a Boeing 737 that had fallen
within thirty kilometers of its largest city.

## What the state knew, and could not assemble

At the moment contact was lost the system possessed, distributed
across several offices, everything required to draw a small circle
on a map. A departure time. A cleared route. A last radio call. A
fuel load. An aircraft performance envelope. The circle of
possibility was not large. What the system did with that information
was nothing coherent.

The airline reported the aircraft missing. The night-duty layers of
the agencies activated as separate organisms, with aviation, the
military, the police, and the young National Emergency Management
Agency each calling its own chain and none commanding the others. No
joint rescue coordination center took charge of the picture, because
there was no doctrine assigning one and no exercised protocol for
who would.

Into that vacuum came the era's new information ecology of mobile
phones and morning drive-time radio. A report whose origin was never
satisfactorily traced placed the crash near Kishi, in Oyo State,
more than a hundred kilometers north. Officials repeated it, the
machinery lurched toward it, and families were told. Some began
driving. For much of that night the Republic searched the wrong
forest at the wrong end of the country, while the actual wreckage
lay under canopy at Lisa, in Ogun State, thirty kilometers from the
runway the aircraft had left.

The farmers of Lisa found it at first light, walking toward smoke
that the government's aircraft had flown past. A crater in the
forest. The 737 driven into the earth almost vertically. No
survivors. And for several hours more, custody of the site belonged
to whoever arrived, first villagers, then journalists, then a
gathering crowd, then progressively the state, reaching its own
catastrophe behind the general public.

## The counterfactual that gives the night its weight

Everyone aboard Flight 210 was killed at impact, and the forensic
evidence, such as it was, established that beyond argument. So a
certain kind of official response, one that was in fact made at the
time, holds that the twelve hours cost nothing. Nobody could have
been saved, and the search failure was embarrassing rather than
lethal.

This reasoning is exactly backwards, and understanding why is
essential to everything Chapter 13 will argue. Search and rescue is
insurance. Its value is not determined by the outcome of any single
event but by its expected performance across the whole class of
events. Nobody in the operations rooms that night knew that Lisa was
unsurvivable, and they could not have known. A large fraction of
transport aircraft accidents are survivable or partially survivable,
including runway excursions, undershoots, and controlled descents
into terrain at low energy, and in those cases the interval between
impact and professional arrival is the interval in which the injured
die of things that are treatable.

The system that took twelve hours to locate Flight 210 would have
taken twelve hours to reach a survivable crash. The lottery that
night was drawn in a way that concealed the failure, and it could as
easily have drawn the other way. You cannot judge an insurance
policy by whether the house happened to burn.

## Loyola Jesuit College, Abuja, December 2005

The school on the Abuja plain was the reconstruction of the Nigerian
professional class in miniature. Founded in 1996 and run by Jesuits,
it had become within a decade one of the academically fiercest
institutions in the country, and its families were civil servants
and doctors, officers and entrepreneurs, north and south, Muslim and
Christian.

At the end of the term, its students flew home. This is a structural
fact about Nigeria that requires explanation for foreign readers,
because it converted a terrible chance into a systemic finding.
Nigeria is a boarding-school nation, a legacy of colonial education
reinforced by the federal unity schools that deliberately mix the
regions and by a professional class dispersed across a federation
whose distances defeat weekend parenting. So at every term's turning
thousands of minors cross the country in concentrated waves, on
predictable dates, on the school run, with seats booked in blocks
and younger children chaperoned by older ones. And parents funded
those flights precisely as a safety purchase, because the flight was
judged, entirely reasonably, safer than the highway.

On December 10, 2005, sixty students of Loyola Jesuit College
boarded Sosoliso Airlines Flight 1145 at Abuja for Port Harcourt.

## Port Harcourt, December 10, 2005, afternoon

The DC-9 arrived over its destination in heavy weather. On approach,
in a thunderstorm, the aircraft encountered windshear, the sudden
and violent reversal of wind near the ground that Chapter 9 will
describe in full. It struck the ground short of and beside the
runway, broke up along the grass, and burned.

The impact was survivable for many aboard. And then the second
disaster began. The airport's rescue and firefighting service
reached the burning wreck late, and it reached it, by the convergent
testimony of survivors' families and subsequent inquiry, without
adequate water or foam. Passengers who had lived through the impact
died in the fire.

One hundred and eight people were lost. Two survived, both terribly
burned. One of them was a teenage girl named Kechi Okwuchi, whose
years of reconstructive surgery abroad, and whose eventual
appearance singing on an American television stage, would become the
tragedy's one publicly bearable thread. Her account of that
afternoon, in which the impact was survivable and the flames arrived
before help, has been given many times on the public record and has
done more than any official document to lodge one detail permanently
in the national memory. The fire trucks came without water.

Sixty children of one school. A headmaster reading names at a carol
service that had become a funeral. A mass grave on the school
grounds, because some of the children could not be told apart.

## What three minutes buys

The international standard for airport firefighting is not
aspirational garnish. It is calibrated to physics, and the
calibration deserves to be spelled out, because it converts a
sentence about dry tenders into a body count.

ICAO's Annex 14 specifies, for an airport of Port Harcourt's traffic
category, response to any point of the runway within about three
minutes, delivering rated quantities of water and foam. Why three?
Because of what happens inside an intact fuselage adjacent to a
kerosene fire. An aircraft cabin is a sealed tube of aluminum
containing a large quantity of flammable furnishings and a limited
quantity of breathable air. An external fuel fire heats the skin,
ignites the interior, and produces, before flame ever reaches a
passenger, smoke and toxic gases in concentrations that incapacitate
within tens of seconds and kill within a few minutes. The survivable
interval for an intact cabin adjacent to a fuel fire is measured in
low single-digit minutes, and that is the number the standard is
built around.

Which means the difference between a two-minute response and a
ten-minute response is not eight minutes of inconvenience. It is
everyone the fire reaches second.

Here is the detail that turns description into indictment. Every
airport in this book's ledger was certified, on paper, at a
firefighting category adequate to its traffic. The vocabulary of the
international standard had been purchased, the trucks had been
procured, at least some of them, the categories had been declared,
and the boxes had been inked. What had not been purchased was the
substance, meaning agent stocks, pump maintenance, access-road
planning, and above all the drills that convert equipment into
capability. Call it certification as costume. It is the purest
expression of the pathology this book has tracked since the empty
regulatory housing of Chapter 3, and at Port Harcourt the costume
met its only honest audience.

## Who bears the risk of a system nobody drills

There is a specific injustice in the Sosoliso response that goes
beyond incompetence, and it is worth stating carefully. The
passengers of Flight 1145 had, in a meaningful sense, paid for the
fire service. Airport charges are levied on every ticket, and a
portion of what those parents spent to fly their children home was,
by the design of aviation finance, a payment for rescue capability
at the destination. They bought an insurance policy embedded in the
fare. The policy was not in force.

This is different from the failures of Chapters 4 through 7, and the
difference matters. Those were failures to prevent, meaning
deferrals in a system whose participants might plausibly claim they
were unaware of the accumulated risk. This was a failure to deliver
a service that had been charged for, in a facility that had been
certified as delivering it, to people who had no way of auditing the
claim and no alternative supplier. In any other market that is
called fraud. In aviation it was called a category declaration.

## The mothers of Loyola

What the Loyola families did next distinguishes 2005 from every
prior chapter in this book, and it began with a decision whose
importance is easy to miss. They organized as the school community
rather than as scattered claimants.

Read that sentence again, because it is the mechanism. Every
previous group of Nigerian air-crash families had faced the state as
individuals, a widow here and an orphaned family there, each
negotiating separately with an insurer, each grieving privately, and
each dispersing when the news cycle moved on. Their grief was
enormous and it was politically weightless, because it was atomized.

The Loyola parents were, by tragic accident, the coalition that
Nigerian aviation safety had never had. They were numerous enough to
matter. They were concentrated enough to organize, being one school,
one city, one parish network, and one set of class lists. They were
elite enough to be unignorable, since their ranks included senior
lawyers who understood process, officials who understood ministries,
and executives who understood leverage. And they were armed with the
single grievance that no Nigerian government could wave away, which
was children.

They formed associations and retained counsel. They attended every
adjournment of every proceeding. They built memorial scholarship
funds. They demanded the Sosoliso report and got it. They pressed
the firefighting findings into the public record. They turned each
anniversary into an audit. And they refused, year after year, to
allow the customary amnesia. Organized, articulate, unignorable
grief is the only force in this entire history that ever outlasted a
news cycle.

## The economics of political attention

This is the place to state the general theory that the rest of the
book depends on, because 2005 is where the theory is demonstrated.
Why do institutions that everyone knows are failing continue to
fail?

The standard answer of corruption, incompetence, and culture is
inadequate, because the same institutions, with the same personnel,
sometimes stop failing very quickly, and Nigeria's aviation
regulator did precisely that within eighteen months of this chapter.
The better answer is about the structure of costs and benefits
across time and across groups.

The benefits of neglect are concentrated, immediate, and delivered
to organized parties, meaning the ministry that keeps the levy
money, the airline that avoids the check, the official who grants
the waiver, and the treasury that defers the radar. The costs of
neglect are diffuse, delayed, and probabilistic, and they fall on a
statistical population of future passengers who do not know they are
the population and cannot organize as one.

That asymmetry is not an accident of Nigerian politics. It is the
default condition of all safety regulation everywhere, and it is why
every country's aviation safety system was built after disasters
rather than before them. The asymmetry breaks only when the diffuse
population is suddenly converted into an organized one, which is
what a catastrophe does and what almost nothing else does. A crash
takes the statistical population and produces, in a single
afternoon, an identifiable, organized, morally unanswerable group
with a permanent grievance and nothing left to lose.

That is the entire political economy of aviation reform, in every
country and in every era, and it explains the timing of everything
in this book. It explains why the reform came in 2006 and not 1996,
though the diagnosis was identical. It explains why India rebuilt
its airspace after Charkhi Dadri and America rebuilt its after the
Grand Canyon. It also explains the plateau of Chapter 10, because a
constituency built out of grief has a half-life, and when the
funerals stop, so does the coalition.

## The boxes that would not speak

The Bellview investigation failed forensically, and the failure has
a policy lesson precise enough to legislate from.

Flight recorders are built around crash-survivable memory units
certified against defined standards, which include severe impact
deceleration, penetration, deep-water pressure, and an hour of
high-temperature fire. They are certified, that is, against the
envelope of survivable and typical accidents. A near-vertical,
high-velocity impact into laterite concentrates energy at the
extreme edge of any capsule's rating, and the units recovered from
the Lisa crater emerged with damage that put their contents beyond
the readout capability then reachable by Nigeria.

There was no domestic laboratory. The foreign laboratories that
perform this work, in Washington, in Farnborough, and in Paris,
routinely perform miracles, but not always, and the wreckage-site
custody of the first days at Lisa, a trampled and scavenged
excavation, had degraded what remained. The final report, when it
surfaced years later, was an honest document about its own defeat.
The sequence could be bounded. It could never be established.

Flight 210 therefore holds a particular place in this history as the
crash about which Nigeria knows least, and as the proof that a state
which cannot find a wreck for twelve hours, and cannot read a
recorder recovered from its own soil, has forfeited even the
consolation of understanding its disasters.

The policy translations are direct, and Nigeria eventually made two
of them. Recorder standards are worth mandating ahead of
international minima in high-risk registries. Wreckage-site custody
is a trainable, drillable discipline that failed at Lisa before any
laboratory could have helped. And a regional African recorder
laboratory, long proposed in continental planning and perennially
unfunded, would repay its cost with the first mystery it retired.
Nigeria's investigators did, in later years, build real readout
capability in Abuja. The families of Flight 210, fifteen years too
early, received instead a single word. Undetermined. That is what an
infrastructure gap is called after it has happened to someone.

## What ambiguity does

The damage of that word compounded outward in a way worth tracing,
because it explains something about Nigerian public life far beyond
aviation.

Bellview was the era's respectable airline, the survivor of the
1990s cull, the regional expander, the operator that had passed the
international operational audit, and the business traveler's choice.
With no authoritative cause ever established, the permanent
ambiguity was colonized by everything an information famine breeds,
including sabotage theories, hidden-failure theories, and
causes-known-and-suppressed theories. None of this was Nigerian
credulity. It was rational inference under informational famine.
Where official causation is absent, the explanation market clears at
whatever price rumor sets.

The consequences ran in two directions. The airline's brand never
recovered, and it declined and ceased operations within a few years,
its corporate records dispersing with it, which guaranteed the
questions would never close. And the public lost the ability to
distinguish the era's best operator from its worst.

That last consequence may have been the deepest damage of the entire
season, and it is an economic point as much as a civic one. Consumer
choice is a safety mechanism, and when passengers can identify and
reward the safer operator, diligence pays and the market pushes the
industry upward. That mechanism requires information. An
investigative system that does not explain crashes does not merely
fail the dead. It disables the one organic force that would
otherwise have improved the living system. Investigative capacity,
in other words, is not a service to blame. It is a service to
innocence, owed to the crew who could not defend themselves, to the
engineers suspected forever without evidence, and to every operator
who spent money on maintenance and could not prove it.

## The go-around that was not flown

The Sosoliso cockpit, in its last ninety seconds, held a decision
structure that aviation psychology has since mapped so thoroughly it
carries a name in every training syllabus, which is plan
continuation bias. It is the deepening commitment to a course of
action as evidence accumulates against it, and it is strongest,
counterintuitively, precisely when the plan is nearly complete.

An approach is its textbook habitat, and the reason is structural.
The crew has briefed the approach, configured the aircraft, and
built a mental model in which the flight ends on that runway, and
the destination is two minutes ahead. Each adverse cue arrives
incrementally, whether the windshear report, the increasing sink
rate, or the visual picture degrading, and no single cue crosses the
threshold that would have forbidden the approach outright had it
been present at the start. Meanwhile the brain prices the
alternative asymmetrically. The go-around is an event, involving
fuel burned, delay incurred, an explanation owed, the passengers
lurching, and, in the part nobody says aloud, the subtle
professional confession that one has misjudged. The continuation is
priced at nothing, because it is simply the plan.

The industry's answer, hardened worldwide in exactly the years after
2005, was to abolish the judgment call entirely. The stabilized
approach doctrine defines gates for speed, configuration, descent
rate, and energy at specified heights on final approach, commonly a
thousand feet in instrument conditions and five hundred feet in
visual ones, and any deviation at the gate mandates a go-around. No
diagnosis is permitted. The decision is made in the briefing room,
where cognition is cheap, rather than at four hundred feet, where it
is impossible. Airlines that enforce the doctrine, and that monitor
compliance through flight-data programs, have driven
approach-and-landing accidents, historically aviation's largest
category, toward the floor.

The crews of Flight 1145, and ten months later of Flight 053, flew
before that doctrine reached Nigerian operations. Between them, the
two crashes taught the Nigerian industry the sentence its training
departments now repeat as liturgy. A go-around is never an admission
of failure. It is the system working.

## What 2005 established

Read the two investigations together and the year's verdict on the
Nigerian state assembles itself with unusual clarity. It could not
prevent, as two mass-casualty weather-related losses in seven weeks
demonstrated. It could not find, as the twelve hours to Lisa
demonstrated. It could not rescue, as the dry tenders at a certified
airport demonstrated. And in the worst case it could not explain, as
the unreadable recorders demonstrated. Prevention, response, and
explanation are the three duties a state owes the flying public, and
all three failed simultaneously, in public, on live television,
before a press that no longer answered to a censor. That
comprehensive visibility is what made 2005 politically different
from everything before it.

After Bellview the machinery had produced its standard outputs of
mourning, a probe, and contradictory official statements. But two
non-standard things happened. The press did not move on, and the
private broadcasters and the matured newspapers kept the search
fiasco on the front pages for weeks and began the cumulative,
cross-referencing coverage, counting off the fourth major crash
since 1992, that converts events into a record. And the
international layer engaged, because the ICAO audit cycle was
already scheduled, foreign carriers' concerns about Lagos were
leaking into the trade press, and the World Bank's transport people
were circulating the sector review that would supply much of the
coming reform's technical text. The state was, for the first time,
being watched by audiences it could not manage.

Then Sosoliso detonated inside that charged field. Within days the
president was at Port Harcourt and at the school, photographed amid
sixty caskets, in images that defined the era. Within weeks the
government convened the aviation summit the industry had requested
for a decade, with terms of reference that became the skeleton of
the 2006 Act, covering regulator autonomy, investigation
independence, infrastructure funding, and fleet standards. The
professional diaspora was recruited into the drafting and the
international agencies were invited into the room.

And the political class, facing elections with the images of 2005
fresh, discovered that aviation reform had become the rarest
commodity in Nigerian public life, which is a policy with no
organized opposition. Even the operators that the coming purge would
kill could not argue publicly against reform across the graves of
sixty children. The window that a royal funeral would fling fully
open in October 2006 was, by January, already unlatched.

## After

Nations, like people, have years that divide their lives into before
and after.

What 2005 changed was not the system's facts. Chapters 3 through 7
had established those in full, and every one of them had been
documented by somebody at the time. What 2005 changed was the
system's witnesses. For the first time the failure was total,
simultaneous, televised, and inflicted on a constituency organized
enough to refuse consolation.

The children of Loyola Jesuit did not die because the system of 2005
was worse than the system of 1996. They died because it was the
same. And the country, at last, could see it.

At the memorial garden on the school grounds, sixty names are carved
in stone, and they are read aloud each December by students who were
not born when the aircraft fell.

Ten months after that carol service, one more aircraft would fall.
This one would be carrying a king.
