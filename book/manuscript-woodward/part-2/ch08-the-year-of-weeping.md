# 8. The Year of Weeping

## Lagos, Saturday, October 22, 2005, 20:45

Bellview Airlines Flight 210 lifted off from Murtala Muhammed
International Airport for Abuja with 117 people aboard, into a line
of thunderstorms that everyone at the field could see.

Three minutes later it was gone from the radio.

And then something happened that had no precedent even in the
history recorded in this book.

For twelve hours, the Federal Republic of Nigeria could not find a
Boeing 737 that had fallen within thirty kilometers of its largest
city.

## What the state knew, and could not assemble

At the moment contact was lost, the system possessed — distributed
across several offices — everything required to draw a small circle
on a map.

A departure time. A cleared route. A last radio call. A fuel load.
An aircraft performance envelope.

The circle of possibility was not large.

What the system did with that information was nothing coherent.

The airline reported the aircraft missing. The night-duty layers of
the agencies activated as separate organisms — aviation, military,
police, the young National Emergency Management Agency — each
calling its own chain, none commanding the others.

No joint rescue coordination center took charge of the picture.
There was no doctrine assigning one. There was no exercised protocol
for who would.

Into that vacuum came the era's new information ecology: mobile
phones and morning drive-time radio.

A report — its origin never satisfactorily traced — placed the crash
near Kishi, in Oyo State, more than a hundred kilometers north.

Officials repeated it.

The machinery lurched toward it.

Families were told. Some began driving.

For much of that night, the Republic searched the wrong forest at
the wrong end of the country, while the actual wreckage lay under
canopy at Lisa, in Ogun State, thirty kilometers from the runway the
aircraft had left.

The farmers of Lisa found it at first light, walking toward smoke
that the government's aircraft had flown past.

A crater in the forest. The 737 driven into the earth almost
vertically. No survivors.

And for several hours more, custody of the site belonged to whoever
arrived: villagers, then journalists, then a gathering crowd, then —
progressively — the state, reaching its own catastrophe behind the
general public.

## THE ARGUMENT: The counterfactual that gives the night its weight

Everyone aboard Flight 210 was killed at impact. The forensic
evidence, such as it was, established that beyond argument.

So a certain kind of official response — one that was in fact made
at the time — holds that the twelve hours cost nothing. Nobody could
have been saved. The search failure was embarrassing rather than
lethal.

This reasoning is exactly backwards, and understanding why is
essential to everything Chapter 13 will argue.

Search and rescue is insurance. Its value is not determined by the
outcome of any single event; it is determined by its expected
performance across the *class* of events.

Nobody in the operations rooms that night knew that Lisa was
unsurvivable. They could not have known. A large fraction of
transport aircraft accidents are survivable or partially survivable
— runway excursions, undershoots, controlled descents into terrain
at low energy. In those cases, the interval between impact and
professional arrival is the interval in which the injured die of
things that are treatable.

The system that took twelve hours to locate Flight 210 would have
taken twelve hours to reach a survivable crash.

The lottery that night was drawn in a way that concealed the failure.
It could as easily have drawn the other way.

You cannot judge an insurance policy by whether the house happened
to burn.

## Loyola Jesuit College, Abuja, December 2005

The school on the Abuja plain was the reconstruction of the Nigerian
professional class in miniature.

Founded in 1996 and run by Jesuits, it had become within a decade
one of the academically fiercest institutions in the country. Its
families were civil servants and doctors, officers and
entrepreneurs, north and south, Muslim and Christian.

At the end of the term, its students flew home.

This is a structural fact about Nigeria that requires explanation
for foreign readers, because it converted a terrible chance into a
systemic finding.

Nigeria is a boarding-school nation. It is a legacy of colonial
education, reinforced by the federal unity schools that deliberately
mix the regions, and by a professional class dispersed across a
federation whose distances defeat weekend parenting.

So at every term's turning, thousands of minors cross the country in
concentrated waves, on predictable dates. The school run. Seats
booked in blocks. Younger children chaperoned by older ones.

And parents funded those flights precisely as a *safety purchase* —
because the flight was judged, entirely reasonably, safer than the
highway.

On December 10, 2005, sixty students of Loyola Jesuit College
boarded Sosoliso Airlines Flight 1145 at Abuja for Port Harcourt.

## Port Harcourt, December 10, 2005, afternoon

The DC-9 arrived over its destination in heavy weather.

On approach, in a thunderstorm, the aircraft encountered windshear —
the sudden, violent reversal of wind near the ground that Chapter 9
will describe in full. It struck the ground short of and beside the
runway, broke up along the grass, and burned.

The impact was survivable for many aboard.

And then the second disaster began.

The airport's rescue and firefighting service reached the burning
wreck late.

And it reached it, by the convergent testimony of survivors'
families and subsequent inquiry, without adequate water or foam.

Passengers who had lived through the impact died in the fire.

One hundred and eight people were lost.

Two survived. Both terribly burned. One of them was a teenage girl
named Kechi Okwuchi, whose years of reconstructive surgery abroad,
and whose eventual appearance singing on an American television
stage, would become the tragedy's one publicly bearable thread. Her
account of that afternoon — the impact survivable, the flames
arriving before help — has been given many times on the public
record, and has done more than any official document to lodge one
detail permanently in the national memory.

The fire trucks came without water.

Sixty children of one school.

A headmaster reading names at a carol service that had become a
funeral.

A mass grave on the school grounds, because some of the children
could not be told apart.

## THE NUMBERS: What three minutes buys

The international standard for airport firefighting is not
aspirational garnish. It is calibrated to physics, and the
calibration deserves to be spelled out, because it converts a
sentence about dry tenders into a body count.

ICAO's Annex 14 specifies, for an airport of Port Harcourt's traffic
category, response to any point of the runway within about three
minutes, delivering rated quantities of water and foam.

Why three?

Because of what happens inside an intact fuselage adjacent to a
kerosene fire.

An aircraft cabin is a sealed tube of aluminum containing a large
quantity of flammable furnishings and a limited quantity of
breathable air. An external fuel fire heats the skin, ignites the
interior, and produces — before flame ever reaches a passenger —
smoke and toxic gases in concentrations that incapacitate within
tens of seconds and kill within a few minutes.

The survivable interval for an intact cabin adjacent to a fuel fire
is measured in low single-digit minutes. That is the number the
standard is built around.

Which means the difference between a two-minute response and a
ten-minute response is not eight minutes of inconvenience.

It is everyone the fire reaches second.

And here is the detail that turns description into indictment.

Every airport in this book's ledger was *certified*, on paper, at a
firefighting category adequate to its traffic.

The vocabulary of the international standard had been purchased. The
trucks had been procured — some of them. The categories had been
declared. The boxes had been inked.

What had not been purchased was the substance: agent stocks, pump
maintenance, access-road planning, and above all the drills that
convert equipment into capability.

Call it certification as costume.

It is the purest expression of the pathology this book has tracked
since the empty regulatory housing of Chapter 3 — and at Port
Harcourt, the costume met its only honest audience.

## THE ARGUMENT: Who bears the risk of a system nobody drills

There is a specific injustice in the Sosoliso response that goes
beyond incompetence, and it is worth stating carefully.

The passengers of Flight 1145 had, in a meaningful sense, paid for
the fire service.

Airport charges are levied on every ticket. A portion of what those
parents spent to fly their children home was, by the design of
aviation finance, a payment for rescue capability at the
destination.

They bought an insurance policy embedded in the fare.

The policy was not in force.

This is different from the failures of Chapters 4 through 7, and the
difference matters. Those were failures to prevent — deferrals in a
system whose participants might plausibly claim they were unaware of
the accumulated risk.

This was a failure to *deliver a service that had been charged for*,
in a facility that had been certified as delivering it, to people
who had no way of auditing the claim and no alternative supplier.

In any other market, that is called fraud. In aviation it was called
a category declaration.

## The mothers of Loyola

What the Loyola families did next distinguishes 2005 from every
prior chapter in this book, and it began with a decision whose
importance is easy to miss.

They organized as the school community, not as scattered claimants.

Read that sentence again, because it is the mechanism.

Every previous group of Nigerian air-crash families had faced the
state as individuals: a widow here, an orphaned family there, each
negotiating separately with an insurer, each grieving privately,
each dispersing when the news cycle moved on. Their grief was
enormous and it was politically weightless, because it was
atomized.

The Loyola parents were, by tragic accident, the coalition that
Nigerian aviation safety had never had.

They were numerous enough to matter. They were concentrated enough
to organize — one school, one city, one parish network, one set of
class lists. They were elite enough to be unignorable: their ranks
included senior lawyers who understood process, officials who
understood ministries, and executives who understood leverage. And
they were armed with the single grievance that no Nigerian
government could wave away.

Children.

They formed associations and retained counsel. They attended every
adjournment of every proceeding. They built memorial scholarship
funds. They demanded the Sosoliso report and got it. They pressed
the firefighting findings into the public record. They turned each
anniversary into an audit.

And they refused, year after year, to allow the customary amnesia.

Organized, articulate, unignorable grief is the only force in this
entire history that ever outlasted a news cycle.

## THE ARGUMENT: The economics of political attention

This is the place to state the general theory that the rest of the
book depends on, because 2005 is where the theory is demonstrated.

Why do institutions that everyone knows are failing continue to
fail?

The standard answer — corruption, incompetence, culture — is
inadequate, because the same institutions, with the same personnel,
sometimes stop failing very quickly. Nigeria's aviation regulator
did precisely that within eighteen months of this chapter.

The better answer is about the structure of costs and benefits
across time and across groups.

The benefits of neglect are **concentrated, immediate, and
delivered to organized parties**: the ministry that keeps the levy
money, the airline that avoids the check, the official who grants
the waiver, the treasury that defers the radar.

The costs of neglect are **diffuse, delayed, and probabilistic**,
and they fall on a statistical population — future passengers — who
do not know they are the population and cannot organize as one.

That asymmetry is not an accident of Nigerian politics. It is the
default condition of all safety regulation everywhere, and it is why
every country's aviation safety system was built after disasters
rather than before them.

The asymmetry breaks only when the diffuse population is suddenly
*converted into an organized one*.

Which is what a catastrophe does — and what almost nothing else
does.

A crash takes the statistical population and produces, in a single
afternoon, an identifiable, organized, morally unanswerable group
with a permanent grievance and nothing left to lose.

That is the entire political economy of aviation reform, in every
country, in every era.

And it explains the timing of everything in this book. It explains
why the reform came in 2006 and not 1996, though the diagnosis was
identical. It explains why India rebuilt its airspace after Charkhi
Dadri and America rebuilt its after the Grand Canyon.

It also explains the plateau of Chapter 10 — because a constituency
built out of grief has a half-life, and when the funerals stop, so
does the coalition.

## The boxes that would not speak

The Bellview investigation failed forensically, and the failure has
a policy lesson precise enough to legislate from.

Flight recorders are built around crash-survivable memory units,
certified against defined standards: severe impact deceleration,
penetration, deep-water pressure, and an hour of high-temperature
fire.

Certified, that is, against the envelope of *survivable and typical*
accidents.

A near-vertical, high-velocity impact into laterite concentrates
energy at the extreme edge of any capsule's rating. The units
recovered from the Lisa crater emerged with damage that put their
contents beyond the readout capability then reachable by Nigeria.

There was no domestic laboratory. The foreign laboratories that
perform this work — in Washington, in Farnborough, in Paris —
routinely perform miracles, but not always, and the wreckage-site
custody of the first days at Lisa, a trampled and scavenged
excavation, had degraded what remained.

The final report, when it surfaced years later, was an honest
document about its own defeat. The sequence could be bounded. It
could never be established.

Flight 210 therefore holds a particular place in this history: the
crash about which Nigeria knows least, and the proof that a state
which cannot find a wreck for twelve hours, and cannot read a
recorder recovered from its own soil, has forfeited even the
consolation of understanding its disasters.

The policy translations are direct, and Nigeria eventually made two
of them. Recorder standards are worth mandating ahead of
international minima in high-risk registries. Wreckage-site custody
is a trainable, drillable discipline that failed at Lisa before any
laboratory could have helped. And a regional African recorder
laboratory — long proposed in continental planning, perennially
unfunded — would repay its cost with the first mystery it retired.

Nigeria's investigators did, in later years, build real readout
capability in Abuja.

The families of Flight 210, fifteen years too early, received
instead a single word.

Undetermined.

That is what an infrastructure gap is called after it has happened
to someone.

## THE ARGUMENT: What ambiguity does

The damage of that word compounded outward in a way worth tracing,
because it explains something about Nigerian public life far beyond
aviation.

Bellview was the era's *respectable* airline — the survivor of the
1990s cull, the regional expander, the operator that had passed the
international operational audit, the business traveler's choice.

With no authoritative cause ever established, the permanent
ambiguity was colonized by everything an information famine breeds:
sabotage theories, hidden-failure theories,
causes-known-and-suppressed theories.

None of this was Nigerian credulity. It was rational inference under
informational famine. Where official causation is absent, the
explanation market clears at whatever price rumor sets.

And the consequences ran in two directions.

The airline's brand never recovered; it declined and ceased
operations within a few years, its corporate records dispersing with
it, which guaranteed the questions would never close.

And the public lost the ability to distinguish the era's best
operator from its worst.

That last consequence may have been the deepest damage of the entire
season, and it is an economic point as much as a civic one.

Consumer choice is a safety mechanism. When passengers can identify
and reward the safer operator, diligence pays and the market pushes
the industry upward. That mechanism requires *information*.

An investigative system that does not explain crashes does not
merely fail the dead. It disables the one organic force that would
otherwise have improved the living system.

Investigative capacity, in other words, is not a service to blame.

It is a service to innocence — to the crew who could not defend
themselves, to the engineers suspected forever without evidence, and
to every operator who spent money on maintenance and could not prove
it.

## The go-around that was not flown

The Sosoliso cockpit, in its last ninety seconds, held a decision
structure that aviation psychology has since mapped so thoroughly it
carries a name in every training syllabus.

Plan continuation bias.

It is the deepening commitment to a course of action as evidence
accumulates against it — and it is strongest, counterintuitively,
precisely when the plan is nearly complete.

An approach is its textbook habitat, and the reason is structural.

The crew has briefed the approach, configured the aircraft, and
built a mental model in which the flight ends on that runway. The
destination is two minutes ahead.

Each adverse cue arrives *incrementally*: the windshear report, the
increasing sink rate, the visual picture degrading. No single cue
crosses the threshold that would have forbidden the approach
outright had it been present at the start.

Meanwhile the brain prices the alternative asymmetrically. The
go-around is an *event*: fuel burned, delay incurred, an explanation
owed, the passengers lurching, and — the part nobody says aloud —
the subtle professional confession that one has misjudged.

The continuation is priced at nothing. It is simply the plan.

The industry's answer, hardened worldwide in exactly the years after
2005, was to abolish the judgment call entirely.

The stabilized approach doctrine defines gates — speed,
configuration, descent rate, energy — at specified heights on final
approach. Any deviation at the gate *mandates* a go-around. No
diagnosis is permitted. The decision is made in the briefing room,
where cognition is cheap, rather than at four hundred feet, where it
is impossible.

Airlines that enforce the doctrine, and that monitor compliance
through flight-data programs, have driven approach-and-landing
accidents — historically aviation's largest category — toward the
floor.

The crews of Flight 1145, and ten months later of Flight 053, flew
before that doctrine reached Nigerian operations.

Between them, the two crashes taught the Nigerian industry the
sentence its training departments now repeat as liturgy.

A go-around is never an admission of failure. It is the system
working.

## What 2005 established

Read the two investigations together and the year's verdict on the
Nigerian state assembles itself with unusual clarity.

It could not **prevent**: two mass-casualty weather-related losses
in seven weeks.

It could not **find**: twelve hours to Lisa.

It could not **rescue**: dry tenders at a certified airport.

And in the worst case, it could not **explain**: the unreadable
recorders.

Prevention, response, and explanation are the three duties a state
owes the flying public.

All three failed, simultaneously, in public, on live television,
before a press that no longer answered to a censor.

That comprehensive visibility is what made 2005 politically
different from everything before it.

After Bellview, the machinery had produced its standard outputs —
mourning, a probe, contradictory official statements. But two
non-standard things happened.

The press did not move on. The private broadcasters and the matured
newspapers kept the search fiasco on the front pages for weeks, and
began the cumulative, cross-referencing coverage — *the fourth major
crash since 1992* — that converts events into a record.

And the international layer engaged. The ICAO audit cycle was
already scheduled. Foreign carriers' concerns about Lagos were
leaking into the trade press. The World Bank's transport people were
circulating the sector review that would supply much of the coming
reform's technical text.

The state was, for the first time, being watched by audiences it
could not manage.

Then Sosoliso detonated inside that charged field.

Within days, the president was at Port Harcourt and at the school,
photographed amid sixty caskets — images that defined the era.

Within weeks, the government convened the aviation summit the
industry had requested for a decade, with terms of reference that
became the skeleton of the 2006 Act: regulator autonomy,
investigation independence, infrastructure funding, fleet standards.

The professional diaspora was recruited into the drafting. The
international agencies were invited into the room.

And the political class, facing elections with the images of 2005
fresh, discovered that aviation reform had become the rarest
commodity in Nigerian public life.

A policy with no organized opposition.

Even the operators that the coming purge would kill could not argue
publicly against reform across the graves of sixty children.

The window that a royal funeral would fling fully open in October
2006 was, by January, already unlatched.

## After

Nations, like people, have years that divide their lives into before
and after.

What 2005 changed was not the system's facts. Chapters 3 through 7
had established those in full, and every one of them had been
documented by somebody at the time.

What 2005 changed was the system's *witnesses*.

For the first time, the failure was total, simultaneous, televised,
and inflicted on a constituency organized enough to refuse
consolation.

The children of Loyola Jesuit did not die because the system of 2005
was worse than the system of 1996.

They died because it was the same.

And the country, at last, could see it.

At the memorial garden on the school grounds, sixty names are carved
in stone, and they are read aloud each December by students who were
not born when the aircraft fell.

Ten months after that carol service, one more aircraft would fall.

This one would be carrying a king.
