# 7. Gwammaja

## Kano, Saturday, May 4, 2002, early afternoon

Alhaji Sani, a composite figure assembled from the accounts the
press recorded in Gwammaja in the days that followed, was at his
stall when the sound came over the rooftops. Engines where engines
had no business being, low enough that he looked up expecting to see
the underside. He did.

Out at Mallam Aminu Kano International Airport, EAS Airlines Flight
4226 had begun its takeoff roll for Lagos with more than seventy
people aboard and a serving federal minister among them. The
aircraft was a BAC 1-11, more than twenty years old. It used a great
deal of runway, and it lifted off late and heavy into the furnace
heat, and then it did the thing that witnesses on the ground
described with terrible uniformity. It stopped climbing.

Staggering at rooftop height with its engines laboring, it cleared
the airport fence, sank across the neighborhoods southwest of the
field, cut a burning lane through houses and shops and the environs
of a primary school, and came apart. The people in the cabin had
little chance and the people beneath had none at all.

When the counting was finally done, and in that dense informal urban
fabric it was never really done, the settled figure approached 148
dead. Roughly half of them from the aircraft and roughly half of
them from the city underneath it.

## The quarter

Gwammaja belongs to the oldest continuously inhabited urban fabric
in West Africa, a weave of family compounds, Koranic schools, and
workshops assembled at walking scale over centuries. The lanes are
too narrow for a fire engine and were never planned to need one.

When the airport was sited in the colonial era it stood well clear
of the walls. By 2002 a city of some three million had flowed around
it.

This is not a Nigerian peculiarity. Every fast-growing city on earth
has besieged its airport. The approaches to Heathrow pass over two
million Londoners, and the old Kai Tak in Hong Kong threaded
apartment canyons. In 2007 a Brazilian airliner would overrun a
runway in the middle of São Paulo and kill 199 people, most of them
on the ground.

What differs between the managed sieges and the lethal ones is an
unglamorous apparatus with a technical vocabulary. Public safety
zones are areas at the runway ends where development is prohibited
or bought out, sized by the statistical distribution of where
aircraft actually come down. Obstacle limitation surfaces are
invisible geometric planes projecting from the runway, inside which
nothing may be built. Third-party risk contours are probability
maps, drawn like weather isobars, showing the annual risk of death
on the ground at each point around an airport.

Every one of those three instruments existed in Nigerian regulation
in 2002. Enforcing them would have required the airport authority,
the state government, the municipal planners, and the emirate's
customary land structures to act as a single institution, and
nothing in this book has yet managed to be one thing, let alone
four.

So the funnels filled with people. And on the afternoon of May 4,
2002, the probability contours that engineers draw off the end of a
runway lay across roofs that held sleeping families. And they
collected.

## The physics of a hot afternoon

The performance calculus that failed over Gwammaja deserves plain
language, because every passenger boarding a heavily loaded flight
from a hot-climate airport sits inside it.

Air is an aircraft's working fluid and heat thins it. A wing
generates lift, and an engine generates thrust, in proportion to the
mass of air each can process, and heat expands air, which means that
on a hot day every cubic meter contains less of it. Engineers fold
this into a single number called density altitude, which is the
altitude at which the standard atmosphere would match today's actual
air. On a forty-degree afternoon in the Sahel, an aircraft sitting
on a runway barely fifteen hundred feet above sea level performs as
though it were departing an airfield four or five thousand feet
higher.

Every increment compounds. A higher true airspeed is required for
the same lift, a longer takeoff roll is needed to reach it, less
excess thrust remains available for the climb, and, in the
certification case that matters most, a thinner margin remains if an
engine falters at the worst possible moment.

Every transport aircraft on earth is certified to survive an engine
failure at exactly that moment, and that certification is the
foundation of commercial aviation's safety. But it holds only
provided a set of assumptions holds, namely that the engines deliver
book thrust, that the weight is what the load sheet says, and that
the obstacle and terrain data are current.

Now degrade those assumptions the way the era degraded them. An old
engine, decades from the test cell, delivers a few percent below
book, which is legal if trend monitoring catches it and invisible if
the monitoring is paperwork. A load sheet built on standard
passenger weights, in a baggage culture that ran on negotiation, is
optimistic by an unknown margin, and a systematic underestimate of
just three or four percent of takeoff weight is enough to consume
the whole obstacle clearance allowance on a hot day. An obstacle
survey in the file describes a smaller city.

Each softness is small, each is deniable, and each is individually
tolerated. Their sum is not an approximation of the certified case.
It is a different case, never computed by anyone. On a cool morning
at light weight, the aircraft that crashed at Kano would very likely
have flown away from the same engine trouble, and nobody would ever
have learned how little margin remained in the account. Hot
afternoons are the audit.

State the general principle, because it reaches far beyond aviation.
Margins do not disappear. They are lent away in slivers, each
borrower confident that the others left enough, and systems reveal
their borrowed margins only at load. A society that meets its
systems only in fair weather knows nothing about them at all.

## The median operator

EAS Airlines was not a rogue carrier, and that is the finding.

It was a mid-tier operator of the deregulation era's second wave.
Its aircraft were legal, its crews were licensed, and its existence
was certified annually by the oversight system Chapter 11 will
dissect. The inquiries found no monstrous deviation. They found the
standard condition of the marginal Nigerian carrier of that decade,
meaning thin capital, elderly equipment, a weight-and-balance
culture loose in the way the whole system was loose, and engineering
fed by a bazaar of brokered parts.

EAS was the median, and the median crashed. When a system's typical
firm, operating typically, produces catastrophe, the actuarial
description of that system is not that it is risky. It is that the
system is priced to kill on schedule.

## How the world's old aircraft found Kano

The BAC 1-11 was Britain's answer to the Boeing 727, a rugged and
deafening workhorse designed in the early 1960s. By 2002 it was
effectively extinct in the skies of the countries that built and
first flew it, banned in practice by noise regulations and retired
by economics. In Nigeria it was the backbone of the deregulated
fleet.

The mechanism that put it there is one of the most important in this
book, and it is not corruption. It is a market operating exactly as
markets operate. Follow the asset.

An airframe rolls out new and enters service with a first-tier
carrier that maintains it to the manufacturer's full program, in its
own hangars, with immaculate records. After a decade or fifteen
years, newer and thriftier types arrive and the aircraft is sold
down, first to a charter operator, then to a second-tier carrier,
then to a third. Each owner extracts value while investing less. The
maintenance remains legal and is simply outsourced, and the records
pass from custodian to custodian like a title deed gathering stamps.
By twenty-five years the aircraft is worth less than one of its own
overhauled engines, environmental rules have closed the rich world's
airports to it, and its residual value is approaching scrap. It is
now precisely affordable to a naira-earning, dollar-starved operator
in Lagos or Kano.

Nothing about that transaction is illegal. And nothing about the
aircraft is safe by default. At twenty-five years an airframe is a
promise that must be actively kept, through heavy checks costing
millions of dollars, parts priced like jewelry, and engineering
judgment costing most of all. It has just been sold, precisely and
only, to the buyer least equipped to keep it.

Here is the general law, and it governs far more than aircraft.
Markets sort assets to whoever values them most, and in a
second-hand market for safety-critical goods, valuing most means
being able to extract revenue at the lowest cost of capital. Where
oversight is weak, the lowest cost of capital belongs to whoever
intends to spend least on keeping the promise. Risk flows downhill,
toward the jurisdictions least able to price it. The old fleet did
not drift to Nigeria. It was sorted there.

## The month the country was already bleeding

The crash fell into a nation still in shock. In January 2002 an
armoury had exploded at the Ikeja cantonment in Lagos, killing more
than a thousand people, many of them drowned in a canal while
fleeing in the dark, and the emergency-response failures of that
night prefigured everything Chapter 13 will systematize.

The May headlines set the armoury's canal beside Gwammaja's rubble
and drew the connection the government least wanted drawn. Two
disasters, one military and one civil, shared a single anatomy,
which was hazardous systems sited among the poor, maintained by
deferral, and answered by mourning.

## The hospital day

Kano's hospitals learned of the crash the way the state did, from
the smoke and then from the crowds.

Within the hour the major hospitals were receiving simultaneous
waves that no plan had allocated, comprising the burned, the
crushed, and the walking shocked, delivered in taxis and pickup beds
by the same neighbors who had dug them out and distributed among
facilities by proximity and rumor rather than by capability. No
casualty-regulating function existed to match patient to resource,
so burn cases arrived at hospitals with no burn capability while
operating theaters elsewhere stood ready and unused. Families
searched facility to facility for days, because no central casualty
register existed to search once. Blood ran short by evening and was
replenished the Nigerian way, by an appeal answered magnificently,
with ordinary Kano queuing at the donor benches until after dark.

The staff performed, by every account, with improvised excellence.
The system performed as designed, which is to say that it had not
been.

And then there is the number nobody has. Severe burns and crush
injuries kill in the second week as reliably as in the first hour,
through infection, renal failure, and the complications that a
mature trauma system exists to prevent. Kano in 2002 had, like the
rest of the country, a handful of dedicated burn beds nationally
against a casualty load in the hundreds. No study ever enumerated
the deaths that followed the days after. Nigerian disaster mortality
is counted at the scene or not at all, which flatters every
emergency response the statistics ever touch. A state that does not
count its second-week dead has made a decision, which is that the
post-disaster patient is the family's project. The families of
Gwammaja, who had already supplied the rescue, supplied the nursing
too.

## The people who never bought a ticket

Here is the moral heart of this chapter, and it deserves to be
argued rather than merely asserted, because it identifies a category
of injustice that the entire vocabulary of aviation safety conceals.

The passengers of Flight 4226 participated, at least formally, in
the system that killed them. They chose to fly, they paid a fare,
and they accepted the ordinary bargain of transport, which is a
small risk in exchange for a large convenience, undertaken
knowingly. Consent does real moral work in that bargain. It is why
we permit dangerous activities at all, and why the death of a
passenger, though tragic, is not the same category of wrong as the
death of a bystander.

The people of Gwammaja consented to nothing. They received no safety
briefing, signed no contract, and accepted no fare discount in
exchange for elevated risk. They were cooking lunch. They were
killed by the externalities of a system in which they were not
participants, by a fleet-age policy never enforced, a weight culture
never disciplined, an obstacle survey never updated, and a city
never planned. Every one of those deferrals was executed by someone
who would never live beneath the climb path of that runway.

Economists have a term for this arrangement, which is an
uninternalized externality, or, when the deferring party captures
the benefit and sheds the risk, moral hazard. The older word is
injustice. And it has a specific structure worth naming, because it
recurs wherever hazardous infrastructure meets unplanned settlement,
from chemical plants to tank farms to high-voltage corridors. The
benefits of the deferral accrue to identifiable parties who have
voice, meaning the operator, the ministry, and the treasury, while
the costs accrue to a statistical population that has none. The
people under the funnel are not a constituency. They are a
probability distribution.

A fair test of any regulatory state is how it treats the people who
cannot exit its failures. By that test the Nigerian system of 2002
failed more completely in Gwammaja than anywhere else in this book,
because every other chapter's victims at least appear somewhere in
the system's own ledgers.

## The ledger of the uncounted

What Nigeria owed Gwammaja, and what it paid, is the cleanest test
of a compensation system available anywhere in this story.

The surface victim's claim is pure liability. There is no
contributory negligence question, no assumption of risk, and no
ticket contract with limitation clauses, and international aviation
law had recognized the ground victim for half a century. Nigerian
practice ran otherwise.

The airline's insurance position, adequate on paper for
certification purposes, became on the day it was needed a fortress
of process. Claims required documents that the informal city does
not generate, including title deeds for compounds held by customary
tenure, death certificates for people who had never held birth
certificates, and letters of administration for estates with no
written will. Adjusters valued compound housing with formulas built
for insured suburbs. Settlements were offered at fractions and
accepted from exhaustion, or litigated into the second decade.
Government gestures, meaning a relief fund, a presidential visit,
and rebuilding pledges, followed the disaster-politics cycle and
receded with it. Years later, journalists marking anniversaries
found Gwammaja households still holding files. And EAS itself did
not survive the year's regulatory aftermath, so its liquidation
converted the remaining claims from slow to moot.

Now the general finding, which is the reason this section exists. A
liability system is the mechanism by which an economy prices harm
back onto the parties that produce it, and when it works, the
anticipation of paying for harm flows upstream into the decisions
that cause harm. Where courts are slow, records informal, and
defendants corporately mortal, the expected cost of harming third
parties rounds to zero, and a cost that rounds to zero is a cost
that no spreadsheet anywhere will ever avoid.

So the uncompensated of Gwammaja are two things at once. They are a
moral failure. And they are a market signal, received clearly by
every marginal operator in the country, which is that the downside
of falling on a neighborhood is survivable for everyone except the
neighborhood. Systems get, in the long run, precisely the accidents
they price for.

## The age rule

The crash forced one durable policy onto the national agenda, and it
deserves its footnote in the history of regulatory design, because
its critics were right and it was right anyway.

In the aftermath the regulator moved toward what became a signature
of Nigerian aviation, which was a ban on importing passenger
aircraft beyond a fixed age. The technical objection was immediate
and correct. Age is a crude proxy, since maintenance rather than
chronology determines whether an aircraft is safe, and a rigorously
maintained twenty-five-year-old airframe is safer than a neglected
ten-year-old one. The economists' objection followed, which was that
the rule raises costs, restricts supply, and prices marginal
travelers off aircraft and onto the roads, which are far more lethal
per kilometer.

Both objections are sound and both miss the institutional point. The
crudeness was the value. Condition-based oversight, meaning the
assessment of each airframe on its actual maintenance state,
requires an inspection depth the Nigerian regulator of 2002
conspicuously did not possess. Applying a sophisticated rule with an
unsophisticated inspectorate does not produce sophisticated
outcomes. It produces discretion, and discretion in that environment
produced the waiver culture. A bright line is cheap to verify. It
can be enforced by a clerk with a registry, and it cannot be argued
away in a meeting.

Here is the design principle, and it travels well beyond aviation
into every domain of regulation in low-capacity states. Where
enforcement capacity is scarce, rules must be designed to be cheap
to verify, even at the cost of being theoretically wrong. The age
rule was a crutch, adopted honestly as a crutch, by an institution
that knew it could not yet walk.

## The hinge in the politics

The politics of 4226 differed from every previous chapter in one
respect that would prove decisive. Nigeria was now a democracy,
three years into the Fourth Republic, with a presidential election a
year away. The crash therefore received something Jeddah, Ejigbo,
and the lagoon never had, which was political competition over its
meaning.

The National Assembly held hearings and summoned officials.
Opposition figures framed the disaster as governmental neglect. The
press, unmuzzled since 1999, investigated the airline, the
regulator, and the ministry with a freedom unimaginable four years
earlier. The government responded with the era's characteristic
package of probes, suspensions, the age-limit policy, and pledges of
airport modernization.

It is easy to be cynical about that response, and most Nigerians
were. The probes joined the national archive of the unpublished and
the hearings produced more theater than statute. But the structural
point stands, and it is the reason this chapter sits where it does
in the book. Accountability had acquired a market. Under military
rule an aviation disaster was an information-management problem for
the regime. Under democracy it became a resource for the regime's
competitors. That is not a moral improvement in the actors. It is a
change in the payoff structure, and institutions reform when someone
powerful profits from their reform.

The machinery of 2002 was still far too weak to convert the demand
into law, but the demand now existed and it did not disperse. Three
years later, when the system faced the greatest test in its history,
that demand was waiting, and this time there would be an organized
constituency to carry it. Mark May 2002, then, as the hinge where
the politics of Nigerian aviation changed phase, even though its
immediate harvest was thin.

## The twin across the ocean

Nigeria's closest institutional twin in world aviation is Indonesia,
and the resemblance is almost eerie. It is a sprawling, populous,
oil-exporting state with a geography that makes flight not a
convenience but a necessity. It deregulated aviation in the 1990s.
It grew traffic explosively on the rich world's hand-me-down jets.
It paid in a drumbeat of disasters that ended, in 2007, in a
European Union ban on its carriers and an American downgrade of its
oversight. The Indonesian version of the Gwammaja problem, meaning
marginal carriers, aging fleets, and encroached airports, produced
the same policy debates on very nearly the same calendar.

The divergence came afterward and it is the instructive part.
Indonesian reform, once seriously begun, was sustained through
growth. The state fused aviation reform to its development strategy,
because the archipelago's economy physically requires flight and the
ban was costing tourism and investment. The regulator was rebuilt
with international partners on published, dated compliance plans.
The market consolidated around carriers with balance sheets capable
of buying new aircraft at record scale, which threw the sorting
machine described above into reverse by sheer financing depth. And
the exit milestones were treated as national projects with
presidential ownership. By 2016 to 2018 Indonesia had left the ban
lists and regained its American Category 1 rating.

Two states with the same disease took the same medicine, and only
one stayed on it. The difference was not knowledge, money, or
culture. It was the political decision to make aviation a growth
strategy rather than a patronage arena, which gave Jakarta's
reformers the one thing Abuja's never durably had, namely a
constituency richer than the constituency for decay. Grief starts
reforms. Only interest finishes them.

## After

Kano has now appeared three times in this history. In 1925 it was
the stage on which the empire performed its arrival. In 1973 it was
the unread warning of the boom. In 2002 it was the externalized bill
of the deregulated bust.

The city had given warning enough and the country had three years to
act on it. It did not.

The scene now shifts south, to a forest clearing near a village
called Lisa, and a runway at Port Harcourt, in the year when
everything broke at once.
