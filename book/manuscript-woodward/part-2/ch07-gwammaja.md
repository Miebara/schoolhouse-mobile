# 7. Gwammaja

## Kano, Saturday, May 4, 2002, early afternoon

Alhaji Sani — a composite figure, assembled from the accounts the
press recorded in Gwammaja in the days that followed — was at his
stall when the sound came over the rooftops.

Engines where engines had no business being. Low enough, he said,
that he looked up expecting to see the underside.

He did.

Out at Mallam Aminu Kano International Airport, EAS Airlines Flight
4226 had begun its takeoff roll for Lagos with more than seventy
people aboard, a serving federal minister among them.

The aircraft was a BAC 1-11, more than twenty years old.

It used a great deal of runway. It lifted off late and heavy into
the furnace heat. And then it did the thing that witnesses on the
ground described with terrible uniformity.

It stopped climbing.

Staggering at rooftop height, engines laboring, it cleared the
airport fence, sank across the neighborhoods southwest of the field,
cut a burning lane through houses and shops and the environs of a
primary school, and came apart.

The people in the cabin had little chance.

The people beneath had none at all.

When the counting was finally done — and in that dense, informal
urban fabric it was never really done — the settled figure
approached 148 dead.

Roughly half of them from the aircraft.

Roughly half of them from the city underneath it.

## The quarter

Gwammaja belongs to the oldest continuously inhabited urban fabric
in West Africa. Family compounds, Koranic schools, workshops, woven
at walking scale over centuries. The lanes are too narrow for a fire
engine and were never planned to need one.

When the airport was sited in the colonial era, it stood well clear
of the walls.

By 2002, a city of some three million had flowed around it.

This is not a Nigerian peculiarity. Every fast-growing city on earth
has besieged its airport. The approaches to Heathrow pass over two
million Londoners. The old Kai Tak in Hong Kong threaded apartment
canyons. In 2007 a Brazilian airliner would overrun a runway in the
middle of São Paulo and kill 199 people, most of them on the ground.

What differs between the managed sieges and the lethal ones is an
unglamorous apparatus with a technical vocabulary:

**Public safety zones** — areas at the runway ends where development
is prohibited or bought out, sized by the statistical distribution of
where aircraft actually come down.

**Obstacle limitation surfaces** — invisible geometric planes
projecting from the runway, inside which nothing may be built.

**Third-party risk contours** — probability maps, drawn like weather
isobars, showing the annual risk of death on the ground at each
point around an airport.

Every one of those instruments existed in Nigerian regulation in
2002.

Enforcing them would have required the airport authority, the state
government, the municipal planners, and the emirate's customary land
structures to act as a single institution.

Nothing in this book has yet managed to be one thing, let alone
four.

So the funnels filled with people. And on the afternoon of May 4,
2002, the probability contours that engineers draw off the end of a
runway lay across roofs that held sleeping families.

And they collected.

## THE NUMBERS: The physics of a hot afternoon

The performance calculus that failed over Gwammaja deserves plain
language, because every passenger boarding a heavily loaded flight
from a hot-climate airport sits inside it.

Air is an aircraft's working fluid, and heat thins it.

A wing generates lift, and an engine generates thrust, in proportion
to the *mass* of air each can process. Heat expands air, which means
that on a hot day every cubic meter contains less of it.

Engineers fold this into a single number: density altitude — the
altitude at which the standard atmosphere would match today's actual
air.

On a forty-degree afternoon in the Sahel, an aircraft sitting on a
runway barely fifteen hundred feet above sea level performs as
though it were departing an airfield several thousand feet higher.

Every increment compounds:

- Higher true airspeed required for the same lift
- Longer takeoff roll to reach it
- Less excess thrust available for the climb
- And — the certification case that matters — a thinner margin if an
  engine falters at the worst possible moment

Every transport aircraft on earth is certified to survive an engine
failure at exactly that moment. That certification is the foundation
of commercial aviation's safety.

But it holds *provided* a set of assumptions holds:

- The engines deliver book thrust
- The weight is what the load sheet says
- The obstacle and terrain data are current

Now degrade those assumptions the way the era degraded them.

An old engine, decades from the test cell, delivers a few percent
below book — legally, if trend monitoring catches it; invisibly, if
the monitoring is paperwork.

A load sheet built on standard passenger weights, in a baggage
culture that ran on negotiation, is optimistic by an unknown margin.

An obstacle survey in the file describes a smaller city.

Each softness is small. Each is deniable. Each is individually
tolerated.

And their sum is not an approximation of the certified case.

It is a *different case*, never computed by anyone.

On a cool morning at light weight, the aircraft that crashed at Kano
would very likely have flown away from the same engine trouble, and
nobody would ever have learned how little margin remained in the
account.

Hot afternoons are the audit.

State the general principle, because it reaches far beyond aviation:

*Margins do not disappear. They are lent away in slivers, each
borrower confident that the others left enough. And systems reveal
their borrowed margins only at load.*

A society that meets its systems only in fair weather knows nothing
about them at all.

## The median operator

EAS Airlines was not a rogue carrier. That is the finding.

It was a mid-tier operator of the deregulation era's second wave.
Its aircraft were legal. Its crews were licensed. Its existence was
certified annually by the oversight system Chapter 11 will dissect.

The inquiries found no monstrous deviation. They found the standard
condition of the marginal Nigerian carrier of that decade: thin
capital, elderly equipment, a weight-and-balance culture loose in
the way the whole system was loose, engineering fed by a bazaar of
brokered parts.

EAS was the median.

And the median crashed.

When a system's *typical* firm, operating *typically*, produces
catastrophe, the actuarial description of that system is not
"risky."

It is priced to kill on schedule.

## THE ECONOMICS: How the world's old aircraft found Kano

The BAC 1-11 was Britain's answer to the Boeing 727 — a rugged,
deafening workhorse designed in the early 1960s.

By 2002 it was effectively extinct in the skies of the countries
that built and first flew it, banned in practice by noise
regulations and retired by economics.

In Nigeria it was the backbone of the deregulated fleet.

The mechanism that put it there is one of the most important in this
book, and it is not corruption. It is a market operating exactly as
markets operate.

Follow the asset.

An airframe rolls out new and enters service with a first-tier
carrier that maintains it to the manufacturer's full program, in its
own hangars, with immaculate records. After a decade or fifteen
years, newer and thriftier types arrive, and the aircraft is sold
down — to a charter operator, then a second-tier carrier, then a
third. Each owner extracts value while investing less. The
maintenance remains legal; it is simply outsourced, and the records
pass from custodian to custodian like a title deed gathering stamps.

By twenty-five years, the aircraft is worth less than one of its own
overhauled engines. Environmental rules have closed the rich world's
airports to it. Its residual value is approaching scrap.

And it is now precisely affordable to a naira-earning,
dollar-starved operator in Lagos or Kano.

Nothing about that transaction is illegal.

And nothing about the aircraft is safe *by default*. At twenty-five
years, an airframe is a promise that must be actively kept — heavy
checks costing millions of dollars, parts priced like jewelry,
engineering judgment costing most of all.

It has just been sold, precisely and only, to the buyer least
equipped to keep it.

Here is the general law, and it governs far more than aircraft:

*Markets sort assets to whoever values them most. In a second-hand
market for safety-critical goods, "values most" means "can extract
revenue at the lowest cost of capital" — and where oversight is
weak, the lowest cost of capital belongs to whoever intends to spend
least on keeping the promise.*

Risk flows downhill, toward the jurisdictions least able to price
it.

The old fleet did not drift to Nigeria. It was sorted there.

## The month the country was already bleeding

The crash fell into a nation still in shock.

In January 2002, an armoury had exploded at the Ikeja cantonment in
Lagos, killing more than a thousand people — many of them drowned in
a canal while fleeing in the dark. The emergency-response failures
of that night prefigured everything Chapter 13 will systematize.

The May headlines set the armoury's canal beside Gwammaja's rubble
and drew the connection the government least wanted drawn.

Two disasters, one military and one civil, sharing a single anatomy:
hazardous systems sited among the poor, maintained by deferral,
answered by mourning.

## The hospital day

Kano's hospitals learned of the crash the way the state did — from
the smoke, and then from the crowds.

Within the hour, the major hospitals were receiving simultaneous
waves that no plan had allocated: the burned, the crushed, and the
walking shocked, delivered in taxis and pickup beds by the same
neighbors who had dug them out, distributed among facilities by
proximity and rumor rather than by capability.

No casualty-regulating function existed to match patient to
resource. Burn cases arrived at hospitals with no burn capability
while operating theaters elsewhere stood ready and unused.

Families searched facility to facility for days, because no central
casualty register existed to search once.

Blood ran short by evening and was replenished the Nigerian way — by
an appeal, answered magnificently, with ordinary Kano queuing at the
donor benches until after dark.

The staff performed, by every account, with improvised excellence.

The system performed as designed. Which is to say, it had not been.

And then there is the number nobody has.

Severe burns and crush injuries kill in the second week as reliably
as in the first hour — through infection, renal failure, and the
complications that a mature trauma system exists to prevent. Kano in
2002 had, like the rest of the country, a handful of dedicated burn
beds nationally.

No study ever enumerated the deaths that followed the days after.

Nigerian disaster mortality is counted at the scene or not at all,
which flatters every emergency response the statistics ever touch.

A state that does not count its second-week dead has made a
decision: the post-disaster patient is the family's project.

The families of Gwammaja, who had already supplied the rescue,
supplied the nursing too.

## THE ARGUMENT: The people who never bought a ticket

Here is the moral heart of this chapter, and it deserves to be
argued rather than merely asserted, because it identifies a category
of injustice that the entire vocabulary of "aviation safety"
conceals.

The passengers of Flight 4226 participated, at least formally, in
the system that killed them. They chose to fly. They paid a fare.
They accepted the ordinary bargain of transport: a small risk, in
exchange for a large convenience, undertaken knowingly.

Consent does real moral work in that bargain. It is why we permit
dangerous activities at all, and why the death of a passenger,
though tragic, is not the same category of wrong as the death of a
bystander.

The people of Gwammaja consented to nothing.

They received no safety briefing, signed no contract, accepted no
fare discount in exchange for elevated risk. They were cooking lunch.

They were killed by the externalities of a system in which they were
not participants — by a fleet-age policy never enforced, a weight
culture never disciplined, an obstacle survey never updated, a city
never planned. Every one of those deferrals was executed by someone
who would never live beneath the climb path of that runway.

Economists have a term for this arrangement: an uninternalized
externality, or, when the deferring party captures the benefit and
sheds the risk, moral hazard.

The older word is injustice.

And it has a specific structure worth naming, because it recurs
wherever hazardous infrastructure meets unplanned settlement, from
chemical plants to tank farms to high-voltage corridors:

*The benefits of the deferral accrue to identifiable parties who
have voice — the operator, the ministry, the treasury. The costs
accrue to a statistical population that has none.*

The people under the funnel are not a constituency. They are a
probability distribution.

A fair test of any regulatory state is how it treats the people who
cannot exit its failures. By that test, the Nigerian system of 2002
failed more completely in Gwammaja than anywhere else in this book —
because every other chapter's victims at least appear somewhere in
the system's own ledgers.

## THE ECONOMICS: The ledger of the uncounted

What Nigeria owed Gwammaja, and what it paid, is the cleanest test
of a compensation system available anywhere in this story.

The surface victim's claim is pure liability. There is no
contributory negligence question, no assumption of risk, no ticket
contract with limitation clauses. International aviation law had
recognized the ground victim for half a century.

Nigerian practice ran otherwise.

The airline's insurance position — adequate on paper for
certification purposes — became, on the day it was needed, a
fortress of process.

Claims required documents that the informal city does not generate:
title deeds for compounds held by customary tenure, death
certificates for people who had never held birth certificates,
letters of administration for estates with no written will.

Adjusters valued compound housing with formulas built for insured
suburbs.

Settlements were offered at fractions and accepted from exhaustion,
or litigated into the second decade.

Government gestures — a relief fund, a presidential visit,
rebuilding pledges — followed the disaster-politics cycle and
receded with it.

Years later, journalists marking anniversaries found Gwammaja
households still holding files.

And EAS itself did not survive the year's regulatory aftermath. Its
liquidation converted the remaining claims from slow to moot.

Now the general finding, which is the reason this section exists.

A liability system is the mechanism by which an economy prices harm
back onto the parties that produce it. When it works, the
anticipation of paying for harm flows upstream into the decisions
that cause harm.

Where courts are slow, records informal, and defendants corporately
mortal, the expected cost of harming third parties rounds to zero.

And a cost that rounds to zero is a cost that no spreadsheet
anywhere will ever avoid.

So the uncompensated of Gwammaja are two things at once.

They are a moral failure.

And they are a *market signal*, received clearly by every marginal
operator in the country: the downside of falling on a neighborhood
is survivable — for everyone except the neighborhood.

Systems get, in the long run, precisely the accidents they price
for.

## The age rule

The crash forced one durable policy onto the national agenda, and it
deserves its footnote in the history of regulatory design, because
its critics were right and it was right anyway.

In the aftermath, the regulator moved toward what became a signature
of Nigerian aviation: a ban on importing passenger aircraft beyond a
fixed age.

The technical objection was immediate and correct. Age is a crude
proxy. Maintenance, not chronology, determines whether an aircraft is
safe. A rigorously maintained twenty-five-year-old airframe is safer
than a neglected ten-year-old one. The economists' objection followed:
the rule raises costs, restricts supply, and prices marginal
travelers off aircraft and onto the roads, which are far more
lethal per kilometer.

Both objections are sound. And both miss the institutional point.

The crudeness was the value.

Condition-based oversight — assessing each airframe on its actual
maintenance state — requires an inspection depth the Nigerian
regulator of 2002 conspicuously did not possess. Applying a
sophisticated rule with an unsophisticated inspectorate does not
produce sophisticated outcomes. It produces discretion, and
discretion in that environment produced the waiver culture.

A bright line is cheap to verify. It can be enforced by a clerk with
a registry. It cannot be argued away in a meeting.

Here is the design principle, and it travels well beyond aviation
into every domain of regulation in low-capacity states:

*Where enforcement capacity is scarce, rules must be designed to be
cheap to verify, even at the cost of being theoretically wrong.*

The age rule was a crutch, adopted honestly as a crutch, by an
institution that knew it could not yet walk.

## THE ARGUMENT: The hinge in the politics

The politics of 4226 differed from every previous chapter in one
respect that would prove decisive.

Nigeria was now a democracy — three years into the Fourth Republic,
with a presidential election a year away.

The crash therefore received something Jeddah, Ejigbo, and the
lagoon never had: *political competition over its meaning*.

The National Assembly held hearings and summoned officials.
Opposition figures framed the disaster as governmental neglect. The
press, unmuzzled since 1999, investigated the airline, the
regulator, and the ministry with a freedom unimaginable four years
earlier.

The government responded with the era's characteristic package:
probes, suspensions, the age-limit policy, pledges of airport
modernization.

It is easy to be cynical about that response, and most Nigerians
were. The probes joined the national archive of the unpublished. The
hearings produced more theater than statute.

But the structural point stands, and it is the reason this chapter
sits where it does in the book.

Accountability had acquired a market.

Under military rule, an aviation disaster was an
information-management problem for the regime.

Under democracy, it became a *resource* for the regime's
competitors.

That is not a moral improvement in the actors. It is a change in the
payoff structure — and institutions reform when someone powerful
profits from their reform.

The machinery of 2002 was still far too weak to convert the demand
into law. But the demand now existed, and it did not disperse. Three
years later, when the system faced the greatest test in its history,
that demand was waiting, and this time there would be an organized
constituency to carry it.

Mark May 2002, then, as the hinge where the politics of Nigerian
aviation changed phase — even though its immediate harvest was thin.

## The twin across the ocean

Nigeria's closest institutional twin in world aviation is Indonesia,
and the resemblance is almost eerie.

A sprawling, populous, oil-exporting state, with a geography that
makes flight not a convenience but a necessity. It deregulated
aviation in the 1990s. It grew traffic explosively on the rich
world's hand-me-down jets. It paid in a drumbeat of disasters that
ended, in 2007, in a European Union ban on its carriers and an
American downgrade of its oversight.

The Indonesian version of the Gwammaja problem — marginal carriers,
aging fleets, encroached airports — produced the same policy debates
on very nearly the same calendar.

The divergence came afterward, and it is the instructive part.

Indonesian reform, once seriously begun, was *sustained through
growth*. The state fused aviation reform to its development
strategy, because the archipelago's economy physically requires
flight and the ban was costing tourism and investment. The regulator
was rebuilt with international partners on published, dated
compliance plans. The market consolidated around carriers with
balance sheets capable of buying new aircraft at record scale — the
sorting machine described above, thrown into reverse by financing
depth. And the exit milestones were treated as national projects
with presidential ownership.

By 2016 to 2018, Indonesia had left the ban lists and regained its
American Category 1 rating.

Two states with the same disease took the same medicine.

Only one stayed on it.

The difference was not knowledge, money, or culture. It was the
political decision to make aviation a growth strategy rather than a
patronage arena — which gave Jakarta's reformers the one thing
Abuja's never durably had.

A constituency richer than the constituency for decay.

Grief starts reforms.

Only interest finishes them.

## After

Kano has now appeared three times in this history.

In 1925, as the stage on which the empire performed its arrival.

In 1973, as the unread warning of the boom.

In 2002, as the externalized bill of the deregulated bust.

The city had given warning enough. The country had three years to
act on it.

It did not.

And the scene now shifts south — to a forest clearing near a village
called Lisa, and a runway at Port Harcourt, in the year when
everything broke at once.
