# Loop D — Voice & Style Calibration

Target voice: the calm, analytical, historically panoramic, comparative
essay style associated with Fareed Zakaria. The book is an original work;
the voice is a register, not an impersonation.

## The ten writing rules

1. **Open with a scene or a vignette**, never with a thesis statement. The
   reader should be somewhere — a runway at Kano in 1925, a canal bank at
   Ejigbo — before being told what it means.
2. **Move story → facts → analysis**, in that order, in every major section.
   The scene earns the data; the data earns the argument.
3. **Compare relentlessly but fairly.** Every chapter contains at least one
   sustained international comparison, chosen because it isolates a variable
   — never to flatter or to shame.
4. **End chapters looking forward.** The final paragraph reflects, connects
   to the core thesis, and opens a question the next chapter will inherit.
5. **Explain every term of art at first use**, in-line, in one clause:
   "a wet lease — renting an aircraft with its crew, so that the plane flies
   under one flag while answering to another."
6. **Prefer the measured sentence to the dramatic one.** The material is
   dramatic enough. Adjectives of horror are rationed; numbers and silence
   do the work.
7. **Use the historical panorama.** Zoom out at least once per chapter: what
   was happening in Nigeria, in Africa, in world aviation at that moment.
8. **Address the reader as an intelligent generalist.** No jargon walls, no
   condescension. Rhetorical questions sparingly, and only when the chapter
   will answer them.
9. **Keep the moral register humane and precise.** Institutions are
   criticized structurally; individuals only on the documented record; victims
   are named to be honored, not to decorate.
10. **Return to the thesis from a new angle each time** — the crash as
    symptom, the institution as cause, the comparison as proof that outcomes
    are chosen.

## The structural template (per section/chapter movement)

- Sentence 1: scene or hook (concrete, sensory, dated).
- Paragraphs 2–5: facts and context (timeline, data, actors, definitions).
- Paragraphs 6–10: analysis (institutional, political, economic, global).
- Final paragraph: reflective conclusion or forward-looking question.

Crash chapters follow the fixed macro-template:
**Scene → Timeline → Investigation → Reaction → Institutional Analysis →
Comparative Note → Reflection.**

## Diction and mechanics

- Sentences average 18–24 words; vary rhythm with occasional short declaratives.
- First person singular used rarely (preface, epilogue, occasional asides);
  first person plural ("we") for shared civic reasoning.
- Numbers: spell out one through nine; numerals for 10+, all casualty counts,
  altitudes, runway designations.
- Institutional names spelled out at first use with abbreviation in
  parentheses: Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority (NCAA). Thereafter the
  abbreviation. One-time note distinguishing the Nigerian NCAA from the US
  National Collegiate Athletic Association is unnecessary — context suffices.
- Terminology locked (see consistency sheet): "the NCAA," "the AIB" (before
  2022), "the NSIB" (after 2022), "ICAO," "SMS — safety management system."
- Dates: 11 July 1991 style. Aircraft: "Boeing 727-200," registration in
  parentheses at first mention (5N-RAM).
- No scare quotes; no exclamation marks; no rhetorical italics more than
  once per chapter.

## Per-chapter tone samples (150–250 words each in the full styling pass; abbreviated keynotes here)

**Ch. 1:** "The airplane arrived in Nigeria before the idea of Nigeria had
settled. On a hot afternoon in 1925, a Royal Air Force biplane dropped out
of the Harmattan haze over Kano, and the emirate's ancient walls — which had
seen caravans from Tripoli and armies from Sokoto — acquired a new kind of
gate…" — wonder, distance, the long lens.

**Ch. 2:** "Every new nation buys three things first: a flag, an anthem, and
an airline. The flag is cheap, the anthem is free, and the airline is the
most expensive symbol a poor country can own…" — irony without contempt.

**Ch. 3:** "Decay is rarely dramatic. It is a spare part not ordered, a
simulator session not renewed, a runway light that fails and is not
replaced…" — accumulation, quiet dread.

**Ch. 4:** "The pilgrims boarded in Jeddah wearing the white of ihram, the
cloth of consecration. The aircraft that carried them wore three flags at
once — Canadian ownership, Nigerian branding, Saudi runway — and answered,
in the end, to none of them." — solemnity, the accountability triangle.

**Ch. 5:** "Armies expect to bury soldiers. They do not expect to bury the
staff college." — terseness, institutional grief.

**Ch. 6:** "Air traffic control is a conversation in which everyone must
tell the truth at the same time. Over Lagos in 1996, the instruments that
kept that conversation honest had been broken for years." — system-as-
metaphor.

**Ch. 7:** "The people of Gwammaja never bought a ticket. The aviation
system crashed into them anyway." — third-party injustice, controlled anger.

**Ch. 8:** "Grief can be an accident of geography. In December 2005 it
concentrated, with terrible precision, on the parents of one school." —
restraint at maximum load.

**Ch. 9:** "Reform in Nigeria has usually required a funeral. In October
2006 it received a royal one." — pivot chapter; elegy turning to agency.

**Ch. 10:** "Success in aviation safety is invisible: it is the crash that
does not happen, year after year. The question after 2012 was whether
Nigeria's quiet skies were an achievement or an interval." — vigilant
optimism.

**Ch. 11:** "No regulator anywhere is loved. The good ones settle for being
feared by the right people." — aphoristic essay voice.

**Ch. 12:** "An old airplane is not dangerous the way an old car is
dangerous. It is dangerous the way an old promise is dangerous: everything
depends on whether it has been kept up." — economic reasoning made tactile.

**Ch. 13:** "The first disaster belongs to physics. The second belongs to
the state." — the book's sharpest chapter; keep sentences short.

**Ch. 14:** "Aviation became the safest way to move because it became the
most honest industry on earth — not the most virtuous, the most honest."
— conceptual, almost philosophical.

**Ch. 15:** "Culture is the explanation of last resort, and it is almost
always wrong." — brisk, data-forward, five mirrors held up in turn.

**Ch. 16:** "The reform agenda for Nigerian aviation fits on one page.
It has fit on one page for thirty years. The page is not the problem." —
policy voice, verbs of obligation.

**Ch. 17:** "Every forecast about Africa is a forecast about Nigeria wearing
a disguise." — panoramic, forward-looking, ends on the preface's image.

## Consistency sheet (enforced in revision)

- NCAA = Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority (est. 1999; empowered 2006; re-chartered 2022).
- AIB = Accident Investigation Bureau (2006–2022); NSIB = Nigerian Safety
  Investigation Bureau (2022– ). Pre-2006 investigations: "the ministry's
  accident investigation unit."
- NAMA = Nigerian Airspace Management Agency; FAAN = Federal Airports
  Authority of Nigeria; NiMet = Nigerian Meteorological Agency; NEMA =
  National Emergency Management Agency.
- ICAO = International Civil Aviation Organization; SMS = safety management
  system; SARPs = standards and recommended practices; USOAP = Universal
  Safety Oversight Audit Programme; IASA = the FAA's International Aviation
  Safety Assessment; IOSA = IATA Operational Safety Audit; ARFF = aircraft
  rescue and firefighting; AOC = air operator certificate.
- "Nigeria Airways" (never "Nigerian Airways"); "the Flying Elephant" only
  after introducing the logo.
- Lagos airport: "Lagos international airport, Ikeja" historically; "Murtala
  Muhammed International Airport" after the 1976 renaming; "MMA" sparingly.
