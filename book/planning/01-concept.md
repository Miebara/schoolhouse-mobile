# Loop A — Book Concept Document

## Working title

**Broken Wings: A History of Aviation Crises in Nigeria**

Subtitle options (for the publisher's discussion):
1. *A History of Aviation Crises in Nigeria* (adopted)
2. *How a Nation's Institutions Failed in the Sky — and How They Can Be Rebuilt*
3. *Nigeria, Aviation, and the Anatomy of Institutional Failure*

## Core thesis

**Aviation crises in Nigeria are not isolated accidents but symptoms of deeper
institutional, political, and regulatory failures.**

Every commercial aircraft accident is, in the language of safety science, an
"organizational accident": the final crash is merely the last link in a chain
forged long before the aircraft left the ground — in ministries, boardrooms,
maintenance hangars, control towers, and budget negotiations. Nigeria's
aviation history is an unusually legible record of that chain-forging. The
country's crashes cluster around moments of institutional decay (the collapse
of Nigeria Airways, the deregulation free-for-all of the 1990s, the regulatory
capture of the early 2000s), and its periods of safety cluster around moments
of institutional repair (the Demuren-era NCAA, the post-2006 reform wave, FAA
Category 1 certification in 2010). Aviation is therefore not a niche subject:
it is a precision instrument for measuring the health of the Nigerian state.

Three corollaries structure the book:

1. **The crash is the symptom; the institution is the disease.** Technical
   causes (fuel exhaustion, windshear, engine failure) are real but never
   sufficient explanations. The question is always: what allowed a known,
   manageable risk to become fatal?
2. **Nigeria is not uniquely cursed; it is comparatively instructive.**
   Ethiopia, South Africa, Kenya, Indonesia, Brazil, and the United States all
   faced comparable crises. Their divergent outcomes isolate the variable that
   matters: institutional design and political will, not geography, wealth, or
   culture.
3. **Reform is possible and has happened.** Nigeria's own record contains the
   proof: the 2007–2012 reform era produced measurable, internationally
   certified improvement. The tragedy is not that Nigeria cannot fix its
   skies, but that it fixes them only after burying its dead — and forgets
   quickly.

## Audience

**Primary:** aviation professionals (pilots, engineers, dispatchers, air
traffic controllers), regulators and civil servants in NCAA/NSIB/NAMA/FAAN
and their African peers, aviation trainers and safety instructors, and
students of aviation management, transport policy, and public administration.

**Secondary:** educated general readers of serious narrative non-fiction;
policy analysts, development economists, and scholars of African political
economy; journalists covering transport and governance; the Nigerian diaspora.

The dual audience dictates the register: technically accurate enough that a
pilot nods along, but every term of art explained in plain English so a
general reader never stalls.

## Tone and style

- Calm, authoritative, humane; never sensationalist. The dead are treated
  with dignity; the living are treated with fairness; institutions are
  treated with rigor.
- Narrative-driven analysis: each movement runs **story → facts → analysis**.
  Open with a scene; ground it in verified data; ascend to institutional,
  political, and comparative interpretation.
- Panoramic and comparative: constant, disciplined use of other countries'
  experiences — not as decoration but as controlled experiments.
- Essay-like chapters with reflective, forward-looking conclusions that
  return, each time from a new angle, to the core thesis.
- Technical vocabulary introduced once, defined in context, then used
  naturally. A glossary backstops the reader.

## Length and shape

- 350–450 printed pages ≈ 90,000–115,000 words.
- Four parts, seventeen chapters, plus front matter (preface, introduction)
  and back matter (epilogue, appendices, glossary, notes on sources).
- Page budget:
  - Front matter: 20–30 pages
  - Part I (chs. 1–3): 60–80 pages
  - Part II (chs. 4–10): 140–190 pages
  - Part III (chs. 11–14): 80–100 pages
  - Part IV (chs. 15–17): 60–80 pages
  - Back matter: 30–50 pages

## Positioning

There is no single-volume, analytically serious, narratively compelling
history of Nigerian aviation and its crises written for both professionals
and general readers. Existing material is fragmented: accident reports read
by specialists; newspaper retrospectives on anniversaries; academic papers on
regulatory policy; memoirs by industry figures. *Broken Wings* occupies the
space held in other domains by books like *The Checklist Manifesto* (medicine),
*The Big Short* (finance), or *Command and Control* (nuclear weapons): the
authoritative narrative-analytical account that becomes the standard
reference and the standard gift.

Comparable titles (for the marketing conversation): William Langewiesche's
aviation essays; Andy Pasztor's safety reporting; *Why Planes Crash* genre
titles (which this book deliberately transcends by insisting on institutions
over incidents); Chinua Achebe's *The Trouble with Nigeria* (for the moral
register of institutional critique); Zakaria's *The Post-American World* (for
the panoramic comparative method).

## Ethical commitments

- Victims' names used sparingly and respectfully; no gratuitous detail.
- Findings attributed to investigations, not asserted as the author's own
  forensic conclusions.
- Living persons criticized only on the documented record.
- Every factual claim traceable to the source matrix; verification pass
  required before publication.
