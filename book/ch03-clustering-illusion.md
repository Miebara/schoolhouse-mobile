## 3. Shapes in the Clouds

### *The Clustering Illusion, Coincidence, and the Law of Small Numbers*

Dobelli's third chapter is about a man who sees faces in clouds and a woman who
finds a message in a slice of toast. The brain is a pattern-detector that was
built under an asymmetric penalty: mistaking a rock for a bear costs you a
startle, mistaking a bear for a rock costs you your genes. So the detector is
tuned to fire early and often. It over-reports. It finds regularity in noise,
and — crucially — it does not report its own error rate. Nobody experiences a
false positive as a false positive. You experience it as a pattern.

He gives the standard demonstration: people asked to write down a "random"
sequence of coin flips produce something far too orderly, avoiding long runs,
because genuinely random data looks suspiciously clumpy to us. Real randomness
contains streaks. Six heads in a row is unremarkable in a few hundred flips, but
show it to someone and they will search for a cause. During the London Blitz,
Londoners were convinced the bombing pattern was targeted, because certain
districts were hit repeatedly and others were spared; statisticians later divided
the map into grids and found a distribution indistinguishable from random. The
clusters were real. The targeting was not.

Now apply it, because this is where religious epistemology actually lives. Not
in cosmological arguments — almost nobody believes because of the fine-tuning
argument. People believe because of *signs*.

You are agonising over whether to take the job in another city. You open the
Bible at random and your eye falls on a verse about journeys. That afternoon, a
friend you haven't spoken to in two years calls from that very city. On the drive
home the radio plays the hymn your grandmother sang. Three hits in one day. The
feeling this produces is not a considered inference; it is a sensation of being
addressed. And once you have felt addressed, no argument reaches you, because
the experience did not arrive by argument.

Here is what the pattern-detector is not telling you. It is not telling you how
many events passed through your awareness that day — thousands — nor how many of
them *could* have counted as a sign had they happened to fit. That is the number
that matters, and it is unknowable and enormous. The verse would have been a sign
if it had mentioned journeys, or waiting, or courage, or fear, or trusting, or
staying, or almost anything, because scripture is a large corpus of poetic
material written to be applicable to human situations in general. The phone call
would have counted from anyone connected to either city, or to the decision, or
to your childhood. The hymn would have counted in twenty variants. When the
target is defined *after* the arrow lands, hitting the target tells you nothing.
Statisticians call it the Texas sharpshooter: shoot at the barn, then paint the
bullseye around the densest cluster of holes.

Dobelli has a second, related chapter on plain coincidence, and the arithmetic
there deserves stating because it is so consistently misjudged. Take the
dream-then-death case: you dream of a relative dying and within the week they
do. This feels like a message. Now count. Roughly eight billion people dream
every night, several dreams each, and a substantial fraction of dreams involve
someone close to us in danger. Multiply by 365. The number of dream-death
coincidences that must occur worldwide every year runs into the millions, purely
by chance, with no mechanism required at all. It would be astonishing if they
*didn't* happen. What you have experienced is not an improbable event; it is one
of the many millions of tickets in a lottery that is drawn continuously and
never reported when it loses.

The law of small numbers finishes the job. Dobelli's version: extreme results
show up in small samples far more often than intuition allows, which is why the
"best" and "worst" schools in a country are almost always tiny ones. A church of
sixty people will produce, over a decade, several coincidences so striking that
retelling them will sound like proof, and will produce them at exactly the rate
that chance predicts for a sample that size. This is why a congregation's shared
folklore of remarkable events is not cumulative evidence. It is a small sample's
tail, curated and repeated.

The most instructive case is the one where people got to check. In the 1990s a
book called *The Bible Code* claimed that skipping letters at fixed intervals
through the Hebrew text of Genesis revealed the names of modern figures and the
dates of their assassinations. It was a genuine phenomenon, in the sense that
the letters really are there. Then a mathematician named Brendan McKay ran the
same technique on the Hebrew translation of *War and Peace* and extracted a
comparable set of predictions, and other researchers pulled assassination
prophecies out of *Moby-Dick*. The method finds signal in any sufficiently long
text, which means it is not detecting signal at all — it is manufacturing it. The
lesson generalises far beyond one silly book: **a pattern-finding procedure that
has never been run on a control text has produced no information.** Almost no
religious sign-reading has ever been run against a control.

You can test this on yourself this week, and I recommend it. Decide in advance
what would count as a sign — write it down — and then start noticing signs
about a question you do not care about. Whether to repaint the kitchen. Within
three days you will have two or three uncanny hits, and because you have no
emotional stake, you will be able to feel the machinery working. That sensation
of being addressed is available on demand, about anything. Which tells you it is
a property of the detector, not of the world.

**In conclusion:** the number of ways the universe could have "spoken to you"
today is vast, unrecorded, and never counted. Before you treat a coincidence as
a message, ask how many events could have qualified, how many opportunities
there were, and whether you would have noticed the absence. If you would not
have noticed the absence, you have not observed a signal. You have observed a
brain doing the one thing it cannot stop doing.
