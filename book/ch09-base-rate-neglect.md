## 9. The Arithmetic of Miracles

### *Base-Rate Neglect, Neglect of Probability, and the Conjunction Fallacy*

Dobelli's chapter on base-rate neglect uses the standard medical example, and it
is worth doing the arithmetic slowly, because almost everyone gets it wrong and
the people who get it wrong include doctors.

A test for a disease is 99 percent accurate. The disease affects one person in
ten thousand. You test positive. What is the chance you have it? The intuitive
answer is 99 percent. The correct answer is about one percent. Out of a million
people, a hundred have the disease and the test catches ninety-nine of them —
but of the 999,900 healthy people, one percent test positive anyway, which is
9,999 false positives. Your positive result is one of roughly ten thousand, and
ninety-nine of those ten thousand are real. The evidence was strong. The base
rate was stronger.

Dobelli's lesson: **the prior probability dominates, and we ignore it.** We
attend to the vividness of the evidence in front of us and neglect how rare the
thing being claimed is to begin with.

Now take a miracle claim. Someone reports that a man was dead for three days and
returned to life. Consider the two competing explanations and their base rates.

Explanation A: a permanent exception to the operation of biology occurred, of a
kind for which we have no other confirmed instance in the entire recorded
history of medicine — hundreds of millions of deaths, all of them staying dead.

Explanation B: a story about a dead man returning was told, retold, believed and
written down decades later by devoted followers. Base rate for this: enormous.
Resurrection and post-mortem appearance claims are attested for Apollonius of
Tyana, for Romulus, for Zalmoxis, for Krishna, for the Buddha in some traditions,
for Sabbatai Zevi, and for religious founders on every continent. Reports of the
recently dead appearing to the bereaved are one of the most common experiences in
grief — surveys of widows and widowers routinely find that between a third and
half report sensing, seeing or hearing their dead spouse. Not credulous people:
ordinary people, under the specific psychological conditions that produce it.

This is David Hume's argument from 1748, and Dobelli's chapter gives it a
statistical spine. Hume's formulation: no testimony is sufficient to establish a
miracle unless the falsehood of the testimony would be more miraculous than the
event it reports. Restated in Bayesian terms: when the prior probability of the
claim is astronomically low and the prior probability of mistaken, embellished
or sincerely-misremembered testimony is high, testimony of ordinary quality
cannot move you. And the testimony here is not of ordinary quality — it is four
anonymous documents, written thirty-five to sixty-five years after the events, in
a different language from the one the participants spoke, by authors who never
claim to have been present, dependent on each other and disagreeing about who
went to the tomb, when, what they found, and who they told.

The neglect of probability compounds it. Dobelli notes that we are almost
insensitive to the difference between small probabilities: people react to a one
in ten thousand risk and a one in ten million risk in much the same way, which
is why lotteries and terrorism both command attention out of all proportion. In
religious reasoning this shows up as a shrug — "well, it's not impossible." Once
a claim is in the category "conceivable," its actual probability stops
functioning in the argument at all. But "not impossible" is a category that
contains everything, including every rival religion's miracles that you
comfortably reject.

Then the conjunction fallacy, which Dobelli illustrates with the famous "Linda"
problem: told about a politically engaged philosophy graduate, people judge it
more likely that she is a feminist bank teller than that she is a bank teller.
This is arithmetically impossible — every feminist bank teller is a bank teller —
and it happens because the detailed story is more plausible-*feeling* than the
sparse one. Every added detail makes a conjunction more coherent and less
probable. Religious claims are enormously conjunctive: a being exists, and it is
conscious, and it is omnipotent, and it is perfectly good, and it created this
universe deliberately, and it is interested in humans specifically, and it
communicated through one particular tribe, and it took human form once, in one
province, at one time, and it requires certain beliefs about that event. Each
clause makes the picture richer and the whole thing less likely, and the felt
plausibility moves in the opposite direction from the mathematics.

Now the numbers that ought to end the conversation about modern miracles. Lourdes
receives millions of pilgrims a year and has done since 1858; the total number of
cures the Catholic Church itself has certified as miraculous is around seventy.
Not seventy percent — seventy cures, across roughly 200 million visits. The
spontaneous remission rate for many cancers is, on its own, higher than that.
Whatever is happening at Lourdes, it is performing worse than doing nothing.

A concession, because Chapter 25 demands I make them. Base-rate reasoning cannot
rule out a genuinely unique event — that is its structural limit. If a
resurrection happened exactly once in the history of the universe, base rates
would tell you not to believe the report, and base rates would be wrong. This is
a real limitation and I will not pretend otherwise. But note what accepting it
costs: you must then apply the same generosity to every uniquely-attested
miracle in every tradition, and you have no principled way to accept yours and
decline the rest. The base rate is not a proof. It is the reason you already
disbelieve all the others.

**In conclusion:** before assessing any miracle report, ask two questions in
order. How often does the claimed event happen? How often does *this kind of
report* turn out to be mistaken, embellished, or sincerely misremembered? Then
compare the two numbers. Do not start with how compelling the witness is. The
witness is almost always compelling. That is the base rate for witnesses.
