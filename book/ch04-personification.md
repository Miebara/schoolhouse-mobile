## 4. Why We Hear a Person in the Thunder

### *Personification and the Fundamental Attribution Error*

Dobelli has a short, sharp chapter on personification: we understand the world
far more readily in terms of persons than in terms of processes. A newspaper does
not report "aggregate consumer demand contracted 0.4 percent"; it reports that
the finance minister failed. Charities do not publish mortality tables; they show
you one child with a name. Dobelli's point is not that this is stupid — it is
that it is a *compression algorithm*, and like all compression it throws
information away. The face survives. The mechanism is discarded.

Pair it with the fundamental attribution error, which Dobelli also treats: our
systematic habit of explaining events by the dispositions of agents rather than
by circumstances. Someone cuts you off in traffic and you conclude something
about their character; you cut someone off and you know it was the merge lane.
We over-attribute agency, and we do it automatically, in milliseconds, before
any deliberation is available.

Now, the largest personification in human history.

There is something it is like to be a creature whose survival depended on
detecting other minds. The most dangerous and most valuable things in the
ancestral environment were agents: predators, prey, rivals, allies. A mind
tuned to detect agency will produce false positives constantly — the rustle that
was wind, the shadow that was nothing — and the false positives are nearly free
while the misses are fatal. So we inherited a detector with the threshold set
low. It fires on ambiguous input. It fires on weather, on illness, on
earthquakes, on luck, on the sequence of events in a life.

You can watch it work in yourself with no religious content at all. Your laptop
refuses to connect and you say *it doesn't want to*. Your car starts on the
fourth try and you thank it. A run of bad luck feels *targeted* — not merely
unfortunate but pointed, as though something were making a point. Nobody
believes their laptop has intentions, and everybody talks as if it does, because
the agency-detector is not under argumentative control. It hands you a person and
then invites you to reason about the person.

Gods are what you get when this detector is applied to the largest ambiguous
inputs available: the weather, the harvest, disease, fortune, death. Notice how
precisely the attributes of gods across cultures match the outputs of a mind
built for social life rather than for physics. Gods want things. They can be
offended, flattered, negotiated with, and appeased. They notice whether you are
looking. They keep score. They respond to gifts, to public praise, to displays
of loyalty, and to the humiliation of their rivals. These are not the properties
of a cosmic principle. They are the properties of a high-status primate.
Anthropologists have documented god-concepts of astonishing variety, and the
variety is almost entirely in the surface details — number, iconography, food
preferences — while the social grammar stays fixed. That is the signature of a
shared cognitive template, not of a shared discovery.

And the psychological literature backs up the crude version. Show adults
animated geometric shapes moving on a screen — the classic Heider and Simmel
films from 1944 — and they will spontaneously narrate the triangle as bullying
the circle. Children reliably attribute purpose to natural objects; ask why
rocks are pointy and you get "so animals won't sit on them." Deborah Kelemen's
research calls it promiscuous teleology, and the striking finding is that it
does not fully disappear in adults. Under time pressure, physical scientists —
people who have spent careers rejecting purpose in nature — revert to
purpose-based explanations at elevated rates. The template is never deleted. It
is only overridden, effortfully, and the override is the first thing to fail when
you are tired, frightened, or grieving.

Which is exactly when religion does its recruiting. This is not a cynical
observation; it is a structural one. Bereavement, diagnosis, war, childbirth, the
loss of work — the moments when the agency-detector's override is weakest are
the moments the pew is most available, and the correlation between crisis and
conversion is one of the most robust findings in the sociology of religion. If
belief tracked evidence, conversion would cluster around the availability of
evidence. It clusters around the collapse of control.

Two honest notes. First, none of this shows that the detector is *always* wrong.
Sometimes there really is a bear. A cognitive tendency to see agents does not
prove there are no agents — it proves the detector's output is not evidence of
one. Second, the argument cuts less deeply against sophisticated theology than
against ordinary belief. A philosopher's God — timeless, impassible, the ground
of being — is not obviously a personification. But then a philosopher's God is
not the being anyone prays to, thanks for a parking space, or believes has
opinions about their sex life. When theologians want intellectual respectability
they retreat to the abstraction; when they want a congregation they come back to
the person. Watch how quickly the ground of being starts wanting things again
once the sermon is under way.

**In conclusion:** when you find yourself with the strong sense that something
*meant* this to happen, notice that you have received a person, not an
explanation — and that you received it before you had time to think. Ask what
the impersonal account looks like. It will be less satisfying, harder to hold in
mind, and much more likely to be true. The thunder is a pressure differential.
It has never once been about you.
