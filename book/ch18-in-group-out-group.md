## 18. Saved, Lost, and the Price of Asking

### *In-Group Out-Group Bias and the False-Consensus Effect*

Dobelli's chapter on in-group out-group bias reports one of the most reliable and
most depressing findings in social psychology: **group loyalty forms
instantaneously, on arbitrary grounds, and immediately distorts judgement.**

Henri Tajfel's minimal group experiments are the demonstration. Divide people at
random — by a coin flip, by a preference between two abstract painters, by nothing
at all — tell them which group they are in, and they will immediately allocate
rewards to favour their own group, judge in-group members as more likeable and more
capable, and, most strikingly, accept a lower absolute payoff for their own group
in exchange for a larger *gap* over the other. No history, no contact, no
interests at stake. Just a label. Dobelli's summary: the group forms first and the
reasons are invented afterwards.

Religion is the most powerful in-group technology ever built, and it is worth
seeing the components laid out.

**The boundary is metaphysical and therefore absolute.** Ordinary group
divisions — nation, class, football club — are contingent and everybody knows it.
Religious divisions are stated as facts about the deepest nature of persons: saved
and unsaved, believer and unbeliever, the elect and the lost, umma and kafir, in
the light and in darkness. There is no stronger available formulation of us and
them, because it is not a claim about preference but about ontological status and
eternal destination.

**Out-group members are assigned a defect.** This is the part that should trouble
a thoughtful believer most. The doctrine does not merely say that unbelievers are
mistaken; it says something about their character. They are hard-hearted, in
rebellion, suppressing what they know, loving darkness, without hope, without a
moral foundation. Notice what this does: it makes it impossible to consider the
possibility that an honest, careful person examined the evidence and concluded
otherwise. The out-group is defined so as to be unable to constitute evidence. If
disbelief is by definition a moral failing rather than a conclusion, then no
disbeliever's reasoning ever has to be engaged, and the in-group's position is
sealed against precisely the information that could correct it.

**The in-group is genuinely, materially good to you.** Chapter 24 develops this
properly, and it must be said here because it is what gives the boundary its
force. These are the people who bring food when you are bereaved, who visit you in
hospital, who look after your children, who lend you money and do not mention it.
For a great many people the congregation is not one social circle among several —
it is, functionally, all of them: closest friends, extended family, spouse,
professional network, the people at the wedding and the people at the funeral.

Now compute what asking the question costs.

For someone embedded in that structure, "I don't think this is true" is not an
intellectual position. It is a threat to every relationship they have. It risks a
marriage, in traditions where a believing spouse is expected. It risks contact
with grandchildren. In high-boundary groups it is enforced explicitly: formal
shunning among the Jehovah's Witnesses and the Exclusive Brethren, disfellowship,
excommunication, the amputation of an entire life. In gentler traditions it is
enforced by nothing more brutal than a change in how people look at you, which is
quite sufficient. And in the harshest cases the penalty is not social at all — the
number of countries where apostasy remains a capital offence is not zero.

This is the central structural argument of the whole book, so let me state it
without hedging. **In a system where the social cost of reaching one conclusion is
catastrophic and the cost of reaching the other is zero, the conclusions people
reach carry no evidential weight.** Not because they are dishonest — because the
scales were loaded before they started. When you learn that a jury was told that
one verdict would cost them their families, you do not treat their verdict as
information about the defendant. You cannot use the near-unanimity of a
congregation as evidence for God, for the same reason you cannot use an election
result from a country with one legal party.

And the false-consensus effect, Dobelli's related chapter, explains why nobody in
the group perceives any of this. We systematically overestimate how widely our
views are shared — the research finds people projecting their own positions onto
the general population by wide margins. Inside a religious community, where the
in-group is most of your social world, this becomes total. Belief appears to be the
human default, and unbelief a strange minority perversion requiring explanation.
Then the group's own account of outsiders fills the gap, and it is an account
composed entirely of people who have never spent an unguarded evening with an
atheist.

The best available test is what happens to people who leave, and here the
in-group's prediction is checkable. Doctrine holds that those who leave will
descend into despair, meaninglessness, moral collapse. The follow-up literature on
apostates, and the enormous accumulated testimony of the deconversion
communities, reports something different and duller: a period of real grief and
disorientation, usually one to three years, driven overwhelmingly by *social
loss* rather than by metaphysical emptiness — and then, in most cases, a life. The
pain is real and this book will not minimise it. But it is the pain of losing a
community, which is what leaving a community feels like. It is not evidence that
the doctrine was true.

**In conclusion:** before trusting any conclusion — yours or anyone's — price the
alternatives. Ask what it would cost you socially to reach the opposite one. If
one answer costs you nothing and the other costs you everything you have, then
your belief is not the output of an inquiry, and no amount of sincerity fixes
that. Then ask the harder version: do you actually know any thoughtful
unbelievers, or only your group's description of them?
