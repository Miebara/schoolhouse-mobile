## 21. When Prophecy Fails

### *Cognitive Dissonance*

Dobelli's chapter on cognitive dissonance uses Aesop's fox, who cannot reach the
grapes and decides they were sour anyway. The mechanism, from Leon Festinger's
1957 theory: holding two incompatible cognitions is aversive, and we resolve the
discomfort by adjusting whichever one is cheapest to adjust. Usually that is the
belief, not the behaviour — and never the sunk investment. Dobelli's practical
example is the man who buys an expensive car that turns out to be unreliable and
becomes its most passionate defender.

The founding fieldwork for this theory is, as it happens, a study of religion, and
it is the single most important piece of research in this book.

In 1954 Festinger and two colleagues joined a small Chicago group led by a woman
he called Marian Keech, who reported messages from beings on the planet Clarion.
The message was specific and dated: on 21 December, a flood would destroy the
world, and at midnight a flying saucer would collect the faithful. This is an ideal
experiment, because the prediction was unambiguous and unhedged. The members acted
on it in ways that made retreat expensive: they quit jobs, gave away possessions,
left spouses, and removed all metal from their clothing per instructions.

Midnight passed. Nothing. The researchers watched several hours of acute distress.
Then, at 4:45 a.m., Keech received a new message: the group's faith and light had
been so great that God had spared the world. The catastrophe had been *called off
because of them*.

Festinger's prediction, made in advance, was the counterintuitive part and it held:
**the group did not disband — it began proselytising.** Members who had previously
avoided publicity started calling newspapers. Having failed the most direct test
imaginable, the belief did not weaken; conviction and evangelism both increased.

Festinger's explanation is the core of the theory. When a belief is disconfirmed
but the investment in it is enormous and the social support is dense, abandoning
it is the most expensive option available. So the cheaper resolution is chosen:
reinterpret the disconfirmation, and then recruit — because every new person who
agrees restores some of the social proof that the failure destroyed. Proselytising
after disconfirmation is not confidence. It is repair work.

Once you have this, the history of religion reads differently.

**The Millerites, 1844.** William Miller calculated Christ's return for 1843, then
22 October 1844. Perhaps fifty thousand people sold property, settled debts,
abandoned crops. The day passed and became known as the Great Disappointment. The
movement did not vanish; it produced the Seventh-day Adventist Church, now around
twenty million members, via the doctrine that what occurred on that date was an
*invisible* event in heaven — a rescheduling into unfalsifiability of exactly the
kind Chapter 8 describes.

**The Jehovah's Witnesses.** Expectations were attached to 1914, 1925 and, most
damagingly, 1975. Each failure was followed by reinterpretation and by literature
revised rather than retracted. The organisation grew.

**The New Testament itself.** This is the case that ought to matter most to a
Christian reader, and it is not a sceptic's invention — it is mainstream New
Testament scholarship. The earliest texts expect the return within the lifetime of
the hearers: *this generation will not pass away*, *we who are alive and remain*,
*the time is short*. Paul advises against marriage on the basis that the present
order is ending imminently. It did not end. And you can watch the adjustment
happen inside the canon: by the later texts the delay is being explained rather
than expected — with God a day is as a thousand years, the delay is patience, no
one knows the day or the hour. The most consequential failed prophecy in Western
history was managed by precisely the mechanism Festinger observed in a Chicago
living room, and the result was a religion that has now lasted two thousand years
on a promise of imminence.

Now bring it down to the scale of an individual life, because this is where the
chapter earns its place. Cognitive dissonance is why the arguments in the previous
twenty chapters may make things worse rather than better.

A man prays for two years for his wife's recovery. She dies. He has two
incompatible cognitions: *God answers prayer*, and *she is dead*. The available
resolutions are ranked by cost. Abandoning the belief costs him his community, his
identity, his hope of seeing her again, and the meaning of the two years he spent
praying — a catastrophic price, paid at the worst moment of his life. Reinterpreting
costs almost nothing: God had a better plan, she is at peace, he answered
differently. Which does he choose? And having chosen, is he now more or less
committed than before?

More. Reliably more. This is why bereavement so often deepens faith rather than
breaking it, and why the failure of the most fervent prayer of someone's life
functions as a strengthening event. It is also why arguing with a believer at the
moment their belief is under strain tends to backfire — you are adding pressure to a
system whose cheapest available response is to increase conviction. Chapter 26 draws
the practical conclusion: this is not something you can do to another person. The
only case where the cost structure is favourable is your own, on an ordinary day,
when nothing has just happened.

**In conclusion:** notice the moments when a belief of yours has just been
contradicted, and watch what your mind does in the following minutes. It will not
feel like rationalising; it will feel like remembering something important you had
overlooked, and the relief will arrive fast. Write down, in advance, what you would
say if the prayer failed. Then, when it fails, read what you wrote. That gap
between the two — the one you predicted and the one you produced — is dissonance
reduction, caught in the act. It is the closest you will ever get to watching your
own mind decide what to believe for reasons that have nothing to do with truth.
