## 6. God as the Answer to "Why?"

### *The Need for Causality, False Causality, and the Fallacy of the Single Cause*

Dobelli devotes three separate chapters to our relationship with causation, which
tells you how much damage it does. The first is our *need* for causality: we
cannot tolerate an uncaused event, so we supply a cause, and the supplied cause
is judged by how much relief it delivers rather than by whether it is true. The
second is false causality: we read correlation as causation with almost no
resistance. The third is the fallacy of the single cause: complex outcomes have
tangled, multiple, interacting causes, and we insist on one.

His illustration of the first is superb and worth borrowing. Newspapers report
that the stock market fell "on fears of a slowdown in China." The journalist does
not know why the market fell — nobody does, because it is the aggregate of
millions of decisions — but "the market fell 1.2 percent and we don't know why"
is unpublishable. So a cause is manufactured to satisfy a need, and it is
manufactured *daily*, and it is believed, and it is forgotten. Try this for a
week: read the reason given for each day's market move, and notice that the same
Chinese slowdown explains a fall on Tuesday and a rise on Thursday.

God is the same manufacture, performed on the largest possible scale.

Take the classic form of the cosmological argument: everything has a cause, so
the universe has a cause, and that cause is God. Set aside for a moment whether
the premise is true (in quantum field theory it is at best contested), and notice
what the argument *does*. It is driven entirely by intolerance of a brute fact.
And then it commits the very error it was invoked to prevent, because it stops.
If everything requires a cause, so does God; if something can be uncaused, the
universe can be. Every version of this argument therefore contains an exemption
clause written specially for the conclusion — Dobelli would file this under
confirmation bias, and Chapter 8 will come back to it. The argument does not
explain the universe. It renames the mystery and then declares the renaming to be
an explanation, which is the psychological function it was recruited for in the
first place.

The everyday version is more consequential than the philosophical one. A woman's
son dies at nineteen. The need for causality is not an intellectual itch here; it
is an unbearable pressure, and "a drunk driver was on that road at that minute
and there is no reason it was him" is a sentence the mind will do almost anything
to avoid. So a cause is supplied: God has a plan, he needed him, there is a
purpose we cannot see. Notice that this explanation explains nothing — it
specifies no mechanism, makes no prediction, and could be attached to any
outcome whatsoever, including the opposite one. Its entire content is relief. I
do not say this to mock the woman. I say it because the strength with which a
grieving parent holds that belief is routinely offered as evidence for it, and
it is evidence of nothing except how much pressure the need for causality can
generate.

False causality then supplies the day-to-day maintenance. You prayed and the job
came through. You tithed and the unexpected cheque arrived. You stopped going to
church and things fell apart. Each of these is a correlation of one, in a life
containing thousands of events, with no control condition and no counting of the
misses. Dobelli's example is the medieval belief that lice cause good health —
observed correlation: healthy people had lice, sick people didn't. The
correlation was real. The direction was backwards; fevered bodies are too hot for
lice. Nobody in that village was stupid. They had a correlation and no control
group, which is the same equipment you have when you conclude that the tithe
produced the cheque.

The fallacy of the single cause finishes it off. Why is that man an alcoholic?
Why did the marriage survive? Why did the country descend into war? The honest
answer in each case is a web of a dozen partial causes with feedback loops, none
sufficient, most unmeasurable. Religion offers instead a single cause with
unlimited explanatory reach — sin, or grace, or God's will — and single causes
are enormously easier to hold in mind. That ease is the entire appeal, and the
ease is not a virtue. An explanation that covers everything with one term has the
same information content as no explanation, because it cannot distinguish between
cases. If God's will explains both the recovery and the death, it explains
neither.

There is a deeper point hiding here, and it is the one I would most like you to
keep. The demand for a *why* behind existence feels like the deepest question a
person can ask. But look at where the demand comes from: from a mind built by
Chapter 4's agency-detector, which is designed to answer *who did this and what
did they want*. That machinery is superbly adapted to a world of rivals and
allies and hopeless when pointed at the origin of spacetime. "Why is there
anything?" may be a question of the same shape as "what is north of the North
Pole?" — grammatically impeccable, and referring to nothing. That is a genuinely
unsettling possibility. It is not made less likely by being unsettling.

**In conclusion:** when you are handed a cause, ask what it predicts that its
denial would not. If the answer is nothing — if the same cause would have been
cited had the outcome been reversed — then you have been handed relief and
charged for information. Sometimes there is no why. "I don't know" is not a
lesser answer than a comforting one. It is the only honest description of most of
what has ever happened to you.
