# Chapter 8. The Year of Weeping

Grief can be an accident of geography. In December 2005 it
concentrated, with terrible precision, on the parents of one school.

But the weeping of the year began seven weeks earlier, on the night
of 22 October, when Bellview Airlines Flight 210, a Boeing 737 bound
from Lagos to Abuja with 117 people aboard, took off into a line of
thunderstorms and, within minutes, ceased to exist as far as the
Nigerian state could tell. Not crashed. Vanished. Contact was lost
in the climb. Then, for the better part of a night and a morning,
the Federal Republic of Nigeria could not locate a Boeing 737 that
had fallen within a few dozen miles of its largest city. The search
looked in the wrong places. At one stage officials announced a crash
site over a hundred kilometers away, and families began driving
toward a place where nothing had happened. The real site was found
the next morning by villagers at Lisa, in Ogun State, barely thirty
kilometers from the airport. A crater in the forest. The aircraft
driven into the earth nearly vertically. No survivors. No fire
visible above the canopy to guide anyone in the night. The
passengers had been dead since minutes after takeoff. The search had
been a search for the situational awareness of the state itself, and
it took twelve hours.

Seven weeks later, on the afternoon of 10 December, Sosoliso
Airlines Flight 1145 from Abuja arrived over Port Harcourt in heavy
weather. Aboard were 110 people, and their composition is the fact
around which Nigerian memory of the year has crystallized ever
since. Sixty were students of Loyola Jesuit College in Abuja, one of
the finest secondary schools in the country, flying home as a group
for Christmas in the custom of a boarding school nation whose
children commute by air. On approach, in a thunderstorm, the
aircraft hit windshear, struck the ground short of the runway, broke
up along the grass, and burned. And then the second disaster began.
The airport fire service reached the burning wreck late, and reached
it, by the convergent testimony of survivors and inquiry, without
adequate water or foam. Passengers who survived the impact died in
the fire. One hundred and eight people were lost. Two survived,
among them a teenage girl, Kechi Okwuchi, whose long recovery from
her burns, and eventual appearance years later singing on an
American television stage, became the one publicly bearable thread
of the tragedy.

Sixty children of one school. A headmaster reading names at a carol
service that became a funeral. A mass grave on the school grounds
because some of the children could not be told apart. The Loyola
parents did not disperse into private grief. They organized. Their
organization, more than any other single force, converted 2005 from
another entry in the ledger of sorrow into the political crisis that
finally broke the system open.

## The night of the search

The hours after Flight 210 disappeared deserve reconstruction at the
pace they were lived. No other dataset shows every component of the
disaster machinery of the state, under load, at once.

At the moment contact was lost, the system knew, or held the means
to know, a departure time, a cleared route, a last radar return, and
a fuel endurance. The circle of possibility was small. What the
system did with it was nothing coherent. The airline reported the
aircraft missing. The night duty layers of the agencies activated as
separate organisms, each calling its own chain, none commanding the
others. No joint rescue center took charge. Into the vacuum came the
new information ecology of phones and morning radio. A report placed
the crash near a town in Oyo State, three hundred kilometers north.
Officials repeated it. The machinery lurched toward it. For much of
the night the Republic searched the wrong forest at the wrong end of
the country while the actual wreckage smoldered under canopy thirty
kilometers from the departure runway. The farmers of Lisa found it
at first light, walking toward the smoke their government had flown
past. For hours more the site belonged to whoever arrived.
Villagers. Journalists. Crowds. The state reached its own
catastrophe behind the general public.

The counterfactual gives the night its full weight. The impact at
Lisa was unsurvivable. But nobody knew that during the night. Had
the aircraft gone down in a survivable configuration, everyone alive
at impact would have owed their outcome to a search that took twelve
hours to find a Boeing inside the Lagos commuter belt. Search and
rescue is insurance written before the event. The policy of Nigeria,
the night revealed, had lapsed decades earlier, and nobody had told
the passengers paying premiums in trust.

## The school

Loyola Jesuit College sits on the Abuja plain like the argument for
Nigerian possibility made in brick. A Jesuit boarding school, founded
in 1996, that had become within a decade one of the academically
fiercest institutions in the country. Its families were the
professional class in miniature. Civil servants and doctors,
officers and entrepreneurs, north and south, Muslim and Christian in
a school run by Catholic fathers. The December flight was a ritual
of that world. The school run south for Christmas, seats booked in
blocks, younger children chaperoned by older ones.

Sixty of the students boarded 1145. The scale of the loss has few
peacetime parallels anywhere. Whole class cohorts halved. Sibling
pairs taken together. The choir, the debate team, and the football
side each with specific nameable holes. The Jesuits buried the
unidentifiable together on the school grounds. That decision,
wrenching at the time, created the memorial garden where the grief
of the community found its permanent address.

What the Loyola families did next distinguishes 2005 from every
prior chapter. They organized as the school community, not as
scattered claimants. Among them were senior lawyers who understood
process, officials who understood ministries, and mothers who
understood, as one told the press, that they had nothing left to
threaten. They formed associations. They attended every adjournment
of every proceeding. They built memorial scholarship funds. They
made themselves the permanent institutional witness of the disaster.
They demanded the Sosoliso report and got it. They turned each
anniversary into an audit. Organized, articulate grief is the only
force in this history that has ever outlasted a news cycle. In 2006,
when the aircraft of a Sultan fell, it was waiting, fully
assembled.

The survivors complete the story of the school. Two people lived
through 1145, both terribly burned. The younger, Kechi Okwuchi,
endured years of reconstructive surgery abroad, because Nigerian
burn medicine could not provide it. That too is a finding. Her
testimony about the fire, the impact survivable, the flames arriving
before help, is the survival section of the official report given a
voice. It has done more than any document to keep the dry fire
trucks of Port Harcourt in the national memory. A country that will
not read reports will sometimes still listen to a survivor.

## Two timelines

Flight 210. The 737 departed Lagos at night into known thunderstorm
activity. Within about three minutes, radar and radio contact ended.
No distress call. The aircraft came down at extreme velocity and
steep angle. The flight data recorder was never recovered in usable
condition. The cockpit voice recorder yielded nothing. The
investigation, deprived of its two primary witnesses, could never
establish the sequence. Storm upset. Instrument failure. Spatial
disorientation. Some compound of them. The final report could only
bound the possibilities. Flight 210 is the crash about which Nigeria
knows least, and the proof that a state which cannot find a wreck
for twelve hours and cannot read a recorder from its own soil has
forfeited even the consolation of understanding its disasters.

Flight 1145. The aircraft flew a stormy afternoon approach into Port
Harcourt. The investigation found it descended into windshear from a
thunderstorm cell over the field. The crew continued the approach
when the conditions called for abandoning it. The go around was
available and not taken. The aircraft touched down catastrophically
short. The impact was survivable for many. The fire was not, because
the response failed in speed, in equipment, and in extinguishing
agent. The detail Nigeria has never forgotten. Fire trucks that came
without water.

## The boxes that would not speak

The recorders of Flight 210 failed the investigation, and the
failure carries a policy lesson precise enough to legislate from.

Recorders are built around armored memory units rated to survive
defined crash forces and an hour of fire. Rated, that is, against
the envelope of typical accidents. A near vertical high speed impact
into hard laterite concentrates energy at the extreme edge of any
rating. The units recovered from the crater emerged with damage
beyond the readout capacity then reachable by Nigeria. No domestic
laboratory existed. The foreign laboratories work miracles routinely
but not always. The era compounded the loss. Recorders of that
generation captured fewer parameters, and the site custody of the
first days, a looted and trampled excavation, degraded what
remained.

Had 210 carried the recorder fit of a decade later, or had the site
been secured, or had a regional readout laboratory existed, this
chapter would contain a causation section. The translations are
direct. Recorder standards are worth regulating ahead of minimums in
high risk registries. Site custody is a trainable discipline. And a
regional African recorder laboratory, long proposed and perennially
unfunded, would repay its cost with the first mystery it retired.
Nigeria has since built real readout capability in Abuja. The
families of 210, fifteen years too early, received instead the word
undetermined. That is what infrastructure gaps are called after they
happen to someone.

## The go around that was not flown

The Sosoliso cockpit in its last ninety seconds held a decision
structure that psychology has since mapped so well it has a name in
every training syllabus. Plan continuation bias. The deepening
commitment to a course of action as the evidence against it
accumulates, strongest exactly when the plan is nearly complete.

An approach is the textbook habitat. The crew has briefed it and
configured for it. The runway is two minutes ahead. Every new
adverse cue arrives incrementally. None crosses the threshold that
would have forbidden the approach outright had it appeared at the
start. The go around, meanwhile, is priced by the brain as an event.
Fuel. Delay. Explanation. The subtle confession of misjudgment. The
continuation is priced as nothing. It is the plan.

The industry answer, hardened worldwide in exactly the years after
2005, was to abolish the judgment call. Defined gates on every
approach. Speed, configuration, descent rate. Any deviation mandates
the go around. No diagnosis permitted. The decision is made in the
briefing room where thinking is cheap, not at four hundred feet
where it is impossible. Airlines that enforce the doctrine have
driven approach accidents, historically the largest category, toward
the floor. The crews of 1145, and ten months later of 053, flew
before it reached Nigerian operations. Between them the two crashes
taught the industry the sentence its training departments now repeat
as liturgy. A go around is never an admission of failure. It is the
system working.

## The investigations

The two inquiries mark the exact state of Nigerian accident
investigation at its lowest point. After the decay. Before the
reform.

The Bellview investigation failed forensically, as described, and
the failure had architecture behind it. Recorder recovery was a
capability question. Site management degraded evidence
irrecoverably. The final report, when it surfaced years later, was
an honest document about its own defeat. Its real finding, never
phrased this way, was that Nigeria had been operating a jet airline
system without the investigative infrastructure the world treaty
assumes. The duty to investigate had been budgeted as a contingency
to be improvised when the day came. The day had been coming
annually.

The Sosoliso report was completable and completed. Windshear.
Continued approach. Inadequate emergency response. Findings clear
enough to act on. Note what the two reports jointly established
about the state in 2005. It could not reliably prevent. It could not
find. It could not rescue. And in the worst case it could not even
explain. Prevention, response, and explanation are the three duties
a state owes the flying public. 2005 was the year Nigeria was seen,
by its own citizens, in real time, to be failing all three at once.
That comprehensive visibility is what made 2005 politically
different from everything before it.

## The operator and the mystery

Bellview deserves a closer look, because its profile complicates the
morality tale. By 2005 it was the closest thing the deregulated era
had produced to a success story. A private carrier that had outlived
the cull of the 1990s, expanded into regional international routes,
passed the industry operational audit, and cultivated exactly the
business traveler respectability ADC had owned a decade earlier. The
airline the public boarded that October was the reputational upper
tier of the industry.

That is what made the aftermath so corrosive. The forensic defeat
meant no authoritative account ever separated the possibilities, and
into the permanent ambiguity flowed everything an information famine
supplies. Sabotage theories. Hidden structural failure. Causes known
and suppressed. The airline, brand destroyed, staggered into decline
and ceased operations within a few years, its corporate records
dispersing with it, which guaranteed the questions would never
close.

For the argument of this book, the Bellview mystery performs one
essential function. It shows that investigative capacity is not a
service to blame. It is a service to innocence. Had the state owned
the laboratory and the site discipline of a mature system, the
likeliest beneficiaries would have been the professionals of the
airline itself. The crew who could not defend themselves. The
engineers suspected forever without evidence. In the absence of
findings, everyone is convicted of something by someone, forever.
The families received no cause. The professionals no vindication.
The public no reason to believe that boarding the best airline of
the era differed from boarding the worst. That last conclusion,
statistically false and experientially irresistible, may have been
the deepest damage of the season. A public that cannot tell good
operators from bad ones cannot reward safety with custom, and the
market loses its one organic safety instrument.

## The children of the boarding school nation

One structural fact beneath the Sosoliso tragedy converts terrible
chance into systemic finding. The children were on that plane
because of how the Nigerian elite educates.

Nigeria is a boarding school nation. A legacy of colonial education,
reinforced by unity schools that deliberately mix the regions, and
by the dispersal of the professional class across a federation whose
distances defeat weekend parenting. The result by the 2000s was
that at every term boundary thousands of minors crossed the country
in concentrated waves on predictable dates. The school run. Every
airline scheduled for it. Every parent of means funded it as a
safety purchase, the flight being judged, reasonably, safer than the
highway.

The system had thereby aggregated its most precious and least
consenting passengers into peak density cohorts, and attached to
that concentration no special diligence at all. No enhanced dispatch
conservatism for school wave flights. No seasonal weather review for
the December wave flying into the turn of the seasons. The tenth of
December 2005 was the arithmetic of that inattention. One aircraft.
One storm. One continued approach. Sixty children of one school,
because the concentration that made the school run efficient made
its worst case unbearable. Schools and parents diversified bookings
across flights in later years. Grief performed, once again, the risk
management the system had not. A transport system is trusted with
whatever the structure of its society loads onto it. Its diligence
owes proportion to the cargo. The boarding school nation loaded its
children. The system priced them as seats.

## The winter of accounting

The weeks between the two crashes, and the weeks after the second,
deserve their chronology, because they show a state deciding under
maximum pressure how much to change.

After Bellview the machinery produced its standard outputs. Mourning
declared. A probe announced. The minister summoned. Officials
contradicting each other on consecutive days. But two nonstandard
things happened. First, the press did not move on. The private
broadcasters and the matured newspapers kept the search fiasco and
the radar questions on the front pages for weeks, and began the
cumulative coverage that converts events into a record. Second, the
international layer engaged. The ICAO audit cycle was already
scheduled. Foreign carrier concerns about Lagos were leaking into
the trade press. The World Bank transport review was circulating.
The state was being watched by audiences it could not manage.

Sosoliso detonated inside that charged field. Within days the
president was at Port Harcourt and at the school, photographed amid
sixty caskets. Within weeks the government convened the aviation
summit the industry had requested for a decade, with terms of
reference that became the skeleton of the 2006 law. Regulator
autonomy. Investigation independence. Infrastructure funding. The
professional diaspora was recruited into the drafting. The
international agencies were invited into the room. And the political
class, facing elections with the images of 2005 fresh, discovered
that aviation reform had become that rarest commodity. A policy with
no organized opposition. Even the operators the coming purge would
kill could not argue publicly against reform across the graves of
sixty children. The window that the royal funeral of 2006 would
fling fully open was, by January, already unlatched. States change
when the cost of not changing is made legible to the people who
govern them. The terrible achievement of 2005 was legibility.

## The second disaster

Chapter 13 treats emergency response in full. Here the evidence of
2005 needs only precise entry, because it is the strongest in the
book.

Search and rescue first. The twelve hours to Lisa were not a
technology failure. The aircraft crashed inside one of the most
densely populated regions in Africa. They were a coordination
failure among bodies whose disaster roles had never been drilled,
under a doctrine never written, with communications improvised
across personal phone networks. The benchmark to hold. Mature
systems measure the interval from lost contact to dispatched rescue
in minutes, because the survivable crash literature says the first
hour is where the saveable die.

Firefighting second. The international standard specifies, for an
airport of that size, response to any point of the runway within
about three minutes with rated quantities of water and foam. The
numbers are not decoration. They are calibrated to fuel fire physics
and the survival window of an intact cabin. The difference between
two minutes and ten is not eight minutes. It is everyone the fire
reaches second. The Sosoliso gap between standard and delivery was
therefore not a shortfall but a category change, and it had been
purchasable in advance for the price of trucks, maintenance, and
drills. The state had bought the vocabulary of the standard. The
airport was certified. The category was declared on paper. It had
not bought the substance. Certification as costume is the purest
expression of the pathology this book tracks, and 2005 displayed it
at the exact point where costume meets fire.

## What unsolved means elsewhere

The forensic defeat of Bellview invites one specific comparison.
What do strong systems do when they cannot determine a cause? The
American practice treats undetermined as a status requiring more
public process, not less. Dockets published. Hearings held.
Hypotheses tested openly. Interim safety recommendations issued
regardless. The designers understood that public confidence attaches
to the visible integrity of the process, not to the availability of
an answer. An unsolved crash handled openly leaves the institution
stronger. Handled opaquely, it converts every future assurance into
speculation. Bellview was the Nigerian test of that principle. The
decade long journey of the report to an inconclusive publication,
with families learning more from anniversary journalism than from
their government, taught the public a durable lesson in the opposite
direction. The reflexive conspiracy theories that attend every
Nigerian aviation event to this day are the interest on that
lesson. Trust, once taught to fail, compounds like debt.

## Reflection

Nations, like people, have years that divide their lives into before
and after. For Nigerian aviation 2005 is the wound that finally
taught, though the teaching required one more mass funeral to
complete. What 2005 changed was not the facts of the system, which
Chapters 3 through 7 had established in full. It changed the
witnesses. For the first time the failure was total, simultaneous,
televised, and inflicted on a constituency organized enough to
refuse consolation. The children of Loyola did not die because the
system of 2005 was worse than the system of 1996. They died because
it was the same. And the country, at last, could see it.

At the memorial garden on the school grounds sixty names are carved,
and visitors describe the particular silence of the place. It is the
silence this book has argued against from its first page. The
silence of the unpublished report. The unanswered hazard log. The
undrilled airport. The state that mourns fluently and reforms only
under compulsion. In October 2006, ten months after the carol
service that became a funeral, one more aircraft would fall,
carrying a king. The compulsion would finally arrive.
