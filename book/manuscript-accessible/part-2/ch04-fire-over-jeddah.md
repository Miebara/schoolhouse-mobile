# PART II. THE CRASHES 1990 TO 2020

# Chapter 4. Fire Over Jeddah

The pilgrims boarded in Jeddah wearing the white of ihram, the cloth
of consecration. Most were farmers and traders from northern
Nigeria at the end of the journey of their lives. The aircraft that
would carry them home wore three flags at once. Built in America.
Owned and crewed in Canada. Painted in the green and white of
Nigeria Airways. Departing a Saudi runway. It was 11 July 1991, the
morning already furnace hot, and Flight 2120 was one more rotation
in the annual machinery of the Hajj airlift. The flying Nigerian
institutions oversaw least, performed by strangers, under
arrangements nobody fully owned.

The aircraft was a DC-8, twenty three years old, operated by the
Canadian charter company Nationair under wet lease. A wet lease, to
define it once, means renting an aircraft together with its crew,
maintenance, and insurance. The plane flies under one flag while
answering in every working detail to another. Nigeria Airways, by
1991 unable to mount the Hajj surge from its own decaying fleet, had
contracted the lift. Nationair, a Montreal operator whose fast
growth had outrun its cash, needed the revenue badly. Two desperate
institutions met in a marketplace. Two hundred and sixty one people
boarded the result.

What happened next took twenty nine minutes from pushback to
impact. The evidence began building before the aircraft left the
ground.

## The operator, the contract, and the surge

To understand why the tires were soft, begin with why the aircraft
was there.

Nationair was a child of the deregulated 1980s. Founded in 1984, it
grew in seven years into one of the largest charter operators in
Canada. Its model was the model of every ambitious charter firm of
the era. Buy old aircraft cheap. Fly them hard. Chase the seasonal
spikes of the world, and above all the greatest spike, the Hajj.
Growth that fast is bought on margin. By 1991 the company finances
were strained and its labor relations poisonous. None of this was
hidden. The question the wreckage would pose was who in the world
system was responsible for reading that profile and acting on it.
The answer was everyone slightly and no one sufficiently.

The Hajj contract explains the pressure. Hajj lift is boom economics
squeezed into six weeks. Revenue arrives per flight flown. Every day
an aircraft sits broken, the costs run while the revenue stops, and
pilgrims with calendar bound visas pile up in terminals. The client
measures the contractor in departures achieved. Everything in the
design rewards flying and punishes stopping. Aviation has a word for
the force that is supposed to push back. Airworthiness. The flat
rule that the machine meets specification or does not move. Rules
like that survive only inside institutions built to enforce them
against money. At the Jeddah ramp in July 1991 no such institution
was present. The rule met the money alone and lost.

The Nigerian state was also in this transaction, as the charterer.
Its Hajj apparatus was itself improvised. Pilgrim boards of uneven
competence. Agent networks. A federal machine that measured success
in seats moved. The state wrote no real technical conditions into
its charter. It sent no oversight presence to the operation it had
bought. This was the starved regulator of Chapter 3 behaving abroad
exactly as it behaved at home. The wet lease did not defeat Nigerian
oversight. There was no Nigerian oversight to defeat. The lease
merely gave the vacuum a foreign address.

## The panorama. The gray middle of world aviation

Widen the lens. The world aviation of 1991 was two industries
wearing one name. The visible industry, the scheduled majors of the
rich world, was completing its great safety transformation. The
other industry lived in the gaps. Charter brokers and lease
merchants. Aging airliners moving through second and third
registries. Serving the demand the majors would not touch. Pilgrim
surges. Labor migrations. War zone freight.

This gray middle was not criminal, mostly, and not unregulated,
nominally. It was diluted across jurisdictions, often by design, so
that no single authority saw the whole picture. The early 1990s
were its golden age. What determined which customers bled was the
depth of scrutiny in the receiving state. The strong states skimmed
the genuinely serviceable capacity of the gray market. The weak
states took delivery of its risk. Flight 2120 was not an African
accident that happened to involve a Canadian airline. It was a gray
market accident delivered, as the worst ones systematically were, to
the customer least equipped to inspect the merchandise. The market
has since been substantially cleaned by lease rules, ban lists, ramp
inspections, and insurers. Making your country a hard place for the
gray market to land is among the most undervalued services a
regulator performs. In 1991 Nigeria performed the opposite.

## Timeline

The DC-8 taxied out heavy, near its maximum weight, loaded with 247
passengers, their belongings, fuel, and the July heat that thins air
and lengthens takeoff rolls. What neither the passengers nor in any
real sense the crew had been given the chance to weigh was the state
of the tires. Pressure checks days earlier had found two main gear
tires far below limits. The nitrogen rig needed to service them was
broken. Proper servicing would have delayed the rotation. Management,
under schedule pressure, let the aircraft keep flying.

An underinflated aircraft tire does not simply risk going flat.
Under load at speed it flexes beyond its design, and flexing rubber
makes heat with terrible efficiency. During the long hot takeoff
roll one tire failed. Its neighbor, overloaded, followed. Rims
ground along the runway in a plume of sparks. The crew, feeling
vibration but seeing no engine warning, flew the aircraft off the
runway and retracted the landing gear.

Into the wheel wells, packed among hydraulic lines and beneath the
cabin floor, came wheels that were on fire.

The DC-8 has no fire detection in its main wheel wells. The first
clear warnings arrived as system failures. Hydraulics dying.
Pressure faltering. Then smoke. The crew turned back toward Jeddah.
Behind them the fire burned through the cabin floor. In the final
minutes, as the aircraft descended toward the runway it had left
half an hour before, the floor failed progressively. Witnesses saw
bodies and debris fall from the burning aircraft along the last
miles. Control was lost short of the runway. All 261 aboard died.

It remains the deadliest accident in Canadian aviation history, the
deadliest involving a DC-8, and the deadliest single disaster ever
suffered by a Nigerian operation. Almost no public place in any of
the three countries commemorates it.

## The investigation

Jurisdiction followed the Chicago Convention. Saudi Arabia led the
investigation as the state where the accident occurred, with
Canadian and American participation. Nigeria, whose citizens made up
nearly all the dead and whose flag was on the fin, took part at the
margins.

The Saudi findings were unusually direct. The aircraft had been
dispatched with tires known to be below limits. Maintenance staff
had flagged the problem. Serviceable equipment was unavailable. And
management, under commercial pressure, permitted continued operation
rather than ground the aircraft. The technical chain was established
in forensic detail. The accident became a canonical case study,
taught worldwide to this day, of how a minor ground handling economy
becomes a hull loss. The fatal decision was made not in a cockpit
but at a desk, days earlier, by someone who never boarded.

Nationair did not long survive its reputation. It collapsed into
bankruptcy in 1993.

## What the industry learned

The global profession did learn from 2120. The accident became a
foundation stone of maintenance human factors training. It supplied
the standard answer to a question every maintenance organization
must decide in advance. What happens in our company when the correct
action is unavailable and the schedule is waiting. The Jeddah
answer, fly it anyway, entered the textbooks precisely because tires
had seemed so minor. The lesson generalized. There are no minor
systems on a loaded transport aircraft. Only systems whose failure
modes have not been imagined vividly enough. Tire pressure
monitoring spread on later designs. Dispatch rules tightened around
landing gear defects.

Note the asymmetry that defines this chapter. The international
learning loop closed. Montreal and Washington converted Jeddah into
worldwide practice within a few years. The national loop never
engaged. No Nigerian public inquiry examined how the national
carrier came to place a quarter thousand citizens on an aircraft
whose operator was visibly strained. No reform of charter oversight
followed in Lagos. A state can ride free on the physics lessons of
world aviation, because those travel inside the airframe. The
governance lessons have to be built at home.

## The dead

Unfold the number, because numbers this size defeat the
imagination.

Two hundred and forty seven passengers, drawn overwhelmingly from
Sokoto and its region. The Hajj pilgrim of that time and place was
typically not young and not rich. The pilgrimage came at the end of
decades of saving, often financed by selling land or cattle. Whole
village savings groups traveled together. Their deaths subtracted
not individuals but a stratum. The pilgrim as village elder, as
arbiter, as the person whose standing reorganized a community. They
died in the white of ihram, which is also the color of the shroud.
The north understood that terrible symmetry at once, and the
mourning carried a consolation the state found convenient. Grief
that resolves into the will of God files no lawsuits.

Fourteen crew. Canadians, mostly Montreal based. Their names are
recorded, their families litigation traceable, their memorials
locatable. The ordinary forensic dignity of a country whose
institutions count its dead. The asymmetry between the two halves of
the passenger list is not a detail of the aftermath. It is the
aftermath. The institutional weight of a death in this story depends
on which state was responsible for remembering the life.

In Sokoto the returning flights of that season landed into a city
where nearly every extended family had a claim on the list. No
bodies came home. The scholars resolved it as they had for centuries
of pilgrims lost at sea, with the funeral prayer in absence,
performed for the collective dead in the great mosque and repeated
through the wards. The federal delegation came, condoled, and left.
What remained was a specific residue in northern attitudes. The Hajj
flight became an act of faith in both senses, undertaken in trust of
God because trust of the system would have been eccentric.

## The accountability triangle

Wet leasing is not a scandal. It is a tool, and world aviation could
not function without it. The scandal is a tool used without a
jurisdiction to own its risks. Flight 2120 is the purest specimen in
this book of the accountability triangle. Three states each held a
corner of responsibility and the object at the center belonged to
none.

Canada licensed the operator and its maintenance, but the operation
ran nine time zones away under a contract Canada had no reason to
examine. Saudi Arabia owned the runway, but held only the blunt
instrument of ramp checks over a foreign transient. Nigeria owned
the passengers, the brand, and the contract, and had persuaded
itself that the whole point of chartering was to buy capacity and
shed accountability in one transaction. Each corner could point
honestly at the other two. The center, where 261 people sat, was
sovereign to nobody.

International law has spent the decades since narrowing this gap. An
amendment to the Chicago Convention now lets states formally
transfer oversight duties in lease arrangements. Serious
jurisdictions run foreign carrier assessment programs that treat a
foreign registry as the beginning of oversight rather than its end.
The reform, as usual in aviation, is written in the blood of the
uncompensated. The deeper Nigerian lesson is not about leases. A
state too weak to perform a function does not escape the risks of
that function by contracting it out. It reimports them, unregulated
and marked up. Nigeria in 1991 was buying safety oversight the way
it bought aircraft. Second hand, sight unseen, from whoever would
extend credit. Flight 2120 was the price discovery.

## The long settlement

Money is the coldest chapter of any aftermath and the most
revealing. The settlement geometry followed the accountability
geometry exactly.

The claims of the fourteen Canadian families proceeded through
Canadian channels against an airline sliding into bankruptcy, which
turned their cases into positions in a creditors queue. At least
the trail was documented. The claims of the 247 pilgrim families
entered a different universe. A lattice of insurers, the troubled
underwriting of Nigeria Airways, the political promises of the Hajj
apparatus, and the old Warsaw Convention liability caps, which
valued a human life at figures that read today as obscene. Families
navigated this from villages without lawyers, against counterparties
paid to wait. Partial disbursements came years late and diminished.
Other claims simply exhausted into silence. No Nigerian document
states anywhere what the families of Flight 2120 were finally paid.

The wider point outlives the case. Compensation is part of the price
system of safety. Where deaths cost carriers and states dearly, the
cost flows upstream into maintenance and dispatch decisions. Where
deaths are cheap, cheapness flows upstream too. The families of 2120
were failed three times. By a maintenance economy. By an oversight
vacuum. And by a settlement system that priced those failures at
almost nothing.

## What Nigeria could have done

It is fair to ask what a competent state would have done
differently. The answer fits on an index card, and every element
existed somewhere in 1991.

A charterer with functioning oversight writes technical conditions
into the contract and enforces audit rights. It stations a small
technical team with the operation, with authority to ground. It
requires its regulator, not its airline sales office, to approve the
operator. It prepares for the worst in advance. A manifest system
that knows who boarded. A family assistance protocol. A standing
arrangement for joining any foreign investigation. The cost of the
whole apparatus, per Hajj season, was less than the catering bill.
India ran a larger pilgrim airlift through the same decades with
approximately this machinery, imperfect but present.

The card was not expensive. It was institutionally impossible,
because every line presumes an agency able to impose costs on
politically connected commerce. Chapter 3 described what stood in
that place. The difference between the disaster that happened and
the routine season that could have happened was one institution able
to say no and make it stick. That is the cheapest and rarest
component in the whole machinery of flight.

## Two countries read one report

The most instructive comparison is not another crash but another
readership. What Canada and Nigeria each did with the same document.

In Canada the disaster brought press investigation, regulatory soul
searching, litigation, and a public record of books and
documentaries. The dead of Jeddah entered the national aviation
conscience. The operator was destroyed by exposure. Ask a Canadian
aircraft engineer about Nationair a generation later and you receive
a short course on dispatch pressure and the duty to say no.

In Nigeria, the state of the victims, the report went substantially
unread. It was not translated into policy, testimony, curriculum, or
memorial. The airlift continued under successor arrangements of
varying quality. One document. Two national digestions. The
difference between them is nearly the whole subject of this book.
Aviation safety is often described as a technology or a budget. It
is more fundamentally a reading practice. The institutional habit of
treating every report as addressed to oneself. Canada, which lost
fourteen citizens, read the report as an indictment of Canada.
Nigeria, which lost two hundred and forty seven, read it as news
about a Canadian airline.

## The Hajj after Jeddah

The airlift did not pause long. Through the 1990s and 2000s it
continued on the same architecture and kept generating incidents and
scandals. Stranded pilgrims became a near annual news genre. The
turn came when external requirement met domestic reform. The post
2006 regulator brought Hajj charters inside real certification
discipline. The Saudis tightened admission standards for the
airlift. A permanent national Hajj commission finally gave the
operation an institutional owner. The airlift of recent decades,
flown substantially by Nigerian carriers on modern aircraft, is one
of the least celebrated successes of the reform era, because its
success consists of the absence of news.

So the dead of 2120 did purchase their reforms. A generation late,
by the indirect route, without acknowledgment. The pattern recurs at
every crash in this Part. The system learns eventually, obliquely,
and the learning arrives unsigned, never traced publicly to the
event that taught it, never credited to the dead who paid its
tuition. A country that cannot connect its reforms to its
catastrophes cannot teach its own history. Memory is not sentiment.
It is the medium in which learning either persists or evaporates.

## Reflection

Every July the anniversary passes with little notice in the country
the pilgrims came from. There is a bitter symmetry in that silence.
They died of arrangements designed so that no one had the duty to
notice.
Flags borrowed. Oversight assumed away. Responsibility priced out of
the contract line by line.

Flight 2120 stands first among the seven crashes because it shows
the pattern naked, before weather or piloting can blur the view. A
known deficiency. A commercial pressure. A decision to defer. An
institutional vacuum where the refusal should have come from. Then
physics, which negotiates with no one. Every disaster that follows
will be more Nigerian in its mechanics. None will be different in
kind. The fire always starts in the part of the machine nobody can
see, that everybody deferred, that somebody decided could wait. By
the time it is visible from the cabin the only questions left are
for the investigators. The work of safety, like the work of
institutions, is done or not done in the dark, before the doors
close.

Fourteen months after Jeddah the pattern repeated in a different
costume. Not a leased airliner but a military transport. Not
pilgrims but the officer corps of the Nigerian armed forces. And not
a foreign report unread but a domestic one never written for the
public at all.
