# Chapter 10. Dana 992 and the Fragile Peace

Success in aviation safety is invisible. It is the crash that does
not happen, year after year. The question after 2012 was whether the
quiet skies of Nigeria were an achievement or an interval.

Sunday 3 June 2012, mid afternoon. Dana Air Flight 992, an MD-83
airliner, was inbound to Lagos at the end of the short hop from
Abuja. The same city pair as Bellview 210, flown in reverse, in a
country whose aviation had gone nearly six years without a scheduled
airline catastrophe. Dana was a success of the reformed market. An
Indian owned Nigerian carrier, audited to international standard,
flying a young fleet by Nigerian history, with a punctuality
reputation that had made it a business favorite. The paperwork on
Flight 992 was in order. What the paperwork did not show was that
the aircraft, as it descended toward the city, was dying.

Well before arrival, one engine had begun losing thrust, starved by
a fuel supply fault. The crew registered the anomaly and continued
toward Lagos. No emergency declared. No diversion to the nearer
field behind them. On final approach the second engine collapsed. An
airliner without engines is a glider of brutal arithmetic, and the
arithmetic came up two miles short. The aircraft struck a two story
building in Iju Ishaga, a dense residential quarter northwest of the
airport, and burned. All 153 aboard died, and at least six people on
the ground, with the true ground toll never established to anyone
satisfaction.

The crowd was on the wreck within minutes, in the largest and most
televised scene of citizen response in this book. Thousands filled
the streets, passing water, pulling at hot metal with bare hands,
while the professional response fought its way through its own crowd
and the Lagos traffic. Cranes able to lift the collapsed structure
arrived on a timescale of hours. The nation watched all of it live,
on the smartphones and satellite channels that had not existed in
2005.

## The afternoon, hour by hour

The response of 2012 was the response of the reformed system, and
both its differences from 2005 and its samenesses are findings.

The professional response was faster than 2005. The Lagos State
emergency agency, born of the investments made after that year, had
crews moving inside the hour. The federal agencies followed. And the
response was still defeated by the two variables no siren solves.
Access and equipment. The crowds that had beaten the responders to
the site now stood between them and it. The decisive deficit was
lift. The collapsed building needed heavy cranes, and the first
crane equal to the slab arrived after dark. Nobody aboard could have
been saved by speed. On the ground, under the slab, the crane
timeline was the negotiation, and no accounting of it was ever
published.

Two social facts from the afternoon carry the analysis. First, the
crowd was not incidental. Nigerians came because twenty years had
taught them that the state arrives late, that help is a neighbor,
and that presence is the only oversight. The crowd impeding the
rescue was the accumulated residue of every earlier failed response.
Distrust, embodied, at scale, complicating the very operation it had
gathered to compensate for. Second, the phones. 2012 was the first
fully documented Nigerian disaster. The fire, the crowd, the late
crane, all streamed and time stamped by hundreds of hands. The
information monopoly that had sealed 1992 and managed 1996 was
structurally gone. Whatever the institutions failed to learn from
Dana 992, the public now owned its own archive.

## The investigation and its tempo

The Accident Investigation Bureau performed the forensic core of its
task competently. Recorders recovered and read promptly. Engines
torn down to their fuel systems. A technical narrative established
that has not been overturned. Dual engine power loss traceable to
fuel system faults, compounded fatally by the failure of the crew to
treat the first sick engine as the emergency it was and land while
options remained.

Then the report entered the institutional atmosphere and slowed.
Interim statements came. The final report did not appear until 2017.
Five years, for an accident whose essential mechanics the
investigators understood within months. With 153 families, a
suspended and restored airline, and identical aircraft in national
service awaiting the formal lessons. The delay had no single
villain. It had the usual ecology. Capacity limits. Legal caution.
No actor empowered to demand speed. Strong states also miss the
international twelve month norm, but they publish loud interim
findings so the learning flows while the lawyers argue. The Nigerian
practice, silence and then a completed monument, preserved the form
of investigation while dissipating its function.

The regulatory aftermath showed the same half reformed signature.
The certificate of the airline was suspended within days. The
reformed reflex, unthinkable in 1996. It was restored within months,
amid public statements that did not fully align, and a popular
conviction, fair or not, that connections had flown the airline back
onto the schedule. The airline would be suspended and restored more
than once again before its grounding a decade later. Each cycle was
defensible on the record available. The pattern read, cumulatively,
as oversight by oscillation. A regulator strong enough to act, but
no longer backed enough to make its actions stick against appeal,
litigation, and political fatigue. The Demuren era ended in 2013.
The institution he rebuilt remained, incomparably better than its
ancestor. But the political scaffolding of the reform, the
presidential cover and the fear born of the funerals, had been
quietly disassembled by time.

## What the report finally said and what happened to it

When the final report emerged in 2017 its substance rewarded the
wait even as its tempo indicted it.

The findings assembled a textbook dual failure narrative. The fuel
supply degradation of the first engine, traceable through component
histories. The decision of the crew to continue rather than divert.
The collapse of the second engine drawing on the same fuel system.
And beneath the sequence the organizational layers. The maintenance
decisions on the sick engine in the days before. The dispatch and
pairing questions. The fatigue ecology of a punishing shuttle
schedule. The recommendations ran from the specific, inspection
regimes for the engine type, to the systemic, and one recommendation
carried the whole argument of this book inside it. Measures to
ensure that commercial pressure could not override maintenance
decisions. Each went to a named addressee.

Then the transmission question. What closed? The narrow technical
items, substantially yes. The fleet type aged out of Nigerian
service regardless. The systemic items entered the recommendations
register, and there the public trail thins. No published follow up
in which a citizen, a bereaved parent, or an underwriter could look
up whether the safeguards now exist in auditable form. The airline
flew on until its final grounding, through further incidents and
suspension cycles, each restoration accompanied by assurances whose
documentation the public never saw. The dead of Iju Ishaga
purchased, verifiably, some narrow technical protections, and
unverifiably, everything else. That ratio is the signature of the
plateau. Improving it requires no new catastrophe and no new money.
Only the publication, on a schedule, of the register the
investigator already keeps.

## The echoes. 2013 and 2021

Two further losses frame the fragile peace.

On 3 October 2013 a charter aircraft crashed on takeoff from Lagos,
killing most aboard. It was carrying the body of a former state
governor to his funeral. A detail of almost unbearable symbolic
economy. The funeral charter as casualty. The elite exit vehicle
failing exactly as the public system had, and for the same reasons.
The investigation found an aircraft that should not have been
dispatched and an operator whose condition the audits had not
caught. The improvement of the scheduled system had not reached the
charter periphery, where the oldest risks of Nigerian aviation have
always lived.

And in 2021 the military bill came due again, twice in three months.
In February a Nigerian Air Force turboprop crashed on approach to
Abuja, killing seven. In May another crashed at Kaduna in weather,
killing all aboard, including the Chief of Army Staff, the serving
commander of the Nigerian Army, and much of his personal staff.
Twenty nine years after Ejigbo the pattern reproduced itself with
terrible fidelity. An air force investigating itself. Findings
unpublished. A decapitated command. A civil reform whose writ still
stopped, as it had always stopped, at the barracks gate. No stronger
proof exists that the recovery was a civil achievement rather than a
national transformation. The domain the reforms reached got safe.
The domain they were never allowed to reach kept its 1992 physics.

The institutional response to the decade came in 2022, when the
investigation bureau was reborn as the Nigerian Safety Investigation
Bureau, the NSIB, with a mandate covering aviation, rail, and
marine, on the American model. On paper it is the most advanced
investigative architecture in Africa. Whether it becomes the real
thing will be read not in its founding law but in its publication
rate.

## The stress tests nobody scheduled

The plateau decade ran two system wide stress tests of its own.

The currency crises, in 2016 and again in the 2020s, reran the
dollar famine of Chapter 3 at modern scale. Ticket revenue trapped
in a falling currency. Lease, spares, insurance, and training
obligations firm in dollars. Foreign airlines had earnings marooned
in Nigerian accounts by the billion. The safety relevant finding is
what did not happen. No return of the geriatric fleet. No visible
collapse of maintenance discipline. No accident cluster. The floors
built in 2006, fleet age limits, financial surveillance, audited
operators, held under exactly the economic pressure that had
liquefied the 1980s system. Airlines died. Their passengers did not.
The quality of a regulatory system is measured precisely here, in
what its constraints refuse to yield when everything else is
yielding.

The pandemic tested the other flank. Nigerian aviation stopped
almost entirely, then restarted into a world of parked aircraft and
rusty crews, which the global safety community warned was itself a
hazard. The restart produced incidents but no catastrophe. Again a
qualified pass. Again the asterisk, because the military losses and
charter scrapes of the same period marked exactly where the reformed
perimeter ends.

The passenger experience of the plateau completes the picture
honestly. Nigerian domestic aviation in the 2020s is safe and shabby
at once. Schedules elastic. Cancellations chronic. Terminals
overloaded. Fares volatile with the currency. The consumer file now
generates more enforcement activity than airworthiness does. The
shabbiness matters for one reason. Public patience is finite and
undifferentiated. A public exhausted by delay does not distinguish
operational rot from safety rot. And a political class fishing for
applause in that exhaustion reaches, history shows, for exactly the
symbolic interventions this book warns against. The quiet decade
bought credibility that the check in desk is spending.

## The NSIB and its first shelf

The new bureau inherited a backlog and a laboratory, and its early
output suggested an institution that understood its own probation.
Final reports issued at a tempo its ancestor never sustained.
Preliminary statements published within weeks, in the international
format, on a website that works. First investigations into rail and
marine accidents, modest documents but precedents. Cooperation
agreements with the American and European agencies. A regional
ambition, offering its recorder laboratory to neighbors.

The honest asterisks remain. Tempo on the most sensitive files. The
untested legal shield for safety information. The follow up
register. The unbroached military perimeter. But the trajectory
matters for the ledger of this book. The investigative function, the
one most completely absent at Ejigbo and most tragically defeated at
Lisa, is at this writing the strongest performing safety institution
in the country. Of all the institutions audited in these pages, it
is the only one whose current decade is its best.

## The charter frontier

The 2013 crash marked the edge of the reformed perimeter. The
charter and private sector of the following decade proved the mark
durable, and its political economy explains why.

Nigerian business aviation grew into one of the largest markets in
Africa. Executive jets of the banking and oil aristocracy. Political
charters in campaign seasons. Helicopter shuttles in the delta. Its
structural feature is the hazard sign this book has taught. Its
passengers are the most powerful people in the country, and its
oversight is accordingly the most negotiated space in the system.
The recurring findings of the audits and dockets describe the
pattern. Private registrations flying commercial charters outside
certificate discipline. Foreign registered jets domiciled beyond the
practical reach of their registries. Duty records of ceremonial
accuracy. Enforcement campaigns that crest after each embarrassment
and recede as the calls are returned.

The analytical point was seeded in Chapter 6. The elite exit from
the public system did not exempt the powerful from institutional
quality. It re exposed them to the pre reform system they had funded
their way out of, because private oversight is exactly as strong as
the public regulator behind it. The powerful cannot secede from the
competence of the state. They can only choose which of its
jurisdictions, the audited or the negotiated, to entrust their
families to. The record of their choosing is an irony this book
leaves to its readers among them.

## What a plateau looks like

The honest ledger of Nigerian aviation after 2012 has two columns,
and both are real.

The credit column is substantial and too rarely stated. As of the
middle 2020s, Nigerian scheduled airlines have gone well over a
decade without a passenger jet catastrophe. The longest such run in
national history, sustained across a doubling of traffic, a currency
collapse, insurgency, pandemic, and the failure of several carriers.
The reformed core holds. Nigerians under twenty five have no memory
of the annual mass funeral that structured the relationship of their
parents to the departure gate. By the only measure that finally
matters, the reform worked and kept working.

The debit column is the pattern of this chapter. Five year reports.
Suspension oscillations. A thinly watched charter periphery. A
military sector untouched and repeating. The training college and
the inspectorate straining to hold staff against Gulf salaries. And
beneath all of it, the evaporation of the political constituency
that 2005 and 2006 briefly created. The system is no longer decaying
toward 1996. It is plateaued, maintained by professional inertia,
audit exposure, and institutional memory rather than by any active
political demand. Plateaus are respectable. But aviation is a domain
of moving baselines. Traffic grows. Fleets age. People churn. A
safety system standing still is, relative to its risk base, walking
slowly backward. The fragile peace is peace. It is also fragile, and
it knows it.

## Asiana 214 and the metabolism of a mature system

Thirteen months after Dana 992, an Asiana Airlines 777 crashed on
landing at San Francisco. Three dead, a modern rarity. The
comparison worth drawing is not casualty counts but metabolic rate.
Within days the American investigator was briefing the world on
recorder data, publicly, camera facing, hypothesis by labeled
hypothesis. Within a year, public hearings, a docket of thousands of
pages, a final report, and industry wide training changes. The
entire cycle from wreckage to reform completed inside the interval
in which the Dana file was still marinating.

That velocity is not wealth. It is design. Independence. Deadlines
that bind in practice. And above all a public facing culture in
which the investigation itself is the reassurance, its openness the
product delivered. A state whose citizens can watch it learn does
not need to ask for their trust. The Lagos crowds of 2012, pulling
at the wreckage themselves, trusting nothing they could not touch,
were the photographic negative of that proposition. The distance
between the two images is the remaining distance of the Nigerian
reform.

## Reflection

Part Two closes where it opened, with fire in a place fire should
not be, and with the question of what a country does next. Between
Jeddah 1991 and Iju Ishaga 2012 stretch seven disasters, some three
thousand dead, and a single demonstrated theorem. Nothing about the
dying was necessary. The same state, the same market, the same
professionals, rearranged after 2006 into institutions with money,
autonomy, and witnesses, stopped producing catastrophe within
eighteen months and have not resumed. The crashes were never fate.
They were policy in the strict sense. The predictable output of
arrangements chosen, renewed, and defensible at every point by
someone whose interests they served.

What remains, before this book can argue about the future, is to
understand the arrangements themselves. Not crash by crash but
pattern by pattern. The politics of the regulator. The economics
that made corner cutting rational. The emergency response deficit
that turned accidents into massacres. The culture of silence that
made every lesson single use. That anatomy is Part Three. Its
shortest summary can be given in advance. Institutions are how a
society remembers. Nigerian aviation history is what forgetting
costs.
