# Chapter 7. Kano Again

The people of Gwammaja never bought a ticket. The aviation system
crashed into them anyway.

Saturday 4 May 2002, early afternoon. In the dense old quarters
southwest of the Kano airport, the same city that met the biplanes
of the empire in 1925 and buried the pilgrims of 1973, the weekend
was at full volume. Markets open. A primary school in session.
Mosques filling toward prayer. Out at the airport, EAS Airlines
Flight 4226, a British built BAC 1-11 more than twenty years old,
began its takeoff roll for Lagos with over seventy people aboard,
among them a serving federal minister. The aircraft used a great
deal of runway, lifted off late and heavy, and then did the thing
witnesses described with terrible uniformity. It stopped climbing.
Staggering at rooftop height, engines laboring, it cleared the
airport fence, sank across the neighborhoods, cut a burning lane
through houses and shops, and came apart. The people in the cabin
had little chance. The people beneath had none. When the counting
was finally done, and in that packed informal city it was never
really done, the settled figure approached 148 dead. Roughly half
from the aircraft and half from the city under it. Kano again.

The BAC 1-11 was the British 727. A workhorse of the 1960s, rugged
and loud, and by 2002 extinct in the skies of the countries that
built it, banned by noise rules and retired by economics. In Nigeria
it was the backbone of the deregulated fleet. Chapter 3 explained
the supply side. Adjustment era dollars bought the aircraft the
world was discarding. This chapter is about the demand side. The
system that kept such aircraft in mass passenger service two
decades past their era. And about the third parties who carried the
risk without ever entering the transaction.

## The quarter and the carrier

Gwammaja first. The quarter belongs to the oldest continuously
inhabited urban fabric in West Africa. Family compounds, religious
schools, workshops, built at walking scale over centuries, its lanes
too narrow for a fire engine and never planned to need one. When the
airport was sited in the colonial era it stood well clear of the
walls. By 2002 a city of some three million had flowed around it, as
every African airport city has flowed around its runways. Aviation
planning has a vocabulary for managing this. Safety zones at runway
ends. Height controls. Land use rules under the climb paths where
accident probability concentrates. The vocabulary existed in
Nigerian regulation. Enforcement would have required the airport
authority, the state government, the city planners, and the emirate
land customs to act as one. No institution in this book has yet
managed to be one thing, let alone four. So the funnels filled with
people. The risk contours that engineers draw off the ends of a
runway lay across roofs that held sleeping families, and on 4 May
2002 the contours collected.

The carrier second. EAS was a mid tier operator of the second
deregulation wave, flying a small fleet of old jets on the trunk
routes at fares the market could bear. It was neither the worst
operator of the era nor close to the best. Its aircraft were legal.
Its crews were licensed. Its existence was certified annually by the
oversight system Chapter 11 dissects. That ordinariness is the
finding. Inquiries found no monstrous deviation. They found the
standard condition of the marginal carrier. Thin capital. Old
equipment. A loose weight culture in a system that was loose
everywhere. Engineering dependent on a bazaar of salvaged parts. EAS
was the median, and the median crashed. When the typical firm of a
system, operating typically, produces catastrophe, the actuarial
name for that system is not risky. It is priced to kill on schedule.

## The panorama. The bottom of the cycle

World aviation in May 2002 was an industry in shock. The September
11 attacks had cratered global traffic. The majors were parking
fleets in the desert and dumping their oldest metal at any price.
Insurance had repriced everything. And the attention of the world,
regulatory and journalistic, had swung to security and away from
airworthiness.

Every element bore on Nigeria. The parked fleets made elderly
aircraft cheaper than ever, refreshing the geriatric pipeline just
as Nigerian dollar positions were weakest. The insurance squeeze
pressed compliance budgets from the other side. And the scarce
international attention for African aviation now arrived asking
about cockpit doors rather than engine overhauls. Flight 4226 fell
at the precise moment when the global sorting machine ran at
maximum and the countervailing scrutiny at minimum.

The pattern repeats. The 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic
each reran the cycle. Gluts of retired aircraft. Distressed
operators. Weakened oversight budgets. Each time the thinnest
jurisdictions absorbed the surplus risk on schedule. The business
cycle of aviation safety is real and it runs backward. The hard
times of the industry are the most dangerous season of the weak
regulator, because the sorting accelerates exactly when inspection
contracts.

## Timeline

The last ninety seconds of 4226.

The aircraft taxied out near its weight limits on a hot Sahel
afternoon. Heat thins air. Thin air lengthens every takeoff roll and
flattens every climb. The roll was long. Rotation late. Almost at
once, power problems appeared. The evidence pointed to loss of
thrust on at least one of the two engines at the worst point in the
performance envelope. Too fast to stop. Too low and slow to
maneuver. Too heavy to climb on what remained. Procedure for the
case exists and is trained, but procedure assumes margins. Engine
out climb performance certified against obstacle maps. Clear zones
off the runway ends. The reality of Kano had spent decades diverging
from the certification assumptions. The city had grown to the fence
and past it. The clear zones of 1958 were the neighborhoods of
2002. The aircraft never achieved a climb. It descended across
roughly two kilometers of city and broke up in fire.

On the ground the response followed the national choreography with
one difference. Here the first responders were also the victims.
Gwammaja residents dug their neighbors and the passengers out of
the same rubble with the same hands. The airport fire service, yards
away as the crow flies, was blocked by walls and alleys never
planned for a response off the field. Hospitals overflowed within
the hour. The federal government declared mourning. National
attention held for the customary interval.

## The physics of a hot afternoon

The performance calculus that failed over Gwammaja deserves plain
language, because every passenger boarding a heavily loaded flight
from a hot climate airport sits inside it.

Air is the working fluid of an aircraft, and heat thins it. A wing
lifts and an engine thrusts in proportion to the mass of air each
can process. On a forty degree afternoon the atmosphere delivers
meaningfully less mass per cubic meter than the standard day the
brochures assume. Engineers handle this through density altitude,
the altitude at which a standard atmosphere would match the actual
air of today. On 4 May 2002 the density altitude at Kano stood
thousands of feet above the field. The aircraft sat on a Sahel
runway and performed as if departing a mountain airport. Every
increment compounds. Higher speeds needed for the same lift. Longer
roll to reach them. Less spare thrust for the climb. Thinner margin
if an engine falters at the worst moment.

Now degrade the assumptions the way the era degraded them. An old
engine, decades from the test cell, delivers a little less than
book. A load sheet built on standard weights in a baggage culture
that ran on negotiation is a little optimistic. The obstacle survey
in the file dates from a smaller city. Each softness is small,
deniable, and cumulative. The result is not an approximation of the
certified case but a different case never computed by anyone. On a
cool morning at light weight the same aircraft would likely have
flown away from the same engine trouble, and nobody would ever have
learned how little margin remained. Hot afternoons are the audit.
Margins die by being lent away in slivers, each borrower certain the
others left enough. The lesson runs far beyond aviation. Systems
reveal their borrowed margins only at load. A society that meets its
systems only in fair weather knows nothing about them.

## The investigation

The inquiry assembled a picture that surprised nobody in the
industry. An old aircraft at high weight on a hot day suffering
engine performance failure at rotation, with contested details of
weight documentation and engine history never fully resolved in
public. As with every accident in this Part, the technical narrative
is the least interesting layer. Three systemic findings deserve the
emphasis.

First, the gap between certification and reality. Every transport
aircraft is certified to survive an engine failure at the worst
moment, provided the assumptions hold. Accurate weights. Engines at
specification. Current obstacle data. Age and poverty erode each
assumption independently. None of the erosions is individually
fatal, which is why each is individually tolerated. Flight 4226 is
what their intersection looks like.

Second, the age question, which the crash forced onto the national
agenda. The regulator moved afterward toward one of the signature
policies of Nigerian aviation. A ban on importing passenger aircraft
beyond a fixed age. Economists and purists criticized it on solid
ground. Age is a crude proxy. Maintenance, not chronology, makes an
aircraft safe. The critics were technically right and politically
obtuse. The crudeness was the value. A bright line that a weak
regulator could enforce without the inspection depth it did not
possess. Where oversight capacity is scarce, rules must be cheap to
verify. That is a principle of institutional design that travels far
beyond aviation. The age rule was a crutch, adopted honestly as a
crutch, by an institution that knew it could not yet walk.

Third, the third party problem. Roughly half the dead were people to
whom the system owed its duties at their most absolute. They had
accepted no risk and signed no ticket. They appear in the account
books of aviation, if at all, as an externality. Their compensation
moved through channels even slower than those the passenger families
faced. The land use failure that put them under the climb path was
owned by no institution at all. The airport blamed the city. The
city blamed the state. The state blamed the federation.

## The month of May 2002

The crash fell into a country already bleeding. Nigeria that year
was absorbing the Lagos armoury explosion of January, in which over
a thousand people died, many drowned in a canal while fleeing. The
emergency response failures of that night prefigured everything
Chapter 13 systematizes. The Kano crash landed as a data point in a
national argument already raging. What exactly could this state
protect. The newspapers set the armoury canal beside the Gwammaja
rubble and drew the connection the government least wanted. Two
disasters, one military and one civil, sharing one anatomy.
Hazardous systems sited among the poor, maintained by deferral,
answered by mourning. When the aviation reform finally came in 2006
it drew on outrage banked across all of them. The armoury dead and
the Gwammaja dead collaborated posthumously in the indictment that
no single tragedy had made stick.

## The fence line, everywhere

The injustice of Gwammaja belongs to a global class. Every fast
growing city has besieged its airport. The London approaches pass
over two million people. The old Hong Kong airport threaded
apartment canyons. A Brazilian airliner overran a city center runway
in 2007 and 199 died. The difference between the managed sieges and
the lethal ones is an unglamorous apparatus. Safety zones bought and
kept empty at runway ends. Height rules enforced against every
crane. Risk models that count the people under the funnels. And a
land authority with the standing to say no to development money.

That apparatus fails partially even in strong states. What
distinguished Nigeria was its complete absence as a practiced
system. The rules existed on paper and no actor owned their
enforcement against the growth of any city. The reform era brought
the surveys current. The hard part remains untouched. No government
will soon bulldoze the poor from under funnels its own planning
vacuum invited them into. The realistic policy is forward looking.
The next airports must buy their safety zones now, at greenfield
prices, before the city arrives. Whether that happens is a precise
test of whether Gwammaja taught anything. The funnels of 2060 are
being zoned this decade.

## The ledger of the uncounted

What Nigeria owed Gwammaja and what it paid deserves its own
accounting, because the surface victim is the cleanest test of a
compensation system. No contributory questions. No ticket contract.
Pure liability.

International law had recognized the surface victim for half a
century. Nigerian practice ran otherwise. The insurance position of
the airline, adequate on paper, became a fortress of process. Claims
required documents the informal city does not generate. Title deeds.
Death certificates. Letters of administration. Adjusters valued
compound housing with formulas built for insured suburbs.
Settlements were offered at fractions and accepted from exhaustion
or litigated into the second decade. Government gestures receded
with the news cycle. Years later journalists found Gwammaja
households still holding files. The airline itself did not survive
the year, and its liquidation converted the remaining claims from
slow to moot.

The pattern generalizes and completes the argument. A liability
system is how an economy prices harm back onto its producers. Where
courts are slow, records informal, and defendants corporately
mortal, the price of harming third parties rounds to zero. A cost
that rounds to zero is a cost no spreadsheet will avoid. The
uncompensated of Gwammaja are a moral failure. They are also a
market signal, received clearly by every marginal operator. The
downside of falling on a neighborhood is survivable for everyone
except the neighborhood. Systems get the accidents they price for.

## The hospital day

The medical response deserves its record, because the pattern recurs
at every urban disaster in this book and has never been nationally
examined.

The hospitals learned of the crash the way the state did. From the
smoke and the crowds. Within the hour the major hospitals of Kano
received simultaneous waves no plan had allocated. The burned, the
crushed, and the walking shocked, delivered in taxis and pickup beds
by the same citizens who had dug them out, distributed by proximity
and rumor. No central function matched patient to resource. Burn
cases arrived at hospitals without burn capability while theaters
elsewhere stood ready. Families searched facility to facility for
days because no single casualty register existed. Blood ran short by
evening and was replenished the Nigerian way, by an appeal answered
magnificently by ordinary people queuing at donor benches. The staff
performed with improvised excellence. The system performed as
designed, which is to say it had not been.

The uncounted consequence was the second week mortality. The burn
and crush patients who survived the day and died of infection and
delay. No study ever counted them. Nigerian disaster deaths are
counted at the scene or not at all, which flatters every response
the statistics touch. That too is a finding. A state that does not
count its second week dead has decided that the post disaster
patient is a family project. The families of Gwammaja, who had
supplied the rescue, supplied the nursing too.

## Reaction. The political discovery of aviation

The politics of 4226 differed from every prior chapter in one
respect. Nigeria was now a democracy, three years old, with an
election a year away. The crash therefore received something Jeddah,
Ejigbo, and the lagoon never had. Political competition over its
meaning. The National Assembly held hearings and summoned officials.
The opposition framed the disaster as governmental neglect. The
press investigated with a freedom unimaginable a decade earlier. The
government produced probes, suspensions, the age rule, and pledges.

It is easy to be cynical, and most Nigerians were. But the
structural point matters. Accountability had acquired a market.
Under military rule a disaster was an information problem for the
regime. Under democracy it became a resource for the competitors of
the regime. Institutions reform when someone powerful profits from
their reform. The machinery was still weak. Hearings produced more
theater than statute. But the demand for accountability now existed,
and three years later, when the system faced its greatest test, that
demand was waiting. Mark 4226 as the hinge where the politics of
Nigerian aviation changed phase, even though its immediate harvest
was thin.

## Comparative note. Indonesia, the twin

The closest institutional twin of Nigeria in world aviation is
Indonesia. A sprawling, populous, oil exporting state that
deregulated aviation in the 1990s, grew traffic explosively on the
hand me downs of the rich world, and paid in a drumbeat of disasters
that ended in bans from European skies and an American downgrade in
2007. The Indonesian version of the 4226 problem produced the same
debates on nearly the same calendar. Age limits. Capital floors.

The divergence came later and is the instructive part. Indonesian
reform, once seriously begun, was sustained through growth. The
state harnessed its aviation boom as a development priority. It
rebuilt its regulator with international partners on published,
dated plans. It forced fleet renewal through consolidation around
carriers with real balance sheets. By 2016 to 2018 it had exited the
ban lists and regained American approval. Two states with the same
disease responded to the same treatment. Only one stayed on the
medication. The difference was not knowledge or money. It was the
decision to make aviation a growth strategy rather than a patronage
arena. That gave the reformers of Jakarta what those of Abuja never
durably had. A constituency richer than the constituency for decay.
Grief starts reforms. Only interest finishes them.

## Reflection

There is a moral asymmetry at the heart of this chapter. The
passengers of 4226 participated, at least formally, in the system
that killed them. They chose to fly and paid a fare. The people of
Gwammaja participated in nothing. They were killed by the
externalities of a system while cooking lunch. By a fleet age policy
never enforced. A weight culture never disciplined. An obstacle
survey never updated. A city never planned. Each deferral executed
by someone who would never live under the climb path. Economists
call such arrangements moral hazard. The older word is injustice. A
fair test of any regulatory state is how it treats the people who
cannot exit its failures. By that test the system of 2002 failed
more completely in Gwammaja than anywhere in this book.

Kano has now appeared three times in this history. In 1925 as the
stage of empire. In 1973 as the unread warning of the boom. In 2002
as the externalized bill of the deregulated bust. The city had given
warning enough. The country had three years to act on it. It did
not. The scene now shifts south, to a forest clearing near a village
called Lisa, and to a runway at Port Harcourt, in the year when
everything broke at once.
