# Chapter 16. Rebuilding Trust

The reform agenda for Nigerian aviation fits on one page, and it has
fitted on one page for thirty years. The page is not the problem.

Every element of what follows has appeared in some ministerial
roadmap, audit response, or national aviation policy since the
1990s, and several elements exist in law today. The purpose of this
chapter is therefore not discovery but specification. Each item is
stated with its mechanism, its cost, its owner, and its predictable
enemy, because an agenda without named enemies is a communique.

And it opens with the great cautionary tale of the era. The project
that consumed a decade of ministerial attention while the page went
unexecuted.

## Farnborough, July 2018

The unveiling had everything. A stand at the most important air show
in the world. A livery in green and white with the national colours
sweeping the tail. A minister holding a scale model for the cameras.

Nigeria Air. The national carrier was returning, fifteen years after
Nigeria Airways had been liquidated with its workers unpaid.

What followed instead of an airline was five years of procurement
opera. The project was suspended within months and then revived. A
strategic partner was selected, and it was Ethiopian Airlines, the
mirror of Chapter 15 now summoned as a vendor, chosen over the
objections and the litigation of the association of domestic
carriers, whose members correctly understood that a subsidized state
competitor would destroy them.

And then, in May 2023, days before the administration left office,
came the launch. A Boeing 737 landed at Abuja in the colours of
Nigeria Air. It was an Ethiopian Airlines aircraft, chartered,
repainted, and flown in for the ceremony. It flew home afterward.

The committee of the House of Representatives that investigated the
project delivered its verdict in the harshest parliamentary language
available. The succeeding administration suspended the project.
Anti corruption authorities later brought charges connected to
projects of the ministry, contested in court as this book went to
press, and on which this book takes no position.

The livery survives as a photograph.

## Four attempts and one template

Nigeria Air was not the first attempt to conjure a successor to
Nigeria Airways. It was the fourth. And four failures on a single
template constitute a dataset rather than a run of bad luck.

The first came before the old airline was formally dead. Around
2001, with Nigeria Airways in terminal decline, the aviation
minister of the day, Kema Chikwe, championed the flotation of a new
national carrier announced under the name Nigeria Global, which was
to rise alongside the dying flag and then replace it.

It was launched with ministerial fanfare and it acquired the
trappings of an airline. A name. Designated aircraft arrangements.
Inaugural ambitions on the London route. It never became a
functioning carrier. It dissolved within a couple of years amid
controversy over its leasing arrangements and its funding, leaving
behind legislative queries and a summary of every error the later
attempts would repeat.

The second was the most serious of the era. Virgin Nigeria was
launched in 2004 and 2005 as a joint venture with Virgin Atlantic,
the British carrier supplying management, brand, and operational
standards. This was a deliberate borrowing of external institutional
quality on the model of Kenya Airways, and it deserves credit,
because it flew, and for a period it flew well.

Then the arrangement met the Nigerian political economy. Disputes
over terminal allocation. Friction over brand and control. The exit
of Virgin. Rebranding as Nigerian Eagle and then as Air Nigeria
under new private ownership. And collapse into unpaid salaries and
grounding by 2012.

The lesson is the lesson of Chapter 15 in reverse. Rented
institutional quality works exactly as long as the landlord state
honours the lease.

The third was a succession of paper carriers and announcements
through the 2010s that never reached a runway.

And the fourth was Nigeria Air, the project of Hadi Sirika, minister
of state and then minister of aviation across the Buhari years, and
its most determined champion.

Four attempts. Four ministries. One method. The airline summoned
from the ministry outward. Capitalized in announcements. And
delivered to a state that had still not performed the unglamorous
repairs beneath it.

## Why the flag carrier keeps returning

The recurrence deserves explanation rather than mockery, because
four ministers across two decades and three parties are not simply
four fools. The project of a flag carrier has a political economy
that makes it almost irresistible, and understanding that economy is
necessary to resisting it.

Its benefits are concentrated, visible, and immediate. A livery at
an air show. A ribbon. A photograph on every front page. National
pride invoked at negligible marginal cost. And, in the procurement
itself, contracts with identifiable beneficiaries.

Its costs are diffuse, delayed, and technical. The competitive
damage to existing carriers. The subsidy that will be required
indefinitely. The opportunity cost of the ministerial attention
consumed.

That is the identical asymmetry Chapter 8 identified in the
economics of neglect, operating here in the opposite direction to
produce action rather than inaction, but on the same principle.
Politics systematically overweights the visible.

There is a second driver, subtler and more sympathetic. A flag
carrier is legible as an achievement in a way that regulatory
capacity is not. A minister can point to an aircraft. He cannot
point to a crash that did not happen.

Which returns us to the deepest structural finding in this book. The
things that keep people alive in aviation are invisible, and the
things that are visible do not keep anybody alive.

The counter model exists, it is unglamorous, and it is working one
border away. Ghana, after liquidation, grew its traffic on the
capital of other people while investing its scarce public attention
in its regulator. Fly the flag on the certificate rather than on the
fuselage. Let the insignia of the state mean rigorous oversight, and
let the market own the metal.

The applause line the politicians keep searching for is the one
Chapter 10 identified. A decade without funerals.

## The people the state forgot

There is one more piece of unfinished business from the first flag
carrier, and Chapter 3 told its beginning. It belongs here as well,
because the two stories are the same story.

When Nigeria Airways was liquidated in 2003 and 2004, roughly five
thousand serving staff and a longer tail of retirees became
creditors of a dead airline owned by the state that had killed it.
The associations of the retrenched picketed ministries through four
administrations. Their leaders grew old in the role. The roll of
members who had died waiting ran, by the counts the unions kept,
into the thousands.

Settlement came in 2017 and 2018, at a package reported at roughly
forty five billion naira, paid in tranches from 2018, with
verification disputes running on for years afterward. It arrived
some fifteen years late in a currency that had lost most of its
value across the interval.

And every serving professional in the industry watched it happen.

That is why the item belongs in a reform agenda rather than in a
chapter of history. A state that defaults on its aviation pensions
is teaching its own workforce that deferred compensation is a
lottery ticket, and it is therefore degrading its own future
inspectorate.

Any credible programme for rebuilding trust in Nigerian aviation has
to begin by noticing that the people the system most needs to
recruit have already watched it break a promise to their
predecessors, in public, for twenty years.

## The page

The first item is to finish the investigator.

The safety investigation bureau has the statute. Give it the
substance. A statutory publication clock, requiring an interim
statement within thirty days and a public docket within a year. A
register of recommendations, held in public, in which every
addressee must answer within ninety days, either accepting with
funding and a date or refusing with reasons. And the shield of just
culture drafted in Chapter 14.

The cost is trivial. The owners are the board of the bureau and the
National Assembly for the shield. The enemy is the blame system,
which is to say everybody with something pending. This is the
keystone. It is cheap, it compounds, and the honesty of every other
item depends on it.

The second item is to ring fence the regulator, permanently this
time.

The settlement of 2006 has eroded at the margins, with treasury
consolidation drives eyeing the levies and appointment politics
reclaiming the leadership. Restore and entrench it. Levies protected
at the highest legal altitude available. A director general
appointed from a professionally qualified shortlist to protected
tenure. Staggered board terms that no single administration can
sweep. And technical salaries benchmarked to the market that
actually bids for inspectors, which is the Gulf and the
international agencies rather than the civil service scale.

The cost is self funding from existing levies. The enemy is every
future minister of aviation, structurally, since the item exists to
outlive them.

The third item is to fill the trucks and run the drills, which is
the package of Chapter 13 repeated word for word. Firefighting
substance audited without warning. One full scale mass casualty
exercise per international airport per year, with hospitals in the
loop and after action findings published. A national rescue
coordination centre with a tested contact tree. Manifest integrity
enforced digitally. A family assistance act.

The cost is pocket change against any terminal project. The enemy is
nobody, which is precisely why it starves. Assign a single
accountable coordinator by statute.

The fourth item is to keep capital cheap by making promises legible.
That means full performance of the aircraft finance treaty Nigeria
has ratified, with repossession timelines guaranteed through a
designated commercial court list and registry operations at world
standard, which recaptures the financing discount for every Nigerian
lessee. Add transparent allocation of slots and routes, so that
carriers compete on operations rather than on access. The cost is
administrative and the payoff is basis points on every financed
airframe, which is the cheapest fleet safety money in existence.

The fifth item is to consolidate without cartelizing. Higher capital
floors and honest mergers, paired with continental open skies so
that the pricing power of consolidation is disciplined by
competition rather than by a regulator who will lose that fight. Add
resolution planning for the carriers now too large to ground, which
is the living will of Chapter 12, so that the safety decision is
never again hostage to the schedule decision.

The sixth item is to bring the military inside the perimeter, which
is the unfinished chapter running from Ejigbo to Attahiru. That
means an independent military airworthiness authority on the British
model, sitting inside the defence establishment and outside the
operating chain of command, with published summaries of mishaps as
its minimum floor of transparency. The enemy here is the deepest in
this book, being the informational sovereignty of the barracks, so
sequence the item accordingly. Transparency floor first. Authority
second. No Nigerian aviation reform is complete while the aircraft
of the state answer to nobody.

The seventh item is to rebuild the pipeline. Zaria re equipped
against a dated plan for fleet and simulators. Instructor pay
unhooked from the civil service scale. And a national cadetship
financing scheme, so that the pilot pipeline stops depending on
family wealth and diaspora remittance. The cost is the largest line
in the agenda and it is still small against one year of fuel
subsidy. The enemy is nobody, because pipelines have no constituency
until they are empty.

The eighth item is to publish everything. Registers, audit outcomes,
inspection results, and enforcement logs, machine readable, on the
web, by default. Transparency is the only regulator that works
weekends.

The ninth item is to manufacture the constituency.

The binding constraint throughout this entire book has been the
absence of anybody to demand the other eight items between funerals.
So build the demand deliberately.

An annual statutory report on the state of aviation safety, debated
in public session with the heads of the agencies on the record,
importing on a fixed calendar the scrutiny that currently arrives
only by catastrophe.

A standing advocate for consumers and victims, with docket standing
before the agencies, as the institutionalized descendant of the
parents of Loyola.

And continued deliberate coupling to the external witnesses, whose
audits remain, until domestic demand matures, the rented spine of
the system.

## What the page costs

Sum the programme generously and it remains below the published cost
estimates for the launch of the stillborn national carrier. It
remains below, for a sharper comparison, the value of the ticket
revenues of foreign airlines trapped in Nigerian accounts during a
single bad year of the currency queue, which ran to several hundred
million dollars.

Against that stand the returns this Part has assembled. The treaty
discount on every financed airframe. The insurance rerating that
follows investigative transparency. The route economics of a durable
Category 1. The hub revenues that Chapter 17 will quantify. And the
unquantified one, which is the funerals not held.

State the conclusion plainly, because the framing matters more than
the numbers. Nigerian aviation reform is not an expenditure
competing with schools and clinics. It is among the highest yielding
fiscal instruments the federation owns, mispriced for sixty years
because its dividends are paid in absences.

The budget office that finally prices the absences will wonder what
the country thought it was saving.

## Who holds the pen

An agenda addressed to Nigeria fails. Every item has a desk, and the
desks have interests that can be engaged rather than exhorted.

The presidency owns what nothing else can move. The appointments
that make or unmake the tenure of the regulator. The military
perimeter. And the political air cover that Chapter 9 proved is the
actual fuel of reform. What it gets in exchange is what Obasanjo
got, which is a legacy asset that outlived every rival achievement
of his final term. The pitch to any Nigerian president is not
safety. It is that aviation reform is the most reliably completable
monument the republic owns.

The National Assembly owns the statutes and the annual hearing. Its
interest is the one that 2002 discovered. Aviation oversight is high
in salience, telegenic, and, unusually among committee subjects,
scored by external referees whose findings a legislator can
brandish.

The industry owns the commercial items and is the natural financier
of the advocacy the sector lacks. A trade association that lobbied
for the quality of the system rather than against its own regulation
would be pursuing the lease rates, the insurance premiums, and the
route rights of its members by the most direct route available. Self
interest, properly organized, is the third algebraic term of Chapter
15. It has simply never been organized.

The professions own the content. The reporting culture. The design
of the pipeline. The technical memory that has outlasted every
ministry in this book. Their instrument is credibility, and their
historic error has been spending it only on wages.

And the families own the conscience.

Every reform in this history moved when those five hands briefly
held the same pen. The real sequencing of the agenda, beneath the
phases, is to get them to the same table, and to keep a chair for
the representatives of the funerals so that the table never again
needs new ones.

## The first hundred days

For concreteness, here is the opening programme of a reforming
administration or a newly protected director general, sequenced for
momentum rather than for importance.

In the first thirty days come the publications. Order the register
of recommendations, the registry of certificates, the results of
firefighting inspections, and the enforcement log onto the public
web in whatever state they currently exist, because imperfect data
published beats perfect data promised, and the act itself signals
the terms of the era. At the same time, run one unannounced
firefighting audit at every international airport and publish the
results within the month. The cost is server space and nerve.

In the second thirty days come the clocks. Agree and publish
timelines for interim statements and public dockets on all open
investigations, with the backlog scheduled and dated. Convene the
first quarterly briefing on safety performance, with cameras
present, agency heads on the record, and questions taken.
Institutions learn tempo from the calendar of the people at the top.

In the last forty days the entrenchments begin. Transmit the
prepared bills, meaning the shield of just culture, the family
assistance act, and the levy protections, while the honeymoon lasts.
Announce the pay parity mechanism for inspectors and the diaspora
recruitment round. Commission the full scale exercise at the busiest
airport, and attend it.

None of that completes anything. All of it makes the reversal of the
programme visible and therefore expensive, which the Demuren tenure
demonstrated is the entire art of the opening.

## The ways this fails

An agenda that cannot name its own failure modes is propaganda. Here
are the failure modes of this one, with the mitigations that exist
and the residual risks that do not.

It fails by consolidation of the wrong kind. The treasury raid
dressed as fiscal discipline, meaning the periodic drive to sweep
agency levies into the consolidated revenue fund, which would
reverse the second item in a single appropriations act and recreate
the begging regulator of the first regime inside a budget cycle. The
mitigation is drafting altitude and noise. The residual risk is
real, because a sufficiently determined fiscal centre beats any
statutory fence, and that is what the ninth item is for.

It fails by success. The quiet decade lulls the very coalition it
ought to recruit. No funerals means no urgency. The annual hearing
degenerates into mutual congratulation and the drills become
pageants again. This is the plateau extending itself, which is the
likeliest single future, and its only structural antidote is the
coupling of safety to money, because where nobody profits from
vigilance, vigilance is a mood, and moods end.

It fails by federal drift. The reform is captured at the centre
while the periphery of state airstrips, charter operators, general
aviation, and the drone economy grows faster than oversight reaches
it. The charter crash of 2013 and the military losses of 2021 were
peripheral failures in precisely this sense, and the periphery is
where the next Part Two begins.

And it fails by personalization. The reform gets identified with a
champion and rises and falls with one career. This is the Demuren
pattern, which this book has praised and must therefore also flag as
the known vulnerability of the design.

A reform is finished not when its champion wins. It is finished when
its champion becomes replaceable without event. That is the dullest
possible test, and the only one that matters.

The honest summary of the risk register is short. The engineering is
solved. The politics is unsolved. And the politics is the project.

## What trust actually is

Rebuilding trust is the phrase every Nigerian aviation roadmap uses,
and before this chapter closes the phrase should be cashed out,
because trust in a technical system is not a sentiment. It is an
inference from evidence, and it can be specified.

A passenger trusts an aviation system when she can reason, even
without putting it into words, through four propositions.

That the people operating this machine were qualified by an examiner
who cannot be bought.

That the machine is maintained on a schedule that somebody
independent verifies.

That if something goes wrong tonight, trained help will arrive
inside the window in which help still matters.

And that if the worst happens, the cause will be found, published,
and acted upon.

Four inferences, covering competence, verification, response, and
learning. Each one rests on an institution. Each one is checkable in
principle.

The Nigerian public of 1996 could make none of the four. The public
of the quiet decade can make perhaps two and a half, which is
exactly why its trust remains provisional, cheap to shake, and
expressed in the dark humour that has always been the actuarial
instrument of the Nigerian passenger.

The agenda on the preceding pages is, item by item, a machine for
making those four inferences severally true and severally visible.
The publication clauses matter as much as the substance, because an
inference requires evidence, and invisible rigour builds no trust at
all.

And the same specification explains why trust, once built, is an
economic asset of the first order. Aviation is the advance purchase
of a promise, and every doubt about the promise is priced. In demand
suppressed. In the insurance and lease premiums this book has
tracked. In the diversion of the elite of the country to foreign
carriers. And in the transfer traffic that connects through Addis
Ababa because a connection through Lagos still carries a discount
for uncertainty.

A single percentage point of restored trust is worth more to the
growth arithmetic of Nigerian aviation than any terminal ever
commissioned.

Governments understand buildings. The case this chapter has tried to
make is that trust is also infrastructure. It is the only kind that
compounds. It is the only kind that cannot be commissioned with a
ribbon. And it is the only kind whose construction schedule lies
entirely and permanently within the control of the state itself.
