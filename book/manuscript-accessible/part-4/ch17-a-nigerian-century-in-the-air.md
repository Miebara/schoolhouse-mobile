# Chapter 17. A Nigerian Century in the Air

Every forecast about African aviation is a forecast about Nigeria
wearing a disguise.

Open any of the twenty year outlooks the industry produces, whether
the market forecasts of the manufacturers, the traffic projections
of the airports, or the demographic tables from which all of them
are ultimately derived, and the same shape emerges. The mature
aviation markets are flattening. The great boom of Asia is maturing.
And the steepest remaining curve on every chart belongs to Africa, a
continent whose population will approach two and a half billion by
the middle of the century, urbanizing at historic speed, with a
deficit in ground transport that makes it, like the archipelago of
Indonesia, a geography that flight does not merely serve but
completes.

Within that curve, one country is the curve. On the central
projections Nigeria will be the third most populous nation on earth
by the 2050s, and its largest city will contend for the largest on
the planet. And Nigerians today board aircraft at a small fraction
of world average rates, taking something in the order of one trip
per twenty people per year against a world average many times
higher, which is what makes the arithmetic almost embarrassing to
write down.

Because the phrase growth market anaesthetizes, spell it out in the
only units that matter. Take Nigerian air travel propensity,
measured as trips per person per year, and hold it merely to the
trajectory that middle income countries have actually walked. The
path of Indonesia is the natural template, and Indonesia is not a
fantasy comparison, being a country of similar population scale,
similar income trajectory, and similar geographic necessity. Apply
that trajectory to a population heading past three hundred million
and Nigerian air traffic multiplies severalfold within a generation.
Tens of millions of additional annual passengers. Thousands of
additional daily movements. Second airports for Lagos and Abuja. And
a cargo economy in perishables, in online retail, and in
pharmaceuticals that barely exists today. The twenty year forecasts
the manufacturers publish for Africa, which call for well over a
thousand new airliners with the West African share concentrated in
one country, are conservative translations of demography into seats.

None of this requires Nigerian policy to succeed. The traffic comes
anyway. On the metal of somebody. Through the hub of somebody. And
that is precisely the point.

Demand is destiny. Supply is the choice.

## What growth does to a plateau

Now run that growth against the system Chapter 10 described, because
this is the calculation that should keep the regulator awake at
night.

Surveillance is a coverage function. An inspectorate sized for the
movements of today, subjected to three times those movements, is not
three times as stressed. Coverage failures compound in the way the
stages of emergency response multiplied in Chapter 13, because every
uninspected operator, every unaudited maintenance organization, and
every unexamined new entrant is not an additive gap but a
multiplicative one in the probability chain.

Which means that the margin the reform era banked is, on the plateau
path, an asset being drawn down at a rate set by traffic growth
itself. At five percent annual traffic growth against a frozen
headcount, effective coverage falls by roughly forty percent in a
decade, without a single decision being taken to reduce it.

The quiet decade, extended mechanically into the loud decades ahead,
is not a forecast of safety. It is a forecast of the gap between
load and capacity, widening annually and silently, in exactly the
pattern with which every chapter of Part Two began.

## The hub Nigeria has never played

Geography has dealt Nigeria a hand it has never played, and the
economics of that hand deserve to be laid out, because they are the
strongest commercial argument for everything in Chapter 16.

Lagos sits at the hinge of the Gulf of Guinea. Positioned for the
Atlantic crossings to Brazil and North America. Positioned on the
great trunk that runs north and south down the continent. And
sitting inside a natural catchment of some four hundred million
people within two hours of flying, which is a catchment larger than
the one Addis Ababa serves.

The hub prize is not vanity, and it is worth understanding why hubs
matter economically rather than symbolically. A hub concentrates. It
concentrates maintenance bases, because aircraft spend the night
where they are based. It concentrates training academies, because
crews live where the base is. It concentrates cargo logistics,
ground handling, catering, and avionics shops, which together are
the entire cluster of high wage technical employment that this book
has called the promise keeping economy. And it concentrates
connectivity itself, which is a productivity input for every other
industry in the country.

Today the West African hub is functionally distributed among Addis
Ababa, Istanbul, Dubai, Paris, and Lome. Every one of them carries
Nigerian traffic that Nigerian institutions forfeited. In the
collapse of the flag carrier. In the bilateral giveaways of the lean
decades described in Chapter 2. In every audit cycle that priced
Nigerian operations above those of the neighbours. And in the
terminal experience that Chapter 2 described.

The hub question is therefore this entire book restated as
economics. The prize goes to the jurisdiction whose system of
customs, transfers, security, reliability, and safety can be trusted
at scale. And systems are what this history has been about.

Ethiopia built one on a fraction of the endowment of Nigeria. The
endowment waits.

## The continental stage

The aviation century of Nigeria will be played on a stage the
previous century never built, and the politics of that stage deserve
a section.

The Single African Air Transport Market, launched under the African
Union in 2018 to operationalize an open skies decision that had
stalled since 1999, proposes the aviation equivalent of a
continental free trade area. Any African carrier may fly between any
African cities, without the bilateral route by route haggling that
has kept connectivity within Africa scarcer and costlier than
connectivity between continents.

The economics are not seriously contested. Study after study finds
that liberalization multiplies traffic, cuts fares substantially on
liberalized routes, and creates the network density that makes hubs,
maintenance industries, and training economies viable.

The politics are entirely contested. Open skies reward the prepared,
meaning the Ethiopians, with a mega hub and a fleet already built
for continental reach. And they expose the unprepared, whose
domestic markets are opened faster than their carriers can compete
for them.

That calculation is precisely what has kept Nigeria, a signatory,
hedging in practice. It protects the domestic revenues of its
carriers while its international traffic, which is the richest
origin and destination market in West Africa, enriches the hubs of
everybody else.

The hedge is understandable. On the evidence of this book, it is
also backward.

Protection has never built a competitive Nigerian carrier. Six
decades of protection built Nigeria Airways and the graveyard of
Chapter 12. What built everything that currently works was exposure
to standards. The Category 1 campaign. The international operational
audit. The covenants of the leasing companies.

The continental decision is the same choice at larger scale. Whether
the future of Nigerian aviation is negotiated as a rent to be
defended or as a market to be won.

A Nigeria that chose the second, pairing open skies with the agenda
of Chapter 16, offering the carriers of the continent its traffic in
exchange for the right of its own carriers to compete everywhere,
would be placing the bet Ethiopia placed in 1945 and Indonesia
placed in 2007. That the competence of its people, given
institutions, can win on merit.

Two hundred million passengers worth of gravity says the bet is
underpriced.

## The new weather

Two forces absent from the history in this book will shape its
sequel.

The first is the changed direction of technology. For the century
this book has covered, aviation technology flowed one way. Designed
in the world of strong institutions, absorbed later and older
everywhere else. That is the sorting machine of Chapter 12.

The coming decades scramble that pattern at the edges, and every
scramble is an opportunity and a hazard at once.

Unmanned systems are already outrunning the regulatory apparatus
built for crewed flight, and their accident physics, being cheap,
numerous, low altitude, and over cities, will stress exactly the
capacities this book found thinnest. Surveillance. Investigation
tempo. Liability to third parties.

Electric and hybrid regional aircraft, if their economics mature,
suit the African problem of thin routes almost perfectly, and they
will arrive as new technology available to first movers rather than
as hand me downs. That is an inversion of the entire second hand
history told in these pages, and the countries positioning for it
are positioning now.

And the data revolution in safety, with satellite based surveillance
ending the problem of radar coverage outright and flight data
streaming ending the problem of the lost recorder, cheapens exactly
the infrastructure whose procurement sagas filled Chapter 6.

None of it installs itself. Every item lands, or fails to land, on
the same institutional soil as everything else in this book. But the
soil requirements have dropped. For the first time the frontier is
affordable at Nigerian budget levels, and the inventory of excuses
shrinks annually.

The second force is climate.

The carbon politics of aviation will price and constrain the growth
of the industry everywhere, and the position of Africa in that
bargaining, holding about one percent of historical aviation
emissions alongside the steepest legitimate need to grow, will be a
defining diplomatic file. Nigeria, as the demographic principal,
holds the proxy of the continent whether it prepares or not.

Meanwhile the operational climate itself turns hostile in exactly
the dimensions the crashes in this book made familiar. The
convective weather of Sosoliso and of Flight 053 intensifies on
every credible projection. The dust regimes shift. And the coastal
infrastructure, with the runways of Lagos sitting metres above a
rising lagoon, enters the exposure tables.

The windshear decision that killed a Sultan will be presented to
Nigerian crews more often in 2050 than it was in 2006. Whether it
kills again is, as ever, not a weather question.

## The generation

The people who will run the Nigerian century in the air are alive
now, mostly under thirty, and they arrive holding assets that no
previous generation of Nigerian aviation possessed.

They arrive into a functioning core. The cadet who joins the
industry in the 2020s enters a system with radar, a real regulator,
published regulations, and a domestic safety record her instructors
could not have imagined at her age. A baseline rather than a
battlefield.

They arrive networked. The diaspora that drained the system of the
1980s now functions, in an era of cheap connection, as an accessible
faculty. The training captain in Doha mentoring cadets in Lagos by
video. The inspector benchmarking with counterparts she met on
attachment. The webinars of the associations erasing the isolation
in which the workforce of the 1990s fought its lonely arguments.

And they arrive with the archive in their pockets. The environment
of smartphone documentation, freedom of information, and published
reports means that the version of this history belonging to the next
generation is checkable from a dormitory. A generation that can
check is much harder to govern by liturgy.

What they do not arrive with is the memory.

The last Nigerians who watched the whole arc, from the founding of
Zaria through the waste of the boom, the canal, the lagoon, the
school, and the reform, are leaving the industry and the world.
Several of the people whose recollections inform this book are in
their eighties.

Memory does not transfer by inheritance. It transfers by
institution, through the archive, the curriculum, the memorial, and
the annual hearing. Or it does not transfer at all. And Part Three
measured exactly how little of that machinery exists.

Which identifies the gravest risk this generation faces, and it is
not the plateau, the currency, or the ministry. It is inheriting
quiet skies without the invoice. Mistaking, as the politicians of
their grandparents did in 1975, the artifacts for the system.

## The case against hope, answered

It is customary for books like this one to end in optimism, and the
custom has usually embarrassed the books. So state the case of the
pessimist at its strongest first, because it is not cynicism. It is
pattern recognition.

Every argument this chapter has made from demography and interest
was equally available in 1960, when a new nation with a natural
aviation market bought a flag carrier. And in 1977, when oil money
made anything possible. And in 2010, when Category 1 arrived and the
skies went quiet.

Each time the structural tailwinds blew. Each time the institutional
sails were not raised, or were raised and then struck.

The pessimist does not deny that reform is possible. He observes
that in sixty years it has been sustained for roughly eight, and
asks why the base rate should not govern the forecast.

It is a serious argument. Here is the answer. Three parameters have
changed that the base rate does not price.

The first is the information environment. Every earlier relapse in
this book happened substantially in the dark. The plateau of the
2020s is the first to occur under continuous public documentation,
through smartphone archives, freedom of information law, and a
bureau that publishes. Decay that has to happen in daylight happens
slower and costs more.

The second is the structure of interest. For the first time, the
growth arithmetic makes safety directly and visibly profitable to
actors with power. Fleets financed on covenants that price the
integrity of the system. A hub prize that is contested and
lucrative. An industry in which somebody powerful now loses money
when the system rots. That is the coalition Chapter 15 identified as
the third algebraic term, and it did not exist in 1977.

The third is the stock of demonstrations. The optimists of 1960 had
theory. The optimists of 2010 had one turnaround. Those of today
have a shelf that includes Nigeria in 2006, Indonesia, Korea, Ghana,
and the offshore enclave. Executed, documented, imitable repairs,
two of them performed by Nigerians on Nigerian institutions within
living memory.

Base rates govern systems whose parameters are static. The pattern
the pessimist describes was real. Its parameters are moving.

That does not guarantee the second act. It reprices it, from miracle
to decision.

## Two mornings in 2050

Since this entire book has argued that the future is a choice, let
the choice be rendered as directly as prose permits. Two mornings.
The same city. The same date. A generation from now.

On the first morning, the second airport of Lagos, the seaside field
that the 2030s finally built with its safety zones acquired at
greenfield prices before the city arrived, turns its hourly bank of
departures. A regional jet loads for Douala. The six o clock wave to
Accra, Abidjan, and Dakar boards under continental tariffs that made
the West African shuttle as ordinary as a bus. Across the field the
maintenance campus works the heavy checks of the night, and its
anchor tenant is the successor of the carriers of today while its
second tenant is Ethiopian, paying rent in Lagos now. In the tower,
the watch supervisor is a woman from Kano whose grandmother watched
an aircraft come down over Gwammaja. The safety bulletin of the
morning, pushed to every operations room in the federation, reviews
the runway excursion drill held last month at Port Harcourt.

Nobody in the terminal thinks about any of it. That is the
achievement. The system has become boring. And the boring system
carries two hundred million passengers a year.

On the second morning the same date arrives with the plateau
extended. The traffic doubled anyway, because demography does not
wait, onto the old airports, the old inspectorate, and the old
archipelago of response. The fleet is younger than the fleet of 1995
and older than the fleet of 2025, because the financing dried when
the treaty performance slipped. The vacancy rate of the inspectorate
is a standing committee topic. The backlog of the bureau is measured
in years again.

And somewhere in the system, on this particular morning, a
dispatcher is weighing a line of thunderstorms against a schedule.
An engineer is deferring a finding within the letter of a rule. The
pump of a fire tender is awaiting a part.

The holes are drifting, as they drifted through 1991 and 2005,
toward alignment. On this morning the alignment completes. And the
liturgy resumes, with mourning, probe, panel, and promises, in high
definition, before an audience of three hundred million, over the
graves of people not yet born on the day this book was finished.

Both mornings are fully specified in the chapters behind this page.
Neither of them requires a miracle. Each requires only that the
decisions of the intervening nine thousand mornings lean, on
average, one way or the other.

## Seat 14A, again

Return to the evening flight of the preface. Abuja to Lagos. The
darkened middle country scrolling beneath the wing. The orange
horizon of the delta. The improvised electric sea of Lagos rising to
meet the descent.

The view has not changed. The question has.

It was how a nation that cannot keep its lights on kept its
airplanes up. The answer is now assembled. Because for one compelled
interval it built arrangements that were funded, tenured,
witnessed, and published, and those arrangements let its ordinary
competence accumulate.

And everything before that interval, the whole broken winged
chronicle of Kano and Ejigbo and the lagoon and Lisa and Port
Harcourt and Iju Ishaga, is what the same competence produced when
the arrangements forbade it to accumulate.

Institutions are how a society remembers. They are also, in the same
sentence, how a society intends.

Somewhere below the wing, in the dark between the cities, are the
places this book has visited. Unmarked, most of them. Privately
mourned. Publicly forgotten.

And ahead, past the seatbelt chime and the humid air of the
jetbridge, is the century in which more Nigerians will fly than all
the dead of this history ever dreamed. Schoolchildren to Port
Harcourt at Christmas. Pilgrims to Jeddah in the white of ihram.
Officers to Kaduna. Traders to Kano. The whole nation airborne at
last, as the flypast of the founders promised in 1960.

They will board trusting, as passengers everywhere trust, that the
system beneath them is being maintained by people they will never
meet, against pressures they will never see, in offices they will
never enter.

That trust is either the next chapter of the reform or the first
chapter of another book like this one. Nigeria has now run both
experiments and knows both prices.

Broken wings mend. They are mended every day, in workshops, by
method, on schedules. The question, and it is the only question
there ever was, is whether the mending is treated as an event to be
commemorated, or as what it has always actually been. A discipline.
Daily. Unglamorous. And owed.
