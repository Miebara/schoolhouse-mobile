# PART FOUR. WHAT COMES NEXT

# Chapter 15. Lessons from Elsewhere

When the dark decades of Nigerian aviation come up in conversation,
in Lagos as readily as in London, the explanation that eventually
surfaces, in polite or impolite form, is cultural. Something about
national character. Fatalism. The way we are.

The claim wears a costume of evidence and it dissolves on contact
with the record.

The same Nigerians, in the same country, produced 2005 and 2010 five
years apart. Nigerian pilots and engineers, transplanted into
Emirates and British Airways and Delta, perform to world standards
immediately, which is why those airlines keep hiring them. And the
league table of world aviation safety reshuffles far too quickly for
any explanation as slow moving as culture to fit the data. Indonesia
moved from a European ban to full rehabilitation inside a decade.
Korea transformed itself in less.

What actually varies, at the speed the data varies, is institutional
arrangement.

This chapter holds up the mirrors and asks of each one a disciplined
question. What exactly is the mechanism, and does it travel.

## Ethiopia, or continuity as a policy

The Ethiopian mirror has shadowed this book since Chapter 1, and its
lesson is routinely mis summarized as proof that state ownership
works. The state of Congo owned airlines. So did the state of
Nigeria.

The Ethiopian mechanism is narrower and stranger. It is continuity
as an explicit and defended policy.

Since the founding bargain of 1945, which was an American management
contract with a written mandate to train Ethiopians into every seat,
and through an emperor, a Marxist junta, and a federal republic,
three regimes that agreed on nothing else, Ethiopian Airlines has
kept the same institutional spine. Professional management promoted
from within. An aviation academy feeding every cockpit and hangar.
Fleet renewal on commercial logic. And a sovereign compact holding
that the airline is a national asset whose raiding sits close to
treason.

Every regime arriving in Addis Ababa faced the temptation that the
rulers of Nigeria never resisted. Every one of them independently
concluded that the machine was worth more intact than raided. Sixty
years of compounding later the result is the largest carrier in
Africa, its hub, its maintenance industry, and its trainer, with a
safety record that no African peer approaches.

Now the panel that Nigerian discussion habitually crops out of the
photograph. The excellence of that airline has coexisted, for its
entire history, with a state that has been by turns an autocracy, a
Marxist dictatorship, a famine stricken war economy, and a
federation at war with itself.

Ethiopian Airlines is not the product of good governance in general.
It is the product of one protected institution inside governance
that has often been dreadful.

Which cuts both ways, and both cuts matter.

Encouragingly, Nigeria does not have to fix Nigeria in order to fix
its aviation. The island strategy works, and Chapter 9 showed a
Nigerian island briefly rising.

Cautionarily, the island has to be defended forever. And the defence
in Ethiopia has been a state interest in hard currency, prestige,
and strategic connectivity that every regime independently
recomputed and reaffirmed.

Which produces the most uncomfortable finding in this chapter, and
one that Nigerian readers may find genuinely unwelcome. The oil of
Nigeria was the quiet curse of its aviation system. Not through
corruption. Through the arithmetic of alternatives.

A state that earns foreign exchange from petroleum does not need its
airline to earn foreign exchange. The recomputation in Addis Ababa
always returned the instruction to protect the machine, because the
machine was one of very few reliable sources of dollars in a poor
economy. The recomputation in Lagos always returned the other
answer, because the dollars came out of the ground.

This is the literature on the resource curse applied to a specific
institution, and it generalizes. Petroleum states systematically
underinvest in the institutions that non petroleum states have to
build in order to survive. Not because their officials are worse,
but because the marginal return to building institutions is lower
when rents are available. The prediction is testable, and it holds
across the comparison set in this chapter with uncomfortable
consistency.

## Indonesia, or the full arc

Indonesia remains the master mirror, because it ran the entire arc
of Nigeria and then completed the recovery that Nigeria half ran.

It is a sprawling, populous, oil exporting archipelago. It
deregulated aviation in the 1990s. It grew traffic explosively on
the hand me down jets of the rich world. And it paid in a drumbeat
of disasters that ended, in 2007, in a European Union ban on its
carriers and an American downgrade of its oversight. The parallel to
Nigeria through 2007 is close enough to be eerie.

Then comes the second act, and its four components are worth listing
because together they constitute a replicable programme.

The state fused aviation reform to its growth strategy, because the
economy of an archipelago physically requires flight, and the ban
was costing tourism and investment in measurable amounts, and those
losses had constituencies.

The regulator was rebuilt with international partners on published
and dated compliance plans, which applied the transparency ratchet
of Chapter 11 to the reform itself.

The market consolidated around carriers with balance sheets capable
of buying new aircraft at record scale, which threw the sorting
machine of Chapter 12 into reverse through sheer financing depth
rather than through regulation alone.

And the exit milestones were treated as national projects with
presidential ownership, which is the same manoeuvre Demuren executed
with Category 1, at larger scale.

By 2016 to 2018, Indonesia had left the ban lists and regained its
American Category 1 rating.

Two states with the same disease took the same medicine. Only one of
them stayed on it. The difference was not knowledge, money, or
culture. It was the political decision to make aviation a growth
strategy rather than a patronage arena, which gave the reformers of
Jakarta the one thing the reformers of Abuja never durably had. A
constituency richer than the constituency for decay.

Grief starts reforms. Only interest finishes them.

## Korea, or the transformation nobody expected

One more great mirror, because it answers the question the others
leave open. Can the culture of a national aviation system, since the
sceptics insist on the word, actually be rebuilt, fast, and on
purpose.

South Korea is the documented yes.

Korean aviation in the 1990s was the problem child of the developed
world. A loss record across the decade that made the flag carrier a
byword. An American downgrade in 2001. Foreign partners suspending
codeshare agreements. Military personnel restricted from flying the
national airline.

The diagnosis that entered the popular literature was cultural.
Cockpit hierarchy steepened by language and deference, with first
officers structurally unable to challenge captains. There was real
truth in that diagnosis and the cockpit voice recordings supported
it. But the lesson usually drawn from it, which is that culture is
fate, was refuted decisively by what happened next.

Under regulatory and commercial siege, the airline rebuilt its
training system root and branch. Foreign examiners were given real
authority. English was standardized on the flight deck. Crew
resource management training was applied across the fleet. Checking
was made genuinely adversarial. Hiring was diversified. The
regulator rebuilt alongside.

Category 1 returned within sixteen months. The two decades that
followed rank among the safer records in the industry, and the
cultural cockpit of the diagnosis was, within a single professional
generation, simply gone.

Koreans did not become less Korean. Their cockpits became places
where the cost of hierarchy had been priced and engineered out.

Which yields the sentence this chapter exists to deliver. No
grandmother has ever crashed an airplane. Systems do. And systems
can be rebuilt by anybody willing to pay their political price.

## The near mirrors

Three smaller reflections, all close to Nigeria, sharpen details
that the great ones blur.

Ghana ran the history of Nigeria in miniature and wrote a different
final chapter. The same colonial parent. The same romance of the
flag carrier. The same patronage death, being banned from American
skies in 2004 and liquidated afterward, followed by a second flag
carrier that repeated the cycle within a decade.

Then, quietly, Accra stopped buying fuselages and started buying
oversight. A rebuilt regulator that became a regional reference.
Category 1 in the 2010s. And a policy of growing traffic on the
capital of other people, with its airport a secondary regional hub
and its market served profitably by foreign and private carriers.

Ghana today flies more safely and more connectedly than at any point
in its flag carrier era, on a state aviation budget that is a
rounding error against the launch cost of a national airline. Fly
the flag on the certificate rather than on the fuselage.

Angola is the negative control. An oil state with weak institutions
and the profile of a grey market magnet, which is the situation of
Nigeria with a civil war on top, and it sat under a total European
ban for years. Its partial rehabilitation in the 2020s tracked,
precisely, the fiscal need of an oil state to rejoin polite aviation
society.

Angola prices the alternative. Staying grey is possible
indefinitely. The cost is paid in lease rates, insurance premiums,
connectivity, and the slow export of an aviation economy to the hubs
of other people.

Rwanda is the aspirational miniature. No oil, no scale, and a two
decade old decision to make institutional credibility the national
export. A small, unprofitable, impeccably managed flag carrier. An
authority that audits clean. Treaty grade aircraft finance law
marketed like a tourism campaign. It is the pure strategy version of
what this book recommends, operating one border crossing from
Nigerian airspace.

## The secret of the league table

The single most liberating dataset in aviation governance hides in
plain sight on the international audit dashboards, and it deserves
to be stated as directly as possible.

Plot the states of the world by their aviation oversight audit
scores against their national income, and the two decouple. The
correlation exists, and it is also loose to the point of scandal for
every fatalist theory of development.

Singapore and the Emirates sit at the summit, above most of old
Europe. Middle income states, including Indonesia after its climb
and several African authorities among them Nigeria in its best
cycles, outscore wealthy countries whose oversight has coasted on
reputation.

And the movement happens at the speed of reform rather than at the
speed of growth. States have gained tens of points of effective
implementation in a single audit cycle of a few years, while their
income per person barely moved. Entry to and exit from the American
categories and the European ban list track governance events. A new
act. A new leadership. A crisis metabolized. They do not track
macroeconomic ones.

Whatever aviation oversight is made of, the data says, it is not
made of money beyond a threshold Nigeria crossed decades ago. It is
made of the things this book has spent its pages itemizing.
Statutes. Tenure. Publication. Witnesses. Constituencies. Each of
which is a decision, and none of which is a dividend of development
that has to be awaited.

The league table is posted publicly, updated continuously, and open
to any country that decides to climb it. It has no waiting room.

## What does not travel

Honesty about the comparative method requires the negative list,
because the world is littered with transplanted institutions that
died on arrival, and the failures have their own pattern.

Organograms do not travel. The aviation authorities of Africa are,
on paper, near clones, with the same directorates and the same board
structures, largely drafted from the same international model
legislation, and their actual performance spans the full range from
excellent to fictional. The form is the cheapest part of an
institution to copy and the least of what it is. Development
scholars call the result isomorphic mimicry, meaning the camouflage
of capability without its function, and audit driven fields are
especially prone to it, because the audit itself begins with the
paper.

Personnel do not travel as institutions. Consultant missions,
seconded inspectors, and returned diaspora can all transfer
knowledge, and none of them can transfer the surrounding field of
incentives, and the entire argument of Chapter 11 was that the field
rather than the knowledge sets behaviour. The reform of Nigeria
succeeded not when it acquired knowledgeable people, because it
always had them, but when it rearranged what their knowledge cost
them to apply.

And crisis does not travel, which is the most important negative in
the chapter. The American reform of 1958, the Nigerian reform of
2006, and the Indonesian reform of 2007 each ran on the political
energy of a domestic catastrophe. Countries cannot borrow the
funerals of other countries.

Which leaves exactly one thing that is genuinely portable, and it is
the item every reformer reading this book should take from it. The
prepared design. The statute drafted before the window opens. The
reform plan on the shelf. The costed programme waiting in a drawer.

The deepest common fact about every successful reform in this
chapter is that, at the moment of its crisis, it had already been
substantially written, by professionals who had been waiting,
sometimes for decades, with the file open. The Nigerian Act of 2006
passed in weeks because its substance had existed for years.

Reform by preparedness is not a slogan. It is the only strategy in
this book that a professional without political power can execute
alone.

## How to read a mirror

One methodological caution completes the foundation of Part Four,
because comparison is a powerful instrument and a corruptible one,
and Nigerian public argument has long been fluent in its
corruptions.

The same five mirrors can be, and in ministerial speeches regularly
are, used to prove anything at all. Ethiopia proves that state
ownership works, so order a flag carrier. America proves that
deregulation works, so license everybody. Indonesia proves that bans
are colonial bullying, so denounce the list. Korea proves that
culture is destiny, so sigh and change nothing.

The discipline that separates analysis from advocacy has three
rules.

Compare mechanisms rather than outcomes, by asking what specific
arrangement produced the result and whether its preconditions exist
or can be built at home.

Examine the failures too, because every mirror in this chapter has
them, and a case study that reports only successes is a brochure.

And insist on the full set, because any single comparison is an
anecdote with a flag attached. It is the pattern across five or
eight, with the same terms surfacing in every recovery and the same
absences in every stagnation, that carries evidential weight.

## The algebra

Reduce the mirrors to their common terms and three survive every
border crossing.

The first is architecture over intention. Funding by fee, tenure,
independence, and published records outperform every quantum of
sincerity. The split verdict of South Africa makes the point
domestically, because the same state, in the same decades, kept a
fee funded and professionally staffed aviation regulator performing
to world standard while its politically boarded and budget funded
flag carrier was looted into liquidation. No cultural theory
explains why one institution endured and the other was devoured.
Architecture explains it exactly.

The second is legibility to power. Institutions survive predation
when their value is visible and attributable in the currency the
sovereign actually counts. The dollars of Ethiopian. The Category 1
of Nigeria. That is the reason the most sophisticated move of the
reform was aiming at an external, dated, adjudicated target.

The third is a coalition richer than decay. Somebody with an ongoing
interest has to be structurally attached to the rigour of the
system, because attention is mortal and grief has a half life.

Culture appears nowhere in the algebra, and the omission is the
finding of this chapter.

What the professional literature calls safety culture is real, and
it is downstream. It is the residue that forms inside professionals
after architecture, legibility, and coalition have held long enough
to be believed.

Nigerians did not lack that residue for cultural reasons. They were
never, until recently, governed by arrangements capable of
depositing it.

The next chapter turns from mirrors to blueprint, and examines what
the arrangements would actually cost, who would have to build them,
and what stands in the way.
