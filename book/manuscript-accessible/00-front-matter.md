# A Note on the Title

Broken Wings names the wreckage this history must walk through. But
wings are almost never what breaks. Wings are the most carefully
engineered structures human industry makes. They fail almost never.
None of the seven disasters in this book was the fault of a wing.

What breaks is everything the wing depends on. The pressure in a
tire. The truth of a load sheet. The honesty of a fuel farm. The
funding of a radar. The courage of an inspector. The publication of
a report. The wing is the skill Nigeria always had. The breaking
happened around it.

There is a second meaning. Broken wings can be mended. They are
mended every day, in workshops, by method, on schedules. This title
is not a lament. It is a work order.

# Author's Note

This is a book about airplanes that is not really about airplanes.

Aviation is the most measured industry on earth. Every flight leaves
a record. Every accident brings an investigation. Every
investigation is supposed to be shared with the world. That makes
aviation a rare thing for anyone trying to understand a country. It
is an honest instrument. Politicians can argue with poverty numbers.
It is much harder to argue with a crash.

I have tried to honor two duties at once. The first is to the dead
and the bereaved. They deserve accuracy and restraint. Where this
book tells the story of a crash it relies on official reports where
they exist and on press records where they do not. It says plainly
which is which. The second duty is to the living. Many people worked
honorably inside institutions that failed around them. This book
blames systems often and people rarely. Where it names a person it
stands on the documented record.

A note on numbers. Nigerian casualty figures often differ between
sources. That fact is itself evidence. Passenger lists were
unreliable. Ground victims went uncounted. Records were not kept.
Where sources conflict I use the figure from the final report if one
exists and I say so when one does not.

A note on method. This book was drafted from the public record. A
full check against primary documents must come before print. The
notes at the back map that work. Any errors that survive are mine.

# On Names and Dignity

The dead are named when naming honors them. They are counted with
care when the record allows nothing more. The gap between those two
conditions is one of the subjects of this book.

Survivors are named when they have chosen public life. Officials are
named for documented public acts. Where wrongdoing has no proven
author this book describes the system that made the wrongdoing
ordinary. That is the truer charge.

A few figures in this book are composites. They are labeled as
composites every time they appear. They invent no facts. The
institutions are named without softening throughout. Institutions
have no feelings. They are the only actors here that can take
criticism at full strength. They are also the only ones that
criticism can fix.

# Preface. The View from Seat 14A

There is a moment on any evening flight from Abuja to Lagos when the
lights below simply stop.

The aircraft climbs out over the glow of the capital. Then somewhere
over Niger State the country goes dark. Not dim. Dark. For twenty
minutes the most populous nation in Africa passes below as an
absence. Then the horizon burns orange with gas flares and the vast
electric smear of Lagos rises to meet you. Twenty four million
people making light the way a fire makes light. Bright, uneven, and
mostly off the grid.

I have made that flight many times. At some point I stopped looking
at the view and started thinking about the machine I was looking at
it from. It was built in Seattle or Toulouse. It was financed in
Dublin and insured in London. It was maintained to standards written
in Montreal. It was a small embassy of the international system on
loan to a country that has struggled to build institutions of its
own. Everyone aboard was betting their life that the rules of the
embassy would govern and not the habits of the country.

For most of my lifetime that bet was uncertain. Between 1991 and
2012 Nigeria suffered a chain of air disasters remarkable even for a
troubled continent. A pilgrimage flight burned over Saudi Arabia
with 261 people aboard. A military transport fell into a Lagos canal
with much of a generation of officers. Airliners were lost in the
lagoon, in the forest, in city streets. In 2005 the country ran out
of ways to grieve. A school in Abuja buried sixty of its children at
once.

Then it stopped. That is the strangest and most hopeful fact in this
book. After the crashes of 2005 and 2006 Nigeria did what its
critics said it never does. It reformed. It rewrote the law. It gave
the regulator power and money. It checked every airline again from
zero. In 2010 the American aviation authority certified that
Nigerian oversight met world standards. The scheduled airlines of
Nigeria have not had a mass casualty jet crash since 2012.

I remember when the question changed for me. In the middle 2000s
boarding a flight at Lagos was an exercise in prayer. You studied
the aircraft through the window as if safety were visible in paint.
The jokes in the departure lounge did the work that trust does in
other countries. Then through the 2010s the jokes lost their edge.
The flights simply went. Delayed and maddening, but they went, year
after year. A whole national dread faded so slowly that nobody
marked its passing. The absence of catastrophe has a history too.
Nobody writes it because its evidence is that nothing happened. This
book tries to write it.

So this is not a book about failure. It is a book about the
conditions under which failure happens and the conditions under
which it stops. Nigeria ran both experiments on the same people
within one generation. A nation that cannot keep its lights on kept
its airplanes up. How. Why so late. At what cost. And can it keep
them up. That is the question from seat 14A.

# Introduction. The Instrument Panel of a Nation

On the morning of 29 October 2006 the most senior religious leader
in northern Nigeria boarded a plane. The Sultan of Sokoto, Muhammadu
Maccido, took ADC Airlines Flight 053 from Abuja toward home. A line
of thunderstorms stood across the departure path. The crew had the
weather report. Other pilots that morning chose to wait. Flight 053
did not. Less than a minute after takeoff the aircraft flew into the
storm, lost the fight for climb, and came down in farmland. Ninety
six people died. The Sultan was among them.

Nigeria had buried the victims of three mass casualty crashes in
fourteen months. The national response had become almost a ritual.
Presidential condolences. Three days of mourning. A panel of
inquiry. Editorials asking how such a thing could happen. Then
silence until the next one. There was every reason to expect the
ritual to run again.

It did not. What happened instead is the hinge of this book. But
first ask the question the ritual always skipped. What exactly
crashed that morning?

The quick answer fits in a sentence. An airliner met windshear, a
sudden reversal of wind near the ground, and could not recover. But
pull on that sentence and a whole country comes with it. Why did a
crew with the weather in hand fly into a storm that other crews
refused? What pressures sat in the cockpit with them? Who had
certified the airline, and when had anyone last looked? Why did the
airport have no windshear warning system? Why did a system that had
just buried the dead of two other crashes not treat a storm on
departure as an emergency?

Ask enough questions like this about any crash and you reach a
conclusion the safety profession settled long ago. Accidents are
organizational events. The thinker James Reason gave the idea its
lasting image. Every layer of defense has holes, like slices of
Swiss cheese. Disaster comes when the holes line up. The last hole
is usually a person under pressure in a machine. But the holes were
cut upstream, over years, in offices. A crash is the moment when the
accumulated deferrals of a society become visible all at once, in
fire, to everyone.

The argument of this book follows from that. The aviation crises of
Nigeria were not acts of God. They were not bad luck. They were not
the tax of poverty, because poorer countries have flown far more
safely. They were symptoms. They were the visible, datable,
countable expression of weak institutions in a state that has
struggled to build organizations more durable than the people who
run them. Read in order, the crashes map the institutional history
of Nigeria with strange precision. They cluster in the years of
decay. They recede in the years of repair. The sky is an instrument
panel. This book reads it.

## Why aviation

A fair question. Aviation touches few Nigerians directly. Hospitals
fail more people. Roads kill more in a bad month than aircraft have
in sixty years. Why should aviation carry this weight?

Three reasons.

First, aviation is measured. There is no world registry of failed
clinics. In aviation there is. The Chicago Convention of 1944 binds
Nigeria to international standards for licensing, maintenance, and
accident investigation. The International Civil Aviation
Organization, ICAO, audits compliance and scores it. The American
regulator, the FAA, rates foreign oversight. The European Union
publishes a ban list. No other Nigerian institution is examined this
often, by this many outsiders, against standards this clear. When
we say Nigerian oversight improved after 2006 we are citing audit
scores, not offering an impression.

Second, aviation is unforgiving in a way that makes institutional
decay visible. Most decay is slow and deniable. A school system rots
over a generation and everyone responsible retires first. Aviation
compresses the feedback. A regulator that stops inspecting produces
wreckage on a measurable schedule. This is terrible for victims and
clarifying for analysts. Few subjects let you date decay to the
month.

Third, and this is the hopeful reason, aviation is the industry in
which weak states have most often succeeded anyway. Ethiopia, far
poorer than Nigeria for most of this period, built one of the most
respected airlines in the world and kept it excellent through
revolution, dictatorship, famine, and war. Safety is not a dividend
of wealth. It is the product of specific arrangements that can be
built even where the wider state is weak. Protected funding.
Professional independence. Honest investigation. Published findings.
If islands of excellence are possible then failure is a choice made
somewhere by someone. Choices can be unmade.

## The shape of the argument

The book moves in four parts.

Part One, The Ascent, runs from the first British aircraft over Kano
in 1925 to the end of the 1980s. It is a history of birthmarks.
Nigerian aviation was born colonial. Its routes served an empire.
Its institutions lived in London. Independence nationalized the
symbols of flight while the organs of oversight were never fully
built. The national airline was asked to be three things at once. An
airline, a flag, and a source of patronage. The oil boom paid for
all three. The bust that followed stripped the system bare.

Part Two, The Crashes, is the heart of the book. Seven chapters,
seven disasters, 1991 to 2012, each told on one template. The scene.
The timeline. The investigation. The reaction. The institutional
analysis. A comparison with other countries. Read one crash and you
have a tragedy. Read seven on the same frame and you have a dataset.
The same holes align again and again.

Part Three, The Patterns, steps back from story to anatomy. Four
essays on the recurring failures. The captured regulator. The
economics that made cutting corners rational. The emergency response
that never came. The culture of silence around official reports.

Part Four, The Future Flight Path, turns to the horizon. What other
countries teach. What a real reform agenda looks like, with costs
and owners named. And what the coming decades demand. Nigeria will
fly more in the next thirty years than in its whole previous
history. Whether that future looks like Ethiopia or like 2005 is not
fate. It is design.

## The story in one page

Aviation came to Nigeria as the tool of an empire. Independence
took over its glory without building its guts. Oil money hid the
hollowness through the 1970s. The bust of the 1980s exposed it. A
military state pushed the shortage away from power and onto the
public. Deregulation then multiplied the airlines tenfold above a
starved referee, and the retiring jets of the world sorted
themselves toward the weakest oversight on offer.

The bill arrived across two decades. Jeddah. Ejigbo. The lagoon.
Kano. Lisa. Port Harcourt. Abuja. Each disaster was mourned deeply
and converted into nothing. Then 2005 and 2006 stacked three mass
funerals and the coffin of a king inside fourteen months, and the
political system finally broke open.

What followed was a real reform. Funded, protected, published,
watched by the world. It worked. A decade of quiet skies followed.
Then the politics behind the reform slowly faded while its machinery
held. The country today is safe, stalled, and unguaranteed, with the
busiest aviation decades in African history on the way and the
military sector never reformed at all.

None of it was fate in either direction. The crashes were built. The
quiet was built. The difference was always the arrangement of
institutions. The rest of the book is the evidence.

## The stakes beyond Nigeria

The central governance question of this century is whether societies
can maintain complex technical systems under political stress.
Grids. Banks. Pipelines. Aircraft. The rich world is discovering
what the poor world always knew. Competence is not a possession. It
is a practice. Systems rot invisibly under confident management.

The Nigerian sky is that discovery run at high volume with full
documentation. A society inherited a safety critical system, built
it, consumed it, lost it, rebuilt it, and now maintains it or not,
all within one lifetime, all measured by outside audit. The American
737 MAX scandal taught Americans that their own regulator could
hollow out. Readers who want to know what institutional failure
looks like from inside, what it costs, and what verified repair
actually consists of will find no cleaner case anywhere.

## A word about tone

Books about disaster in Africa tend toward two failures. One is the
catalogue of horrors, in which each crash proves a continent
hopeless. The other is the defensive crouch, in which every
criticism is deflected. Both are lazy. Both insult the dead, who
were killed by specific decisions in specific institutions that
could have decided otherwise.

This book tries a third mode. Call it seriousness. It assumes
Nigerian lives merit the same care as American ones. It holds
Nigerian institutions to world standards because Nigerians have met
those standards whenever the arrangements allowed. It answers
failure with specification. What failed. Why. What must change.

And it ends in cautious hope, because the evidence does. The safest
decade in Nigerian aviation history is the most recent one. That
fact was bought at terrible cost. Whether it was a turning point or
an interlude is not yet settled. This book exists to load the scale.

The tragedy of Nigeria was never a shortage of talent. Nigerian
pilots fly for the great airlines of the world. A Nigerian regulator
once took a system the world had written off and made the FAA
certify it as an equal within four years. The tragedy is a shortage
of institutions that let ordinary excellence accumulate. Airplanes
are where that shortage became visible. What follows is the story of
what it looked like, what it cost, and what it would take to end it.
