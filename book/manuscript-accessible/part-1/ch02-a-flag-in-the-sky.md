# Chapter 2. A Flag in the Sky

Every new nation buys three things first. A flag, an anthem, and an
airline. The flag is cheap. The anthem is free. The airline is the
most expensive symbol a poor country can own.

On the morning of independence, the first of October 1960, the
aircraft of the new national carrier flew overhead as part of the
ceremony. Nigeria Airways had been created two years earlier from
the dissolving West African corporation, at first in partnership
with British firms until it could be fully Nigerian. Its planes
would carry the green and white to London and New York. Its logo in
time would be a flying elephant. A creature chosen for majesty
rather than aerodynamics, though history would have its joke either
way.

It is easy to condescend to the founding dream. This chapter will
not. The dream had hard logic. Nigeria was a natural aviation
market. Fifty five million people at independence, spread across a
huge territory with poor roads. A national carrier promised
connection, foreign exchange, training, and presence. In 1960 an
airline was how a new state introduced itself to the world. And
state ownership was normal. Nearly every airline on earth outside
America belonged to a government.

But the normal model hid a fork in the road. A state airline could
be owned by the nation and run as an enterprise, disciplined by its
books, with the government as shareholder. Or it could be run as an
annex of the state itself. Its jobs part of patronage. Its routes
part of diplomacy. Its management appointed and removed like
ministers. Ethiopian Airlines was built deliberately on the first
model and defended by every regime as too valuable to raid. Nigeria
Airways drifted onto the second. Not at once, and never
irreversibly, but steadily. That drift is the story of this
chapter.

## The boom

For its first decade the airline could claim success. The 1960s
fleet was modern for the continent. Nigerian pilots trained in
Britain and Canada. Nigerian engineers earned licenses recognized
abroad. The airline even survived the civil war of 1967 to 1970,
when airports became targets and the Biafran airlift wrote its own
strange chapter in the night skies.

Then came the boom. The oil price explosions of the 1970s
transformed the treasury faster than any society absorbs well.
Nigeria Airways expanded with the mood. Wide body jets for the
intercontinental routes. New aircraft for a thickening domestic
network. By the late 1970s the airline carried over a million
passengers a year and flew to four continents. Lagos airport,
renamed for the murdered head of state Murtala Muhammed, gained a
new international terminal copied from Amsterdam. The most modern
in Africa, it was said at the ribbon cutting.

The reader who learned the lesson of Chapter 1 will ask the right
question. The fleet was growing. Was the system? Three numbers
answer it.

First, the count of aircraft types. By 1980 the airline operated
roughly a dozen distinct types in a fleet of about thirty. Every
extra type meant separate spares, separate training, separate
certifications. Fleets grow that way when purchases are decided as
diplomacy and theater rather than planning. Complexity was bought at
boom prices to be maintained later at bust prices.

Second, management tenure. The airline ran through more than twenty
chief executives in its life, many lasting months. Each owed his
seat to a patron. Each froze the plans of his predecessor. No
maintenance program survives churn like that. Churn is what
patronage looks like on an organization chart.

Third, the payroll. The workforce grew to several times the industry
norm per aircraft. The airline had become an employment program with
a flying division. The individuals were not the problem. The
arithmetic was. An airline that exists partly to distribute jobs
cannot shrink to its revenue and cannot say no. An airline that
cannot say no stores its risk in the hangar.

What did the boom look like from inside a cockpit? The first
generation of Nigeria Airways captains lived a career their
successors recall as a golden age and they themselves experienced as
a daily argument with decay. The flying was world standard. The
salaries were princely. But the veterans all describe the same
scenes. The trip flown with a defect list that would have grounded
the plane on a neighboring registry. The roster torn up overnight
for a VIP charter. The training postponed for budget seasons. Every
senior pilot ran a private calculation at the head of the jetway.
Which compromises would he carry into the air and which would he
refuse at the cost of a summons to headquarters. The safety of the
company became a private subscription service run by its
professionals against the drift of their employer. When the exodus
began in the 1980s its first recruits were exactly these people.
Institutions do not only lose people to better pay. They lose them
to the exhaustion of winning the same argument ten thousand times.

None of this was secret and none was uniquely Nigerian. It was the
standard sickness of state enterprises everywhere. The difference is
what failure costs. When a state grain board fails the grain rots.
When a state airline fails the failure arrives at several hundred
miles per hour.

## War in the air and the first jet grave

Two events of the first decade complicate the story and deserve
rescue from the footnotes.

The first is the civil war. While the blockaded east starved, its
supply line became a nightly shuttle of chartered transports flying
blacked out into a strip of reinforced road, relief on one channel
and arms on another. The moral history of the airlift belongs to
other books. Its institutional deposit belongs here. The war
normalized, at scale and under the highest sanction, exactly the
aviation the rules forbid. Unregistered aircraft. False manifests.
Night landings on unlicensed strips. Some of that repertoire flowed
after 1970 into the regional gray charter market, and the gray
market never fully closed. When a funeral charter falls out of the
Lagos sky in 2013 with paperwork that should have grounded it, the
lineage runs back, step by step, to the exemptions of the war.

The second event is the crash of November 1969. The airline
flagship, a VC10 inbound from London, descended into forest short of
the Lagos runway in the early morning of 20 November and was
destroyed with all 87 aboard. It was the first great jet loss of the
nation, and it is the most completely forgotten disaster in this
book. No memorial. No annual journalism. No public report that any
Nigerian reader could consult. The war was ending and the state had
no machinery for public technical accounting. What the crash
established was the national grammar for aviation catastrophe.
Mourn intensely. Investigate opaquely. Publish nothing. Remember
privately. Proceed. Every element of the grammar was in place before
the boom began. The boom merely bought bigger aircraft to apply it
to.

## Zaria. The institution that worked

Fairness requires this section. The independence generation built
one aviation institution on the right model, and its fate is as
instructive as the airline.

In 1964, with United Nations and ICAO support, Nigeria opened the
Civil Aviation Training Centre at Zaria. A school for pilots,
controllers, and engineers. Zaria was everything the airline was
not. Technically governed. Internationally supervised. Selective.
It trained students from across Africa. For a generation of African
aviation professionals the name Zaria carried weight, and its
alumni run through every institution in this book, usually on the
creditable side.

Zaria matters twice. First it disproves in advance the lazy claim
that Nigeria could not do aviation. Nigeria could, wherever design
insulated competence from politics. An entrance exam instead of a
patron. A curriculum audited from Montreal instead of a budget
negotiated in Lagos. Second, the later history of Zaria proves the
other half of the theorem. Decades of underfunding. Obsolete
training aircraft. Instructors lost to airline pay. Founding design
protects an institution for a while. Only a political constituency
protects it forever. The airline had a constituency, everyone who
could extract from it. The training college had none. Where the
extraction was, the money went. A national budget is the actual
aviation policy, whatever the gazette says.

The pattern deserves one more sentence because it recurs in every
decade of this book. The institutions that produced excellence were
the ones designed to be boring. The institutions that produced
disaster were the ones designed to be seen.

## The airline as mirror of the state

The first two decades of the airline track the constitutional
history of Nigeria so closely that it can serve as a seismograph.

The airline of the First Republic was the federation in miniature.
Board seats balanced across regions like the cabinet. Routes drawn
to the regional capitals. When the federation broke in 1966 the
airline broke along the same seams. Eastern staff withdrew or were
withdrawn. Aircraft became war assets. The airline of the 1970s was
the airline of the unitary military state. Run from the center on
oil money, its purchases were procurement politics and its payroll
was employment politics. Its management changed with each coup.

Nothing about this correspondence is accidental. A wholly state
owned enterprise in a personalized state cannot have a character
different from the state. Its governance is the state applied to
aircraft. That is why the Ethiopian comparison keeps its force. The
founding bargain in Addis Ababa built a firewall between the
character of the state and the character of the airline. And that is
why every plan to revive a Nigerian flag carrier without first
reforming the grip of the state is a plan to rerun this chapter with
newer aircraft. The airline was never the patient. It was the
thermometer.

## Kano. January 1973

The Hajj is the largest annually recurring movement of people on
earth, and by the early 1970s Nigeria was among its largest sources.
Hajj transport was the strangest market in aviation. A huge demand
spike, a few weeks long, in one direction at a time. No airline
could keep a fleet sized for it. The universal answer was the
charter. Hire aircraft and crews from wherever capacity could be
found and surge the pilgrims across.

On 22 January 1973 a Boeing 707 chartered from a Jordanian carrier
for the Nigerian Hajj approached Kano at the end of a flight from
Jeddah. Visibility was poor in the dry season haze. The approach
went wrong. The aircraft came down hard, broke, and burned. One
hundred and seventy six people died. At that moment it was the
deadliest crash in the history of civil aviation.

Sit with that fact. The deadliest crash the world had seen involved
Nigerian passengers on Nigerian soil, and it is barely remembered
today. Part of the forgetting is general. The 1970s were the
bloodiest decade of world aviation. But part is specific. The
aircraft was Jordanian. The crew was foreign. The charter had been
arranged through the Hajj apparatus rather than the airline. In the
seams between jurisdictions, accountability bled out. Kano buried
the dead in long trenches and the north mourned through the spring.
But there was no reckoning. No public report Nigerians could read.
No named institutional failure. No reform with a date on it. The
official conclusion, as far as the record shows, was that a foreign
airline had crashed a foreign plane in bad weather. An act of God
with a Jordanian registration.

The other conclusion was available even then. Nigeria had built a
system in which the most dangerous flying its citizens did, the mass
charters, was exactly the flying its institutions oversaw least. A
state whose citizens board aircraft it does not oversee has not
escaped responsibility. It has only deferred it. Eighteen years
later at Jeddah the deferred bill arrived with 261 names on it.
Institutions that ignore their first warning are not granted gentler
second ones.

## After Kano. The anatomy of a non response

What a functioning system does after such a disaster has a
checklist, and the checklist existed in 1973. Open an investigation
with international participation. Publish findings. Track
recommendations. Then ask the deeper question. What class of
operation produced this, and does the class need redesign?

The public record of what followed is thin to the point of
transparency. No published Nigerian report. No reform of the charter
system. The government, months from an oil windfall, processed the
event through the two frames available. The religious frame,
martyrdom in pilgrimage, a consolation with deep legitimacy. And the
diplomatic frame, a Jordanian aircraft handled state to state. The
third frame, the systemic one, had no constituency to insist on it.
The Hajj airlift continued on the same architecture into the 1990s,
where Chapter 4 collects the consequence.

The non response of 1973 sets the baseline against which the
response of 2006 must be measured. Same country. Same class of
shock. Opposite outcomes, thirty three years apart. What changed was
not the people or the faith. It was the presence, by 2006, of a free
press, an organized constituency of victims, and a reform design
waiting on the shelf. History rarely runs so clean a trial on a
single nation. This book is the report of that trial.

## The elephant

The emblem of the airline earned its place in the imagery of this
history. The flying elephant was chosen from the same register as
everything else in the founding era. The elephant as African
grandeur. The wings as the new age. Nigerians embraced it and teased
it. By the 1980s the joke had turned proverbial. Elephants, the
market wits observed, do not fly. The airline died narrated under
elephant metaphors of increasing cruelty.

The emblem outlived the fleet. It survives in memorabilia and in the
memory of the older diaspora, for whom the elephant on a London
bound jet remains a pride with no replacement. And it survives as a
standing caution. When a new national carrier livery was unveiled at
an air show in 2018, commentators reached at once for the
comparison. Another symbol procured before the system beneath it.
Symbols are not the enemy in this history. Nations need them. The
enemy is the sequence. A symbol that crowns a working system
compounds its worth. A symbol procured in place of one becomes its
epitaph. The wings on the elephant were at least honest. They were
painted on.

## The routes as foreign policy

One boom era habit compounded quietly for fifty years. The route
network was spent as diplomatic currency. Routes to fellow African
capitals as statement. Frequencies granted to partners as summit
courtesy. The airline flew the statements at a loss and the treasury
absorbed it.

The true price emerged later in two forms. Routes flown for
diplomacy could not be cut without incident, so the dying airline
bled longest where it earned least. And the treaty book, negotiated
for symbolism, underpriced the real asset, access to the Nigerian
market itself. Foreign carriers accumulated rights into Lagos on
terms no commercial negotiator would have granted. Those terms
outlived the airline they were traded against. Later Nigerian
carriers found them arrayed like a mortgage signed by their
grandparents. When a state spends the commercial assets of an
institution as diplomacy, the cost is invisible until the
institution must live on what remains.

## The terminal and the mirror

One more scene from the boom, because it captures the self
understanding of the era better than any statistic.

In the late 1970s Nigeria opened the new international terminal at
Lagos, copied consciously from the most admired airport building in
Europe. The opening was a national event. Here was the boom made
walkable. Proof in glass and terrazzo that Nigeria had arrived.

Anyone who used both buildings over the next twenty years received a
tutorial in the difference between a structure and a system. The
original in Amsterdam stayed excellent because a dense web of dull
institutions renewed it continuously. Its Lagos twin began dying on
opening day. Air conditioning that failed and stayed failed.
Escalators that became staircases. Generators coughing through the
power cuts. By the 1990s the terminal was a byword among travelers,
and foreign regulators cited Lagos for security failures. Same
blueprint. Same decade. Divergent institutions. Two buildings ran
the whole argument of this book as an experiment in concrete.

The terminal was a mirror and it foretold. What happened to the
building was about to happen to the airline, the regulator, and the
whole boom bought apparatus of Nigerian flight. It required only
that the money stop. In 1981 it stopped.

## What the flag concealed

The comparative lens earns its keep here, because the 1970s ran a
live experiment nobody knew they were running.

In Addis Ababa, Ethiopian Airlines spent the decade doing unglamorous
things. Its American management contract had ended with the
Ethiopianization it was designed to produce. The airline kept the
systems, the academy, the maintenance culture. When revolution came
in 1974 and a Marxist junta seized the state, the new rulers left
the airline alone, having grasped that it was one of the only
reliable hard currency machines the state owned. It flew through
terror, famine, and war, and made money. The lesson is
uncomfortable and simple. Institutional protection is a political
choice available even to poor states, even to bad states, the moment
the state decides an institution is worth more functioning than
raided.

Nigeria ran the opposite protocol with ten times the money. The
airline was raided continuously, in jobs, tickets, contracts, and
appointments. The regulator scarcely existed as an independent
force. Civil aviation was a directorate inside a ministry, budgeted
as an afterthought, structurally unable to say no to a national
carrier that outranked it in every currency Nigerian life
recognized. The referee was on the payroll of the home team.

The 1970s were the years when this arrangement was affordable,
because oil paid for the slack. Boom era institutions are never
tested by booms. The test comes when the money stops and the
deferred maintenance, training, and regulation must be paid from an
empty account. In 1981 the oil price broke. What the long emergency
did to Nigerian aviation, one unordered spare part at a time, is the
next chapter. It is the reason the crashes of Part Two should be
read not as lightning strikes but as invoices.

The flag kept flying through all of it. That was the trouble with
the flag. It flew whether or not anything beneath it worked. A
symbol that cannot fail is the perfect roof under which everything
else can.
