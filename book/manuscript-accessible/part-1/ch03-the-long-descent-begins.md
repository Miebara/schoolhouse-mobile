# Chapter 3. The Long Descent Begins

Decay is rarely dramatic. It is a spare part not ordered. A
simulator session not renewed. A runway light that fails and is not
replaced. A salary paid two months late to a man whose signature
certifies that an aircraft is safe.

By the end of the 1980s a visitor to the Nigeria Airways maintenance
base could see the decade in a single exhibit. Along the apron sat
the grounded wide bodies, among them for a time a Boeing 747 that
engineers call a Christmas tree. An aircraft kept unflyable so its
parts could keep others flying. A pump to one sister ship.
Instruments to another. The airline was consuming its own body to
postpone the amputation of its schedule. Controlled parts borrowing
is legitimate at a well run airline. As a way of life it is a family
burning the furniture through a winter. Rational each cold night.
Fatal as a strategy. And always a sign of an income that no longer
matches a way of living.

The income of Nigeria had stopped matching everything. The oil
price broke in 1981 and collapsed in 1986. Federal revenue fell by
more than half in real terms. The naira lost more than nine tenths
of its dollar value across the decade. For aviation this was close
to an extinction event, because aviation is a dollar industry that
happens to earn local money. Aircraft are priced in dollars. So are
spare parts, insurance, simulator time, and even the charts in the
cockpit. A Nigerian airline in 1988 sold naira tickets at controlled
fares and owed dollars to everyone who kept its aircraft legal.
Every devaluation silently multiplied the real cost of
airworthiness. No coup plotter listed this among his grievances. But
when Part Two asks why Nigerian aircraft in the 1990s were old, why
spares were scarce, why training was deferred, the answer begins
here. The exchange rate is the least dramatic villain in this book
and among the most lethal.

## The politics of the descent

The money stopped. Politics then decided where the shortage would be
felt. The politics of the 1980s were military rule. A coup in 1983.
Another in 1985. Beneath them the steady fusion of state and
barracks.

Military government did three specific things to aviation.

First it finished off management continuity. The juntas treated the
airline as a command. Chief executives rotated on the coup cycle.
Each new manager suspended the contracts of the last. The
procurement pipeline, the dull artery through which the future of an
airline flows, was cut and rebuilt every two or three years. An
airline can survive poverty with patience. Ethiopian proved it in
the same decade with less money and a worse war. No airline survives
the abolition of its own memory.

Second, the military era normalized the exemption. Soldiers do not
queue. Regimes born of emergency govern by exception. This flight
will carry whom it is told. This inspection finding will be waived
because the schedule of the general requires it. Each exemption was
small. Their sum was a culture. Professionals of the era named it
the waiver culture. Rules were understood by everyone as the opening
position in a negotiation with power. Part Two is largely the record
of its price. Safety rules work only when they cannot be negotiated.
A rule negotiable for a general is negotiable, eventually, for
anyone with his phone number.

Third, and least visibly, the military era starved the referee.
Civil aviation oversight remained a directorate inside the ministry.
Its inspectors sat on civil service pay. Its budget was a rounding
error. Its independence was a legal fiction under a government in
which the airline chairman outranked the director of civil aviation
in every register that mattered. Inspector posts went unfilled.
Training abroad, a dollar expense, was among the first casualties.
By the end of the decade Nigeria possessed a regulator the way the
grounded 747 possessed engines. The housing was there. Everyone
spoke as if the function were too.

## Structural adjustment and the birth of the private airline

In 1986 Nigeria adopted a structural adjustment program. Devaluation,
deregulation, privatization. Its social history is bitter. Its
aviation consequence was specific. It created the Nigerian private
airline.

The logic was orthodox. The flag carrier was a fiscal hemorrhage and
a monopoly. Private capital would supply what it no longer could.
Licenses flowed from 1988, and by the early 1990s a swarm of private
carriers had risen. Okada Air, Kabo, ADC, and dozens more. Some were
serious enterprises. Okada at its peak flew a fleet that outnumbered
many flag carriers. ADC was building a modern airline. The public,
offered an alternative to an airline that canceled by habit, voted
with its feet. Domestic aviation grew even as the economy shrank.

But look at what had been done. Deregulation multiplied the
operators tenfold at the exact moment the capacity to oversee even
one had been starved to the bone. The new airlines flew the aircraft
that adjustment era dollars could buy. Elderly jets, second and
third hand, cheap to acquire and hungry to maintain. The pilots and
engineers were mostly alumni of the flag carrier and Zaria. Good
people scattered across a dozen thin balance sheets, working for
owners for whom a grounded aircraft was a bleeding artery. Above the
new ecosystem stood the same ministry directorate with the same
handful of inspectors, now responsible for an industry ten times the
size.

The license itself deserves a closer look, because its terms shaped
the fleet Part Two buries. In mature systems an air operator
certificate is among the hardest documents in commerce. Proving out
manuals, maintenance, training, and finances takes years. The
Nigerian certificate of the late 1980s was substantially a political
and commercial document. Capital requirements nominal. Financial
review cursory. Technical examination performed by the starved
directorate. Applicants ranged from serious aviation people to
traders diversifying out of shipping, to fronts for whom an airline
was chiefly a claim on foreign exchange at the official rate. The
register filled with entities for whom the airline was not the
business but the instrument. An instrument gets exactly as much
maintenance as its usefulness requires. When Chapter 12 counts the
corporate graveyard, remember where the headstones were quarried. In
a licensing regime that priced the right to carry the public at
about the cost of the file folder.

America had run this experiment first, and the comparison is exact
enough to be useful. American deregulation in 1978 also unleashed
entry and marginal operators. The predicted carnage never came,
because deregulation of the market was paired with reinforcement of
the referee. The FAA, the investigator, the courts, and the insurers
were mature before the pathogens multiplied. Nigeria ran the same
experiment with the immune system switched off. The 1990s supplied
the laboratory results.

## The two fleets

No feature of the era shows its priorities like the one fleet that
stayed perfect. While the national carrier ate itself, the
presidential fleet was renewed, expanded, and maintained to
standards the civil system could only remember. Its budget lived
outside scrutiny. Its hangars were the best in the country. Its
existence answered the question of whether the Nigerian state knew
how to keep aircraft airworthy. It knew. Airworthiness followed
power, not capability. Treat the presidential fleet throughout this
book as the control group. Proof, parked in Abuja, that every
deficiency elsewhere was a choice of allocation.

Below it the private era produced its own aristocracy. The
emblematic figure was the owner of Okada Air, whose fleet of old
British jets became the largest private air force in Africa,
assembled at scrap prices. Okada was a genuine achievement. It moved
millions the flag carrier stranded. It was also the pathologies of
the era incorporated. A fleet chosen by purchase price. Maintenance
litigated between ambition and cash flow. A proprietor whose
political weight was itself a regulatory fact. The airline faded
through the 1990s under the arithmetic Chapter 12 explains. Old
aircraft age expensively. Its hulks joined the fringes of Nigerian
airports, parted out and sun bleached. The ramps of a country are
its financial statements printed in aluminum. By 1995 the Nigerian
ramp read as follows. Assets acquired 1965 to 1985. Depreciation
catastrophic. Provision for renewal none.

Between the two fleets, the perfect presidential one and the
improvised public one, the middle stratum was exactly what the era
did not build. A functioning national system. Societies do not
maintain what their decision makers do not depend on. It is the
oldest sentence in political economy. The 1980s sky was its
illustration.

## What flying felt like

Statistics numb. Descend to the departure lounge. Millions of
Nigerians carry this memory.

You bought a ticket, but the ticket was the start of a negotiation.
Boarding could be a physical competition in which the passenger list
was reconciled to reality afterward, if at all. Note that word,
manifest, and keep it. In 1996 and again in 2005 the country would
learn at the worst moments that knowing who is aboard a Nigerian
airliner was not a certainty but an investigation. A society learns
its record keeping in ordinary time and pays for it in extraordinary
time.

Around the terminal the state had reorganized into an alphabet of
agencies. Airports under one body. Airspace later under another.
Weather under a third. The alphabet matters less than the pattern.
Each crisis was answered by subdivision rather than empowerment.
More institutions than at independence, less capacity. Organograms
are cheap. Inspectors are expensive. Governments under siege buy the
first and defer the second.

And yet the system worked daily, in the sense that mattered.
Aircraft mostly arrived. The pilots were often superb precisely
because the environment demanded it. Veterans describe hand flown
approaches into airports whose landing systems had died years
before. Fuel decisions made against forecasts that were folklore. A
generation of aviators became virtuosos of the workaround.
Virtuosity of that kind is magnificent and corrosive. It keeps the
schedule alive, and by keeping the schedule alive it hides the decay
from the public and the treasury. The workaround is the enemy of the
work order. Systems that run on heroism are spending their margins
invisibly every day, until the day the heroism is one contingency
short. Nobody can say in advance which day that will be.

## The association men

One set of institutions crossed the descent intact. The professional
associations. The pilots and engineers in their union. The
controllers in theirs. The licensed engineers in their guilds.

Their survival was not an accident. They were funded by members, not
by the treasury. They distributed no rents worth capturing. Their
leaders were elected and could not be reshuffled by decree. Through
the worst decades they did, in their limited way, what the state had
abandoned. Their memoranda on radar and runway conditions form the
best documentary record of the decay. Their licensing discipline
kept individual competence credentialed while institutional
competence dissolved. Their strike threats periodically forced
safety items onto agendas nothing else could reach. Their limits
were structural. Member bodies defend members first, and their
warnings, filed without allies, became the unanswered mail this book
keeps exhuming. But when reform finally came, its staffing ran
straight through them. Any future reform will too. In the politics
of Chapter 16 the associations are the permanent skeleton of a
reform constituency. Organized, fluent, immortal, and waiting for a
state worth advising.

## What the auditors saw

External eyes documented the descent as it happened. Their testimony
deserves exhumation because it proves the decay was known,
recorded, and answered with nothing.

The World Bank transport reviews of the 1980s read like the table of
contents of Part Two written in advance. Navigation aids below
standard. Maintenance funding a fraction of need. The flag carrier
beyond audit. ICAO missions filed parallel findings in the polite
language of the United Nations. The assessments that foreign
airlines commissioned before serving Lagos recorded the deficiencies
item by item, and the carriers priced them in, flying their own fuel
and engineers rather than trusting the system. Even the internal
organs spoke. Ministry reviews. Audit queries filed annually into
the void.

The point is sharp. No one can claim the descent was invisible or
knowable only in hindsight. It was watched, measured, and filed by
five institutional observers across fifteen years. A documented
emergency answered by no budget. The failure of the 1980s was never
a failure of information. What was missing was a mechanism to
convert known facts into forced choices. This book calls that
mechanism a constituency. Its eventual construction out of funerals
is the story of Part Two.

## The decade in one invoice

Total what the 1980s subtracted, because Part Two draws on every
line.

From the airline. The fleet halved and halved again. The maintenance
base run down past regeneration. The cadet schemes suspended. The
international stations surrendered one by one, each surrender
permanent.

From the infrastructure. The navigation aid replacement cycle
abandoned midway, leaving the 1990s a scatter of dead beacons. The
radar deferred into legend. The weather service thinned toward
blindness.

From the institutions. The inspector corps aged without replacement.
A hiring freeze is a time bomb with a fifteen year fuse. The freeze
of the mid 1980s detonated as the inspection deficit of 1999. Zaria
lost its instructors to the Gulf. The accident investigation
function shrank to a filing convention.

From the professions. The diaspora, mapped below.

And from the culture, the most expensive line. A decade of daily
lessons that decline was normal, that workaround was competence,
and that the gap between regulation and reality was not a scandal
but the weather.

Nigeria in 1990 was not simply a poor country with old planes. It
was a country that had possessed much of a functioning aviation
system within living memory and had consumed it. Decline from
possession has a different physics than poverty. The forms all
remain. The directorates, the checklists, the uniforms. Emptied.
That is why every audit kept finding a system that existed in every
particular except function. Countries that never had the thing must
build it. Countries that ate it must first admit the eating. The
admission took Nigeria until 2006, and it was extracted syllable by
syllable by the dead.

## The airline dies slowly

The final act of Nigeria Airways ran through the 1990s to a
conclusion in 2003, but the death was legible by 1990. The fleet
shrank toward single digits. The New York route ended. The European
stations closed. The airline that had flown four continents finished
as an unreliable domestic operator leasing out its London slot. What
stayed vigorous to the end was its function as an extraction site.
The liquidation revealed the expected archaeology. Debts in the
hundreds of millions of dollars. Pensions paid partially and late
over years of agony. The employees were the last creditors standing.
Men who had certified 747s queued for pensions that arrived in
fractions.

The exodus deserves its mechanics. The 1980s created a supply shock,
a collapsing airline full of world certified professionals paid in a
dissolving currency. And a demand shock, the great Gulf expansion
plus the standing hunger of Britain and America for experienced
crews. The clearing price was never close. What is remarkable is not
that thousands left but how many stayed, held by family and by
stubborn hope. And the leaving selected for the connective tissue.
The training captains. The check airmen. The senior engineers. The
people who reproduce competence in others. An institution can
survive losing talent. It cannot survive losing its teachers,
because that loss compounds. By the 1990s Nigerian aviation ran on
the last cohorts the old pipeline had finished. More than one
accident in Part Two has, among its quiet factors, the thinness of
the bench behind the crews.

Two consequences of the long death mattered most. The first was
human capital flight itself. The diaspora became a national export
and a standing rebuke. The excellence was real and it was improving
the safety statistics of other countries. The second was
reputational. For lessors, insurers, and foreign regulators,
Nigerian aviation came to mean unpaid bills and unanswered letters.
The private airlines of the 1990s bought aircraft, insurance, and
credibility at prices marked up by the default of the flag. Nations
pay compound interest on their reputations.

The comparative note is Ghana, whose flag carrier ran the same
trajectory to the same end. Same colonial parent. Same patronage
capture. Same death. The parallel is close enough to count as
replication. The control case, again, is Ethiopian, which entered
the 1990s having survived a regime change more violent than anything
in Nigeria and left the decade profitable and expanding. The
variable was never Africa, poverty, or turbulence. It was whether
the state treated the airline as an institution or as a carcass.

## The abandoned

The liquidation of 2003 and 2004 closed a company and opened a
wound that stayed open for the better part of twenty years, and no
honest account of Nigerian aviation leaves it out.

Nigeria Airways went down carrying roughly five thousand serving
staff and a longer tail of retirees. Pilots. Licensed engineers.
Cabin crew. Ticket clerks and loaders and cleaners. Many of them had
given the airline two and three decades. Their severance, their
pensions, and their accumulated entitlements became claims against
the estate of a dead airline that had been owned, funded, and
finally killed by the state that now had to settle them.

The federal government promised settlement, and then promised it
again. What followed was a generation of waiting.

The former workers organized into associations of the retrenched.
They picketed the ministry gates. They petitioned the National
Assembly through four administrations. Their leaders grew old in the
role and became professional petitioners, arriving at committee
after committee with the same verified files and hearing the same
sympathetic language. Journalists who covered aviation in those
years came to know them by name.

And the associations began keeping a second ledger beside the ledger
of what they were owed. It recorded the members who had died
waiting. Former captains who had flown wide bodies across the
Atlantic finished their lives in penury. Licensed engineers made
public appeals for money to treat illnesses. By the counts the
unions kept, never contradicted by any better official figure, the
dead ran into the thousands before the state substantially paid.

Movement came at last in 2017 and 2018, when the government approved
a settlement package reported at roughly forty five billion naira,
with payments beginning in tranches from 2018 and with verification
disputes and residual claims running on for years after that. The
payment was real and it should be recorded as real.

So should its arithmetic. Settled some fifteen years late, in a
currency that had lost most of its value across the interval, the
money delivered a fraction of the purchasing power the work had
earned. The difference was quietly taken by time and inflation from
the people least able to absorb the loss.

That story belongs in a book about safety, and not as a footnote,
because its effects ran forward into the safety of the system.

Institutions run on deferred payment. A pension is the purest form
of it. Work now, be paid later, on the strength of a promise the
institution makes about its own future. An employer that defaults on
the deferred teaches every serving employee a lesson, and the lesson
is learned quickly and never unlearned. Take everything now, or
leave.

Every working professional in Nigerian aviation spent twenty years
watching what the gratitude of the state to its aviation workforce
looked like in practice, and priced what they saw. The exodus this
chapter has already described was driven by Gulf salaries, and it
was also driven by something the salary tables never showed. A
Nigerian aviation career carried terminal benefits that were a
lottery ticket drawn against the attention span of the treasury.

Which produces a finding that no aviation authority anywhere lists
among its safety indicators, and several of them should. A state
that defaults on its aviation pensions is degrading its own future
inspectorate.

The former staff of Nigeria Airways paid for the death of the
airline twice. Once in their livelihoods. And once again as the
cautionary tale that helped drain their successors abroad.

## 1990

Set the stage as the decade closes.

A country of roughly ninety million people. Treasury gutted.
Currency a fraction of itself. A military regime entering its
baroque late period. A flag carrier in terminal decline, bleeding
its competence into the cockpits of the world. A new private sector,
vigorous and undercapitalized, flying the retired aircraft of the
rich world into airports built for the boom and maintained by the
bust. Navigation aids failing. Radar partial. Fire services
notional. A regulator that existed chiefly on paper, inside a
ministry, under soldiers, in a culture where every rule had its
waiver. A training college on fumes. An investigation function that
had never once given the public a full account of why their dead
were dead.

No single person intended this. That is the point of these first
three chapters and the reason they precede the wreckage. The system
that would kill thousands over the next two decades was not built by
villains. It was accumulated by deferral. Colonial defaults never
revised. Boom purchases never institutionalized. Bust economics
never confronted. Each decade handed the next a slightly hollower
inheritance and a more practiced vocabulary of denial. By 1990 the
holes in the layers of defense were drifting into alignment. The
system now required only traffic, time, and weather. The 1990s
would supply all three.

The first invoice arrived in July 1991, at a runway in Saudi
Arabia, in the oldest costume in Nigerian aviation. A chartered
aircraft. A foreign crew. The pilgrims of the Hajj.
