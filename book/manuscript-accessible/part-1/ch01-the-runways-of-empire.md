# PART I. THE ASCENT 1925 TO 1990

# Chapter 1. The Runways of Empire

The airplane arrived in Nigeria before the idea of Nigeria had
settled.

On the first day of November 1925 a formation of Royal Air Force
biplanes came out of the dusty haze and circled the walls of Kano.
The city below was a thousand years old. Its walls had seen caravans
from Tripoli and armies from Sokoto. Only twenty two years earlier
they had seen British guns. Now the city gained a new kind of gate.
The aircraft had crossed Africa in stages to prove that the British
Empire could move through African skies. Crowds gathered to watch
them land on a cleared strip. The emir received the airmen with
ceremony. And a precedent was set that would shape Nigerian aviation
for a century. The airplane came to Nigeria as an instrument of
somebody else. It landed on Nigerian soil but answered to
institutions an ocean away.

Hold that image. This book returns to it in forms those first
watchers could not imagine. A Canadian crewed charter burning over
Saudi Arabia with Nigerian pilgrims aboard. A British built jet
falling into a Kano neighborhood seventy seven years later. The
history of aviation in Nigeria is a history of imported systems.
Machines, rules, routes, and expectations designed elsewhere and
grafted onto local institutions that were starved or improvised.
Where the graft took, Nigerians flew safely. Where it did not, they
fell. This chapter asks what exactly was planted in those early
decades. The shape of the roots explains the shape of the tree.

## An empire discovers the shortcut

Aviation came to Africa in the 1920s for the same reason railways
came in the 1890s. Imperial logistics. The journey from London to
Lagos by sea took two weeks. Aircraft promised to collapse the
distance and to police the empire cheaply.

The route of those 1925 flights tells you their purpose. They did
not begin in Lagos or follow the population. They ran across the dry
belt, linking British possessions along the old caravan lines. A
corridor drawn for the convenience of the map of the colonizer. When
civil aviation followed in the 1930s it obeyed the same logic. The
airline routes of empire worked down the edges of the continent
toward Cape Town, with West Africa as a branch. Kano became one of
the busiest aviation crossroads in Africa. Not because northern
Nigeria produced traffic but because it sat on the way to somewhere
else.

Three features of colonial aviation proved very durable.

First, the network pointed outward. The routes that received money
connected Nigeria to London. Internal connections were an
afterthought. At independence Nigeria inherited a few good
international airports and a thin scatter of domestic strips. Sixty
years later the pattern persists. The Lagos to London route remains
among the most valuable in Africa while domestic aviation, where
nearly all the crashes happened, stayed the neglected child.

Second, regulation lived abroad. Standards, licensing, and accident
investigation were run from Britain by British staff. In the short
run this was a kind of gift. Colonial Nigeria was overseen by one of
the most competent systems in the world. But it meant that when the
flag came down Nigeria inherited the activity of aviation without
the organs of aviation. The regulator, the investigator, and the
training pipeline were branch offices of institutions headquartered
elsewhere. The institutional memory sailed home with the
expatriates.

Third, aviation was fused with prestige from the start. The 1925
landings were staged as theater. Flight arrived in Nigeria as a
show of power, not as a utility, and it never fully lost that
character. Every later government treated aviation partly as a
stage. The cost of treating a safety critical industry as a stage
fills much of this book.

## The Kano crossroads

Between the wars Kano became a true node of world traffic. The long
range aircraft of the day needed a stop in the dry belt, and Kano
had the airfield, the fuel, and the rest house. Its guest lists in
the 1930s read like a gazette of the imperial world in motion.
Governors, engineers, correspondents heading to wars, aristocrats on
tour. For the city the airfield meant contracts, jobs, and pride.
The caravan city now had a runway. Aviation reached the north first,
and the northern relationship with flight, above all the pilgrimage
traffic to Mecca, grew its own history and its own tragedies. Kano
in 1973. Jeddah in 1991. Kano again in 2002.

The crossroads era also taught a first lesson in aviation economics
that Nigeria forgot and relearned expensively. Hub traffic is
rented, never owned. Kano mattered only while aircraft needed a
Sahel stop. The long range airliners after the war flew over it, and
the transit traffic vanished in a decade. A windfall position had
been mistaken for a permanent endowment, and nothing was invested in
the system beneath it while the rent flowed. The same mistake would
be repeated at national scale with oil. The final chapter of this
book asks whether Lagos can win the West African hub of this
century. That is the Kano question returned after a hundred years.
Traffic goes where the system is, and stays only as long as the
system deserves it.

## The world that was watching

Widen the lens to the world of 1925. Aviation was barely a
technology and mostly a promise. The Atlantic had been flown nonstop
only six years earlier. Navigation was by railway line and river
bend. Yet every serious government had concluded that the promise
was strategic. The Paris Convention of 1919, the first air law,
established that every state has full sovereignty over the airspace
above it. In a colonized Africa that meant the skies belonged to the
empires. The Chicago Convention of 1944 kept the principle. What
changed in 1960 was only the flag to which Nigerian air sovereignty
attached.

Note the mismatch buried in that law. It assigns every state the
duties of a strong state. Oversight, investigation, enforcement.
It never asks whether the state can afford them. Nigeria inherited
the responsibilities of France with the leftover capacities of a
colony. Nobody designed that gap. Everyone in this book lives
inside it.

One more thing about that era. Aviation in 1925 was deadly
everywhere. The airlines of Europe and America consumed pilots at
rates that would horrify a modern reader. No country started safe.
What distinguished the next century was that some societies built,
decade by decade, the machinery that turned a stunt into a utility.
The gap between a maintenance hangar in Atlanta and one in Lagos in
1995 was manufactured by choices, year by year. In 1925 the ledger
stood at zero everywhere. Hold that against every temptation to
read what follows as destiny.

## The war that built the runways

The Second World War gave Nigeria an aviation infrastructure, and
built it for reasons that had nothing to do with Nigeria. From 1940
Britain needed to deliver aircraft to its armies in Egypt without
sailing past enemy airpower. The answer ran through West Africa.
Crated fighters were shipped to the coast, assembled, and flown
across the continent in stages. Lagos to Kano, Kano onward to Sudan
and Cairo. At its peak the route moved hundreds of aircraft a month.
The Americans arrived after 1941 and enlarged everything.

The war left three legacies. The first was concrete. Extended
runways, hangars, fuel farms, radios. A physical plant that carried
Nigerian civil aviation for a generation. The second was human.
Thousands of Nigerians worked the route as fitters, loaders, and
clerks. A few flew. Peter Thomas of Lagos became the first Nigerian
commissioned into the Royal Air Force, and his example planted an
idea. The cockpit could be Nigerian too.

The third legacy was subtler. The wartime system worked brilliantly
because it was run as a closed loop by organizations that owned
every link. Their aircraft, their mechanics, their fuel testers,
their discipline. When the war ended the organizations left and took
the loop with them. What remained was hardware. From the very start
Nigeria experienced world class aviation as something that happened
on Nigerian soil without passing into Nigerian hands. The
difference between owning the artifacts of aviation and owning its
institutions is the oldest theme in this story.

## The aerodrome at the water edge

The early Lagos air station was a world of corrugated iron and
whitewash. A windsock, a radio hut, fuel in drums rolled by hand.
The mail was the true paying passenger. It seems quaint until you
see what it meant. For the first time a schedule connected Lagos to
London in days rather than weeks. The colonial paperwork economy
reorganized around it. Aviation served the map of extraction and
administration, and no Nigerian community was consulted.

The labor deserves its own paragraph. Runways were cleared and
graded by Nigerian hands at colonial wages. The fuel, the baggage,
the endless grass cutting, all Nigerian labor under a thin
expatriate layer that held every certified trade. A Nigerian could
fuel the aircraft but not sign for it. He could fix the engine under
supervision but not certify the fix. The line between doing aviation
work and holding aviation authority was the deepest transfer of the
colonial system to its successor. Decades later, when the American
regulator finally certified Nigerian oversight as an equal in 2010,
the professionals felt it as a second independence. This chapter is
where that feeling was born.

## What the first accidents taught and whom

Colonial aviation was dangerous and nobody pretended otherwise.
Aircraft went down along the African routes with grim regularity.
The response to each crash followed a fixed ritual, and the details
matter.

When an aircraft crashed in colonial Nigeria an inspector
investigated under British rules. The findings went to London, were
filed in London, and changed standards kept in London. The system
was good for its time. Britain understood early that the purpose of
investigation is prevention, not punishment. But note what it meant
here. The colony experienced the crashes. The metropole collected
the learning. The feedback loop that turns accidents into safety ran
through Nigeria without ever touching down.

The deeper habit was about publicity. Colonial accident findings
were internal papers, released to the public rarely and partially.
The idea that a report is owed to the public, that the state must
explain a disaster to the people it fell on, was not part of the
inheritance. When independent Nigeria later sat on reports for years
or forever it was not innovating. It was continuing an
administrative tradition. Institutions pass their reflexes to their
successors. Secrecy is a reflex.

## The corporation years

After the war Britain merged its West African air services into one
body worth remembering. The West African Airways Corporation, WAAC,
founded in 1946 to serve Nigeria, the Gold Coast, Sierra Leone, and
the Gambia. It was a public corporation on the model of the BBC.
Jointly owned by four governments, run from Lagos by professionals,
overseen from Britain.

Judged on its own terms WAAC worked. It flew small airliners on a
web of West African routes. It trained the first generation of
Nigerian ground staff and eventually aircrew. It ran on schedule.
And its structure was everything its successors would not be. It
answered to four governments, which meant no single politician could
raid it. Its safety oversight sat in an external system too far away
to be lobbied.

Watch how it worked on an ordinary morning in 1952. The engineer
signed the daily inspection in a bound log. The load sheet was
computed and countersigned. If an engine check failed at start the
flight canceled. And here is the point. Nobody called anybody. There
was nobody to call. The decision belonged to the system, not to a
negotiation. Passengers grumbled and waited for the afternoon
service. The airplane did not crash. Multiply that uncancelled phone
call across a decade and you have a safety record built out of pure
governance, on 1950s technology, in the same skies that fill Part
Two of this book with wreckage. The variable was never the
continent, the weather, or the century.

Who flew in those years? Colonial officers, expatriate businessmen,
and in growing numbers the Nigerian elite of the independence
generation. Lawyers to constitutional talks. Traders to Accra.
Politicians to Lagos. The airplane was where the future felt real.
The nationalist press covered flight with passion, and one day
Nigerians will fly these planes became a line of the platform
speeches. The ambition was honest and honorable. But note its
object. The ambition was to fly the planes. To hold the visible
seats. Nobody campaigns to inherit the airworthiness inspectorate.
The invisible seats went unclaimed for decades. Glory was
nationalized at once. Drudgery was deferred indefinitely. That
asymmetry is as close as this book comes to a single theory of what
followed.

As the Gold Coast moved to independence its leader pulled his
country out of WAAC to found a national airline of its own. The
logic of the shared corporation collapsed. Nigeria followed, and the
Nigerian operations became a company that would carry the new
national name and its hopes. The regional corporation dissolved into
national fragments within two years. A professionally run airline
owned by four states, too big for any one government to raid, was
exactly the kind of institution West Africa would spend the next
half century failing to build. It existed. It was dismantled for the
flag. Ghana Airways died in 2004. Nigeria Airways died in 2003. The
little planes of WAAC were carrying more than passengers.

## The decade of handover

Decolonization ran faster than any plan. Institutions that assumed a
generation to train Nigerians were given five years. In aviation the
arithmetic was brutal. A pilot took roughly a decade to reach
command. An engineer took as long. An airworthiness surveyor, the
person who inspects the inspectors, took longer still. There was no
Nigerian cohort in the pipeline in 1950 because the pipeline had
been closed to Nigerians. So there was none ready in 1960 regardless
of money or goodwill.

The new state faced the standard menu. Keep expatriates. Hire
international agencies. Promote the available Nigerians faster than
the ladders were built for and hope experience caught up. Nigeria
did all three in proportions set by budget crises.

Oversight fared worst because it was invisible. Political attention
went to the visible transfers. The first Nigerian captain was
celebrated like a boxing champion. The airline was bought out fully
by 1961 so the flag would be clean. Meanwhile the expatriate
surveyors ran down their contracts and were replaced slowly or
nominally. The British airworthiness system stopped covering
Nigerian aircraft with no equivalent built in Lagos. Nobody decided
this in a memo. It happened by the sum of attention paid elsewhere.
It set the pattern this book traces to 2006. Operating roles
Nigerianized quickly and well. Oversight roles did not. Operations
had glamour and a wage market. Oversight had neither. States staff
what they love.

## The archive speaks

A note on evidence closes the chapter. The colonial era left an
exhaustive paper record. The files in London and in the Nigerian
archives hold the monthly equipment returns, the land disputes, the
labor musters, the incident inquiries of every airstrip. The record
is imperial and partial. But it exists, indexed and retrievable,
because that system treated documentation as the substance of
administration.

Set beside it the record of the Nigerian 1980s and 1990s. Vanished
registries. Unfindable reports. A historian can reconstruct the
aviation administration of 1935 more completely than that of 1985.
That inversion carries the institutional lesson of this chapter in
its purest form. Record keeping is the load bearing habit. It is the
floor under oversight, accountability, and memory. States that write
things down can be audited, reformed, and understood. States that
stop cannot. Not even by themselves.

## What was planted

By the first of October 1960, when the green and white flag rose
over Lagos, the deep structure of the next sixty years was in place.

The physical inheritance was real. Runways at Kano, Lagos, and Port
Harcourt. Trained ground staff. Membership in the world system built
at Chicago. The institutional inheritance was hollower than it
looked. Regulation had been an imported service, now withdrawn. The
airline was three parts symbol to one part system. The network faced
outward. And the political meaning of aviation, as spectacle and
sovereignty, had been set by the empire in 1925 and adopted without
revision by the nationalists who replaced it.

None of this made disaster inevitable. Ethiopia alone proves that
the institutions of flight can be built deliberately, even in
poverty, when a state decides to want them. But inheritance sets the
default, and defaults govern whenever attention fails. Nigeria began
with defaults set by an empire that needed runways, not
institutions, and with a political class that needed a flag in the
sky. The next chapter is about that flag. How brightly it flew, and
what was quietly not built beneath it.
