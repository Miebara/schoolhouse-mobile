# Chapter 12. The Economics of Cutting Corners

Follow one aircraft through its life, because the journey explains
more about Nigerian aviation than any regulation ever written.

A Boeing 727 rolls out of a factory in Washington state in 1968 and
goes to work for a major American carrier. For fifteen years it
lives the pampered life of a flagship fleet. It is maintained to the
full programme of the manufacturer, in hangars the airline owns, by
engineers the airline trained. Its records are immaculate. Every
part can be traced. Every cycle is logged. Its technical file is,
in a real sense, worth more than its aluminium.

Then the fare wars of the early 1980s and a new generation of
quieter and thriftier jets push it down the food chain. To a charter
operator. Then to a second tier scheduled carrier. Then to a third.
Each owner extracts value while investing less. The maintenance
stays legal, and that point matters, but it is outsourced and then
outsourced again, and the records pass from custodian to custodian
like a title deed collecting stamps in a jurisdiction that keeps
changing.

By the early 1990s the aircraft is twenty five years old. It is
worth less than one of its own overhauled engines. Noise rules have
closed most of the airports of the rich world to it. Its residual
value is approaching scrap. And it is now, for the first time,
precisely affordable to a startup in Lagos that earns naira and is
starved of dollars. It is ferried south.

Nothing about that transaction is illegal. And nothing about that
aircraft is safe by default. At twenty five years an airframe is a
promise that has to be actively kept, through heavy checks costing
millions of dollars, parts priced like jewellery, and engineering
judgement that costs most of all. It has just been sold, precisely
and only, to the buyer least equipped to keep it.

## The sorting machine

That last sentence is not an accident of Nigerian history. It is the
predictable output of a market working exactly the way markets work,
and the mechanism deserves to be stated plainly because it governs
far more than aircraft.

Markets sort assets to whoever values them most. In a second hand
market for a safety critical asset, valuing something most means
being able to extract the most revenue from it at the lowest cost of
capital.

Now ask what determines the cost to an operator of keeping the
promise. It is not the aircraft, because the engineering requirement
is identical wherever the aircraft flies. A crack grows at the same
rate in Lagos as in Frankfurt. What differs is the expected cost of
not keeping the promise.

In a country with a working regulator, an aggressive system of civil
liability, insurers who take their own risk seriously, and a deep
resale market that punishes bad records, the expected cost of
deferred maintenance is high. Operators therefore bid for the
aircraft on the assumption that they will maintain it fully.

In a country where oversight is weak, courts are slow, insurers do
not inspect, and records cannot be verified, the expected cost of
deferral falls close to zero. Operators can therefore bid for the
same aircraft on the assumption that they will maintain it
partially, which means they can bid more for it, or accept lower
fares for it, and still make money.

So the weak jurisdiction wins the auction. Not through corruption.
Through arithmetic. Risk flows downhill, toward whoever is least
able to price it.

The old fleet did not drift to Nigeria. It was sorted there.

## The graveyard

The corporate cemetery of Nigerian aviation is large enough to
support statistics, and the statistics reorganize several of the
narratives in this book.

Count the carriers licensed from the deregulation wave onward. Okada
Air. Kabo. Triax. Gas Air. Concord. Oriental. ADC. Bellview.
Sosoliso. EAS. Chanchangi. Albarka. Space World. Afrijet. Slok. Air
Midwest. First Nation. Discovery. Medview. Virgin Nigeria and its
successors. And onward through the 2010s. That is several dozen
scheduled or substantial charter operators licensed across roughly
three decades, and the survivors into the middle 2020s can be
counted on one hand. The working life of the median operating
certificate ran well under a decade.

Now hold that number against the maintenance horizon of the
airframes those certificates were flying. A heavy structural check
cycle runs on multi year intervals. Fatigue analysis works on
horizons of a decade and more. Corrosion control programmes assume
continuous custody across the entire life of the aircraft.

The expected survival of the median firm was shorter than the
inspection cycle of the median airframe.

Which produces the central finding of this chapter, and it is not a
moral one. The safety crisis and the corporate mortality crisis were
the same crisis, recorded in two different ledgers.

Firms that cannot survive commercially cannot invest in five year
airworthiness horizons. Not because their owners are wicked, but
because the investment lies beyond the expected lifespan of the
company and therefore outside its decision frame entirely. A firm
that expects to die in three years is not unwilling to fear a metal
problem due in seven. It is structurally incapable of fearing it.

Walk the rows of the cemetery and the causes of death cluster into
five.

There was undercapitalization at birth, with licences issued against
paid up capital that would not have bought a spare engine, let alone
funded a heavy check.

There was the currency scissors, in which naira revenue met dollar
costs with a central bank allocation queue sitting between them.
Chapter 3 set out the mechanism, and every carrier in the graveyard
died with a foreign exchange story attached to it.

There was fleet economics, meaning the geriatric trap that is cheap
to enter and ruinous to sustain, in which maintenance costs rise
with age at exactly the point when the revenue earning reliability
of the aircraft falls.

There was founder dependence, in which the airline is held as a
personal estate and dies with the fortunes, the attention, or the
life of its patron.

And there was, repeatedly, the spiral after a crash, in which a
single hull loss destroyed a brand, its bookings, and its
insurability inside a quarter. Bellview and Sosoliso never
meaningfully outlived 2005. ADC survived 1996 only to be finished by
2006. EAS was gone within the year of Kano.

That last cause deserves a comment, because it looks like market
discipline and is not quite. The market did punish the killing of
passengers. But it punished only after the fact, which is an
enforcement mechanism structurally identical to the politics of
funerals followed by reform that this book has criticized
throughout. Both systems learned only in arrears.

## What the deferral actually buys

Return to the decision at the centre of it all and price it out
properly, because deferred maintenance stays an abstraction until
you see the competing claims on a single scarce dollar.

A heavy check costs seven figures in dollars, plus weeks of lost
revenue on an aircraft that may be a third of the fleet.

Competing for the same dollars in the same month are four other
claims. The lease payment, whose default means repossession and
therefore the loss of the aircraft and with it the company. The
insurance premium, whose lapse means immediate grounding. The fuel
bill, without which nothing moves today. And the payroll, without
which the engineers leave for the Gulf.

Of those five claims on the same dollar, exactly one can be deferred
within the letter of the regulations, because legitimate deferral
mechanisms exist in every aviation system on earth and have to. The
lease payment cannot be deferred at all.

So every incentive in the structure, quarter after quarter, points
the scarce dollar away from the hangar. And here is what makes the
arrangement lethal rather than merely regrettable. The consequence
of deferral is invisible and delayed. The consequence of default is
visible and immediate. Metal fatigue does not send a demand letter.

The corners were not cut by monsters. They were cut by spreadsheets,
rationally, one defensible cell at a time, inside firms whose
bankruptcy was arriving faster than the fatigue of their airframes.

## What a check is buying

It is worth understanding what the money purchases, because the
physical reality is what makes the arithmetic so unforgiving.

The maintenance programme of an airliner is a pyramid. At the base
sit the daily and transit checks, which cover walk arounds, fluid
levels, and the condition of tires and brakes. Nobody skips them,
because their absence is visible by lunchtime. Above them sit the
lettered checks, rising in depth and interval, from overnight
inspections every few weeks to progressively deeper work. At the top
sit the heavy checks, in which the aircraft is taken out of service
for weeks, opened down to its structure, and inspected for the
cracks and corrosion that fatigue mathematics predicts and only
instruments confirm.

The heavy checks are where airworthiness actually lives, and the
intervals are not administrative conveniences. They are set by
damage tolerance analysis, in which engineers calculate how long a
crack takes to grow from detectable to critical and then set the
inspection interval at a fraction of that time, commonly a half or a
third, so that the crack will be found on at least two inspections
before it matters.

Defer the check and you are not skipping a formality. You are
spending the safety margin the engineers reserved for error. You are
flying into the interval that was built to absorb the inspection
that missed something, on the bet that this particular airframe is
average.

Old aircraft raise the stakes twice over, because their inspection
programmes thicken with age. Supplemental structural inspections.
Corrosion control. The aging aircraft directives written after the
cabin roof of a Boeing 737 peeled open at altitude over Hawaii in
1988, killing a flight attendant and exposing an entire generation
of assumptions about metal fatigue as inadequate. So the requirement
rises at exactly the moment when the capacity of the owner to meet
it falls.

## The bazaar

Between the airline and the airframe stands the spares economy, and
the Nigerian version of it deserves attention, because it is where
the abstractions of this chapter became physical objects moving
through customs.

A transport aircraft is several hundred thousand parts, and each
part has a paper twin. A release certificate attests to the origin
of the component, its overhaul status, and its remaining life. In
economic terms the certificate is the part. An engine bearing with
clean paper is worth many multiples of an identical bearing without
it, and the integrity of the entire global system rests on that
paper chain surviving every sale, every shop visit, and every border
crossing.

Now run that system through the Nigeria of the crisis decades.
Carriers starved of dollars needed parts yesterday. A customs regime
could hold a shipment for an aircraft on the ground for weeks, which
turned legality itself into schedule risk. And a brokered grey
market running through Miami, Dubai, and Lagos was perfectly willing
to supply tagged components whose documentation had been laundered
somewhere along a chain that began in salvage yards and part outs of
uncertain history.

The rational airline met the spares decision in exactly the form in
which the tire decision reached the managers of Nationair at Jeddah.
The compliant route was slow and ruinously priced. The grey route
was fast, cheap, and papered well enough to pass the inspection
depth the regulator of that era could bring to bear.

Bogus and suspect parts became a global scandal in the 1990s, and
the American regulator built an entire enforcement apparatus around
them. No serious student of the period believes the exposure of the
Nigerian fleet was below the world average. Where a suspect
component actually failed, nobody can say, and that is the point of
laundered paper. It forecloses the question forever, including in
the accident dockets.

## The insurance that did not inspect

Insurance is supposed to make all of this unnecessary. Understanding
why it did not is one of the most important pieces of analysis in
this book, because it explains why market discipline failed in a
market that appeared, on paper, to have it.

In the strong systems, underwriters act as private regulators, and
they are often feared more than the public ones. Their surveyors
walk hangars uninvited. Their exclusions ground aircraft that no
government inspector has seen. Their premiums convert maintenance
records directly into cash flow, which means that the safety
investment of an operator shows up in its quarterly accounts rather
than only in its accident record. The mechanism works because the
insurer has money at stake. It pays the claim.

Now examine the Nigerian market of the crisis era and watch the
mechanism invert.

Local content requirements routed policies through domestic
underwriters, who retained a small fraction of the risk and
reinsured the overwhelming bulk abroad. The retained sliver was too
thin to fund real surveillance, so surveillance never came. And the
reinsurers, being remote, aggregating, and holding the actual risk,
priced the country rather than the carrier. A flat Nigeria premium.

Follow the consequence. An operator with exemplary maintenance paid
very nearly the same rate as the operator running on brokered parts
and stretched intervals. Which means the insurance market was taxing
virtue, imposing a cost on diligence that diligence could not
recover, and subsidizing recklessness with the difference. That is
exactly backward from the design.

Claims performance completed the inversion. The ledger of Gwammaja
in Chapter 7 showed what the fortress of process delivered to the
people it was supposed to compensate, and every slow claim taught
operators that the policy was a certification document rather than a
contingent asset.

Insurance that neither surveils nor promptly pays is not a transfer
of risk. It is a stamp tax on the operating certificate. For two
decades, that is substantially what Nigerian aviation bought.

## Why deregulation is not a policy

America deregulated its airlines in 1978. Entry multiplied. Fare
wars erupted. Marginal operators proliferated. The safety
establishment predicted carnage and the press ran the story about
fly by night airlines for a decade. The carnage did not come.
American accident rates continued their long decline straight
through.

Nigeria deregulated in 1988. Entry multiplied. Marginal operators
proliferated. The carnage came.

Two countries, the same policy, opposite outcomes. The variable was
not the policy. It was the institutional field surrounding it, and
that field is worth listing item by item, because the absence of any
one strand is survivable and the absence of all of them is not.

A regulator whose inspectors cannot be reassigned by telephone. An
independent investigator publishing findings within months. An
insurance market with actuarial teeth and uninvited surveyors. A
system of civil liability that prices negligence in figures large
enough to reach the boardroom. Leasing companies and financiers
whose covenants enforce maintenance programmes as conditions of the
loan. And a used aircraft market deep and well documented enough
that skimped maintenance destroys resale value, which makes promise
keeping enforce itself through asset prices.

That last item deserves emphasis, because it is the most elegant
mechanism in the entire system and it operates without any
government at all. In a market with trustworthy records, the
maintenance history of an aircraft is capitalized into its price.
Neglect the fleet and you destroy your own balance sheet. The
discipline is automatic, continuous, and requires no inspector. It
requires only that records be trustworthy, which is why the archives
of Chapter 14 are not a clerical matter but the foundation on which
market discipline stands or does not.

So state the general principle, because it applies to every
liberalization in every sector in every developing economy.
Deregulation is not a policy. It is a policy plus a field. Removing
a constraint transfers risk to whichever institution is left holding
it, and if no institution is left holding it, the risk goes to
whoever cannot refuse it.

In Nigerian aviation that was the passenger. And at Kano in 2002 it
was people who were not even passengers.

## The other biography

Balance requires the opposite story, because Nigeria now contains it
as well.

A new generation Boeing 737 arrives in Lagos in the 2020s by a route
that touches nothing in the first biography. It is leased new from
the order book of an Irish leasing company, against a business plan
that the credit committee of the lessor has stress tested. Its lease
covenants specify the maintenance programme, the records standard,
the return conditions, and, in the decisive innovation, maintenance
reserves. Those are monthly payments into escrowed accounts held by
the lessor, which can be drawn only to fund the checks themselves.
It is promise keeping, prepaid.

Its insurers price the audit trail of the operator rather than the
country. And its arrival requires of the state exactly the inputs
that Chapter 16 will list. Treaty grade repossession rights, so that
the credit committee will approve the jurisdiction at all. A
registry whose records the market trusts. A regulator whose
surveillance the covenants can cite.

The aircraft is safer than its ancestor of 1968, and not mainly
because engineering advanced, though it advanced enormously. It is
safer because every party in its economic chain is structurally
invested in the promise being kept, continuously, with money already
set aside.

Nigerian carriers have walked that route. The anchor carrier of the
market was the global launch customer for a new Western aircraft
type in 2019, which was a first for the country and would have been
unimaginable in 1995. The narrowness of the route is the point.
Every rung on it is an institution.

## The new risk inside the success

There is a risk growing inside that success, and honesty requires
this chapter to price it before it closes.

The consolidation the reform forced, through capital floors,
recertification, and the purge, raised the expected survival of the
median operator above the maintenance horizon of the median
airframe. That was the intended and beneficial effect. It also
produced a market in which a handful of carriers constitute the
domestic schedule.

Which means that grounding one of them is no longer an
administrative act. It is a national event. Routes go dark. States
are stranded. Business lobbies mobilize within a day. Thousands of
jobs are deployed as argument.

The carrier knows this and so do its bankers, and the knowledge is
itself an asset on the balance sheet. It is an implicit regulatory
forbearance that lowers the effective cost to the firm of running
close to the line, through exactly the mechanism that banking calls
too big to fail. The 2010s and the 2020s supplied the test cases, in
carriers under the care of the state asset manager whose systemic
weight visibly complicated every enforcement conversation, and in
suspension episodes where the arithmetic of the national schedule
audibly shared the table with airworthiness findings.

The remedies of banking translate almost line for line. Resolution
planning is the aviation equivalent of a living will, meaning a
protocol agreed in advance for keeping the schedule alive through
the grounding of a carrier, so that the safety decision is never
hostage to the continuity decision. Surveillance scaled to systemic
weight means the largest carriers earn the most inspection, precisely
because their failure is the least affordable. And structural
encouragement of market depth ensures that no single operating
certificate is ever the difference between a country flying and not
flying.

None of that apparatus exists in Nigerian aviation law at the time
of writing. It is the next generation of risk in the reform, growing
quietly inside its greatest success, which is exactly how every
previous era in this book incubated its own.

## The false choice

Safety, this chapter has argued, is purchased upstream. In foreign
exchange queues, in treaty compliance, in lease covenants, in
insurance surveillance, and in bankruptcy horizons, long before it
is delivered, or not delivered, in a hangar at two in the morning.

The corollary dissolves the laziest dichotomy in the development
conversation, which is the supposed trade off between safety and
affordability. Run the Nigerian numbers honestly. The era of cheap
corner cutting delivered an aviation sector that was at the same
time the most dangerous in the world and among the most expensive
per completed journey.

Add up what the passenger of 1995 actually paid. The ticket. Plus
the chaos surcharge of a system with no schedule integrity. Plus the
insurance and country risk loadings buried in every fare. Plus the
risk premium itself, which in that decade was not a metaphor but a
measurable probability of death, borne in full by the traveller and
compensated at approximately nothing.

The younger fleets and audited carriers of the reformed era
coincided with the greatest expansion of Nigerian air travel in the
history of the country.

Kept promises compound. Broken ones compound too, at rates no
treasury ever publishes, in a currency Part Two counted.

What a society owes the people its systems fail is the subject of
the next chapter, in its starkest form. The minutes after the
borrowing comes due, when the only remaining duty of the state is to
arrive.
