# Chapter 14. The Culture of Silence

She keeps it in a plastic envelope inside a larger envelope, and she
has carried it to more offices than she can now list in order.

A boarding pass stub. A laminated photograph. A death certificate
whose cause of death line reads, in the handwriting of a clerk, air
crash. And correspondence, three decades of it, with an airline that
no longer exists, a ministry that has been renamed twice, and an
investigation bureau that has been renamed once.

All of it asking one question. Why.

The woman with the envelope is a composite, assembled from the
documented histories of the bereaved of Ejigbo, the lagoon, Lisa,
and Kano. But the envelope is not a literary device. Nigerian
journalists who cover aviation know that envelope, and several of
them have photographed it.

Her file is the Nigerian aviation archive in miniature. Grief fully
documented. Causation forever pending. What she was owed, under a
treaty her country signed before she was born, was a report.

## The honesty machine

Begin with how the system is supposed to work, because its design is
one of the quiet masterpieces of the modern world and almost nobody
outside the industry knows that it exists.

Annex 13 to the Chicago Convention obliges every state to
investigate the accidents that happen on its territory, and it
defines the purpose of that investigation in terms the treaty takes
care to make exclusive. The purpose is prevention. And explicitly,
in words that appear in the text, it is not to apportion blame or
liability.

From that single sentence unfolds the entire apparatus this book has
invoked chapter by chapter.

There are independent investigation bodies, walled off from the
regulator, which may itself be implicated, and from prosecutors,
whose presence silences witnesses.

There is protected safety information, meaning cockpit voice
recordings and the statements of crews, shielded from routine legal
use so that the system can afford to be told the truth.

There are public findings and recommendations, addressed to the
world rather than to a filing cabinet.

And layered above the accident machinery are the voluntary reporting
regimes, through which pilots, controllers, and engineers confess
hazards and near misses by the hundred thousand, unpunished, so that
the system can see its failures before they reach the wreckage
stage. The American voluntary system alone receives on the order of
a hundred thousand reports a year, which is a volume of self
incrimination that no punitive system could ever extract.

Scholars of the field call the underlying bargain a just culture.
The frontline professional trades candour for protection from blame,
and honesty becomes the sensory organ of the system.

Every element of that design is counterintuitive to the punitive
instinct. And every element is empirically vindicated. The
industries and countries that punish error into hiding fly blind.
The ones that pay for truth with immunity see everything, and their
accident curves fall for decades.

Which yields the proposition at the centre of this chapter. Aviation
safety is not mainly a technology. It is a way of knowing, with a
budget. It is a set of institutions for making failure speakable,
fast, in public, and under protection.

And it follows that the state of the accident reports of a country
is not a bureaucratic detail. It is an X ray of the entire safety
system.

## The Nigerian ledger

Compile the record and it sorts into three strata.

The first is the unwritten and the buried. The colonial habit of
treating investigation as internal correspondence passed seamlessly
to the successor state. The Kano disaster of 1973 produced no public
Nigerian account. The loss of the flagship of the national carrier
in 1969 produced none. And the military line, from Ejigbo in 1992
through the twin crashes of 2021, has never published at all. For
roughly the first four decades of independence, the default
disposition of a Nigerian accident investigation was the file rather
than the press.

The second stratum is the late. The era of the investigation bureau
brought real investigations, and a tempo that defeated their
purpose. The report on ADC 086 took years. The inconclusive findings
on Bellview took most of a decade. The final report on Dana 992 took
five years, for an accident whose essential mechanics the
investigators understood within months.

Recall what a report is for. Grounding fleets. Rewriting procedures.
Informing the identical aircraft that is flying tonight. Then the
arithmetic of delay becomes a safety deficit in its own right,
because a report that takes five years protects nobody for five
years.

The third stratum is the reformed and partial present. The current
bureau shows the curve bending. Reports appear, faster, in public,
on a website that works.

What has bent less is everything around the reports. Recommendations
are issued and never publicly tracked to closure. There is no
national voluntary reporting scheme with credible protections at
scale. And the legal environment has never struck the bargain of
just culture by statute, which leaves every frontline confession
theoretically discoverable and therefore, in practice, unspoken.

## What silence costs

Because transparency tends to float free as a virtue word, pin the
cost of its absence to cases already in evidence.

The operational cost is measured in repetitions. Sosoliso in
December 2005 and ADC 053 in October 2006 were, at the level of
decision architecture, the same accident. A crew continuing into
convective weather that forecasts and eyes had both flagged, under
schedule and prestige pressure. Ten months separated them.

In a system with a live reporting culture and rapid interim
publication, the lesson of December, with its findings on dispatch
pressure and its arithmetic of the go around, would have been
circulating in every crew room, every regulator bulletin, and every
simulator syllabus in the country by the following rainy season. The
interval was filled instead with mourning and process, and the
lesson arrived ten months later in a funeral procession to Sokoto.

The commercial cost is priced into everything. The flat country
insurance premium of Chapter 12 is in substantial part a premium for
opacity, because underwriters charge for what they cannot read.
Lease terms carry it. Bilateral negotiations carry it, because audit
and investigative performance weigh directly there. And passenger
demand itself carries it, because after every unexplained event the
demand swung toward road transport, whose fatality rate per
kilometre exceeds that of aviation by orders of magnitude.

That last effect deserves its own sentence, because it inverts the
intuition entirely. Opacity, by displacing travellers onto the
highways, kills statistically even in years when the fleet flies
clean.

And the civic cost compounds longest. Where official causation is
absent or late, explanation is supplied by the market, in theories
of sabotage, in spiritual attribution, and in conspiracy among
elites. None of this is Nigerian credulity. It is rational inference
under a famine of information. Where the state explains nothing, the
explanation market clears at whatever price rumour sets.

Every unexplained disaster deposits another stratum of the reflexive
distrust that Nigerian institutions now meet everywhere, including
on the occasions when they are telling the truth.

The woman with the file does not believe the account of the
government about anything. Why would she. On the one subject she
knows better than any official alive, it never gave her one.

## The reflex of blame

Why is silence so stable?

The economical answer, that secrecy protects the guilty, is true and
insufficient, because it does not explain why silence persists after
the guilty have retired, died, or been replaced by people with no
stake in the concealment.

The deeper mechanism is that the alternative to the honesty machine
is not neutrality. It is an older and far more intuitive system,
which is blame.

In the blame system an accident is a crime scene. The organizing
question is not what failed but who pays. Information becomes
ammunition, so every actor, whether pilot, controller, airline, or
ministry, rationally armours up and says nothing. And the real
function of the inquiry becomes the allocation of punishment and the
protection of hierarchy.

Most of human history has run on the blame system. Most institutions
revert to it under stress. It is the default setting of the human
response to catastrophe, and it feels like justice.

A state built on patronage runs on it structurally, because in a
patronage system responsibility is the currency, and the assignment
of currency is far too valuable to delegate to engineers.

Read that way, the silences of Nigeria were not lapses of
administration. They were the blame system defending itself. Every
unpublished report was a favour owed, a faction shielded, a
hierarchy preserved.

The tension is universal, and it is worth noting that even strong
systems fight it continuously. The investigators of France have
collided for decades with its examining magistrates over exactly
this line, with the safety inquiry seeking candour and the criminal
inquiry seeking culpability, and both holding a claim on the same
wreckage. The difference is that the French collision happens in
public, between strong institutions, and the safety findings emerge
regardless. Where the blame system meets no institutional
counterparty, it simply wins, and the file stays closed.

The American public docket, with hearings on camera, raw evidence
posted online, and findings barred by statute from use as proof of
liability, is best understood as the engineered defeat of the blame
reflex. It rests on a wager, which is that a democracy can be
trusted to watch its own failures being understood. The wager has
paid for eight decades, and it is the cheapest reassurance
technology ever invented, because a state that explains publicly
does not have to ask to be believed.

## The arithmetic of the whistle blower

Between the protected report of the frontline and the published
record of the public stands a third figure this chapter has not yet
given his due. The insider who sees the systemic rot, meaning not an
error but a practice and not a lapse but a policy, and who has to
decide what speaking will cost.

Run his arithmetic as the Nigerian system of the crisis decades set
it.

Speak inside the organization and the hazard report goes into the
void, at best ignored and at worst traced back. The memoranda the
controllers filed about the radar before 1996 are the documented
case, and their authors watched them accomplish nothing for a
decade.

Speak to the regulator and you are addressing an agency that might
be the patron of the operator, in a system where the paper trail
leads back to its author.

Speak to the press and you are effective, because the journalism of
that era was the functioning accountability channel, and personally
ruined, in an industry with three employers and one social network.

Speak to nobody and it is free.

The arithmetic solved itself. And the information starvation of the
system was the sum of ten thousand such rational solutions, each one
individually reasonable, arriving collectively at blindness.

The mature systems rebuilt that equation deliberately. Statutory
protection for whistle blowers, with real remedies. Regulator
hotlines whose confidentiality survives a subpoena. And, in the
deepest fix, a voluntary reporting culture that makes the dramatic
disclosure largely unnecessary, because the undramatic report gets
answered.

The whistle blower framework of Nigeria, adopted with fanfare for
financial crime, has never been seriously extended to safety
critical industry. The aviation professional of the 2020s still runs
a milder version of the old equation. Every silence this book has
priced was purchased at that exchange rate, and the exchange rate is
one statute away from repeal.

## The statute on one page

The just culture law this chapter keeps invoking can be sketched in
its essentials, because the models are public and the drafting is a
month of work for a competent chamber.

The first section defines protected safety information as voluntary
hazard reports, flight data monitoring records, cockpit voice
recordings, and statements given to safety investigators.

The second erects the shield. Protected information is inadmissible
in criminal, civil, disciplinary, and administrative proceedings
against the person who provided it. And it carries the carve outs
that make the bargain honest, and that every serious version of this
statute contains. Wilful misconduct, gross negligence strictly
defined, sabotage, and intoxication remain fully prosecutable on
evidence gathered independently.

The third obliges the institutions. Every operator above a size
threshold must run a confidential reporting system meeting defined
standards, and the regulator must publish annual analyses with
identities removed, so that the yield of the system is visible.

The fourth governs the interface with justice, through a standing
protocol between the safety investigator and the prosecution
service, sequencing access to wreckage and records so that neither
inquiry contaminates the other.

The fifth protects the protectors, by making retaliation against a
reporter an offence in itself.

Nothing on that page prevents Nigeria from prosecuting the genuinely
criminal. Everything on it prevents the state from taxing candour at
rates that return the system to silence.

What the page requires is a formal admission. That the state would
rather know than punish. That is the entire philosophy of modern
aviation safety compressed into one clause, and perhaps the reason
it has waited so long in Abuja, because it is the clause by which a
state declares what kind of state it intends to be.

## The problem of the archive

Beneath the unpublished report lies a deeper stratum, and a book
built substantially on documents owes the reader an account of what
the Nigerian aviation archive actually is.

It is, first, dispersed to the point of fiction. The registries of
the directorate era passed through ministerial reorganizations,
office moves, and agency splits that treated records as furniture.
Veterans describe archives lost to floods, to termites, to the
simple entropy of unfunded storage, and, at sensitive moments, to
convenience. The liquidation of Nigeria Airways scattered the
technical and corporate memory of the airline among receivers,
warehouses, and skips.

The colonial strata survive better in London than in Lagos. Chapter
1 made the observation, and it bears repeating here as a finding
rather than an irony. It is easier today to reconstruct the aviation
administration of 1935 than that of 1985.

There is no national aviation archive. There is no systematic oral
history programme capturing the generation, now in its seventies and
eighties, that holds the only surviving record of entire decades.

And the consequence runs well beyond the inconvenience of
historians. The capacity of an institution to learn is bounded by
its capacity to remember. The memory infrastructure of registries,
archives, databases, and librarians is the substrate on which the
learning system runs.

The regulator that recertified the industry in 2006 had first to
reconstruct, aircraft by aircraft, records that should have been a
query away. Every reform cycle in this history spent its first year
rebuilding a factual picture its predecessor had already built and
lost. Amnesia is expensive on a schedule. The state has paid for its
aviation memory several times and has owned it never.

There is a second consequence, which Chapter 12 identified and which
deserves restating here because it connects the archive directly to
the market. Trustworthy records are what allow the maintenance
history of an aircraft to be capitalized into its resale price,
which is the mechanism by which markets discipline maintenance
without any inspector at all. Destroy the records and you destroy
that mechanism.

The archive is not clerical. It is load bearing.

## The other publishers

Where the state declined to publish, others published, and the
culture of silence is incompletely described without its
adversaries.

The Nigerian press has been, across the entire span of this book,
the de facto national accident archive.

It runs from the reporters working the canal bank at Ejigbo against
a military censor, through the aviation desks of the 2000s whose
cumulative coverage built the public record the investigation bureau
was not building, to the digital era outlets whose freedom of
information suits and leaked report stories forced documents into
daylight after the Act of 2011 gave the tradition a legal
instrument.

The professional associations published in their fashion, issuing
communiques and strike threats that said in public what filed
reports had said in vain. And the diaspora published, from beyond
the reach of domestic consequences.

That counter archive achieved real things, and the political rupture
of 2005 ran on it. But its existence also defines the exact shape of
the pathology. A society can know its disasters thoroughly, and
Nigerians never lacked information about their crashes in the
newspaper sense, while the institutional circuit stays broken.

The circuit runs from finding to recommendation to verified change.

Journalism generates salience. It does not generate closure. It can
force the question and it cannot sign off the corrective action. It
can report that the fire tenders were empty and it cannot make the
pumps be tested next March.

The press is the smoke alarm of the system. The Nigerian one has
been loud, brave, and, measured against what smoke alarms can do,
effective. The building needed sprinklers.

## Reflection

Of all the patterns in Part Three, this one costs the least to break
and resists the longest, because it is not a budget line. It is a
reflex, and reflexes are the deepest layer of governance.

Yet the record offers its usual Nigerian consolation, which is that
the reflex has been broken before, in this very country, in this
very industry. The settlement of 2006 that separated the
investigator from the regulator, and the statute of 2022 that
deepened it, were exactly such breaks. The blame system conceding
territory to the honesty machine, under the compulsion of funerals,
one law at a time.

What remains is the last mile. The shield of just culture that makes
candour safe. The register of recommendations that makes findings
binding in fact rather than in rhetoric. The publication tempo that
lets the truth arrive while it can still protect somebody.

None of it requires a naira the system does not already have. It
requires only what the woman with the envelope has been requesting
for thirty years. That the state address its citizens the way an
adult addresses adults, with an account of what happened.

## The anatomy, complete

Part Three set out to convert seven disasters into four patterns,
and the conversion is now done.

A regulator whose strength turned out to be architectural rather
than personal, and therefore both buildable and reversible.

An economics that sorts risk toward whoever is least able to price
it, and that can be sorted again, upstream, in capital markets and
treaty compliance and insurance surveillance.

A response deficit that is shallow, cheap, and separable from every
deeper reform, which makes it the highest yielding purchase
available to the Nigerian state.

And a silence that costs nothing to end but pride.

Four patterns. None of them fate.

What a country might actually do with that knowledge, and what
several other countries have already done with it, is Part Four.
