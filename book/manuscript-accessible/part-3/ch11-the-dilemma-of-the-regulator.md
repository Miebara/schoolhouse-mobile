# PART THREE. THE PATTERNS

# Chapter 11. The Dilemma of the Regulator

Start with a Friday, and let the year float. It could be 1988. It
could be 2004. The year hardly matters, and that is the point of the
story.

An inspector of the civil aviation authority walks the ramp at Lagos
and finds an aircraft he cannot lawfully release. The details vary
in the telling. A defect carried past the window the rules allow. A
certification that lapsed last month. A crew that has run out of
duty hours. He writes the finding and he grounds the aircraft.

The owner of the airline makes two telephone calls.

By Monday one of three things has happened. The finding has been
overruled from somewhere above the head of the inspector. Or the
inspector has been sent on a course in a distant city and his ramp
assignment has gone to somebody else. Or, and this was the most
common outcome of all because systems teach faster than they punish,
nothing has happened, because the inspector knew the first two
outcomes by heart and wrote the finding narrowly enough to be
waived in the first place.

The aircraft flies on Monday evening. Nothing about that weekend
appears in any record that an international auditor will ever read.
Multiply that Friday by twenty years and you have Part Two of this
book.

The question this chapter asks is not why individual Nigerians
failed to stop those weekends. Many of them tried. Some paid for
trying, and their memoranda sit in the files to this day. The
question is why the institution was built so that trying had to be
heroic. A regulatory system that depends on brave inspectors is
already a broken system. The whole art of institutional design is to
make the safe finding the cheap one.

## Capture is everywhere. Architecture is not.

Begin with what is universal, because the idea that Nigeria suffers
from some private national disease is the main obstacle to
understanding this subject.

Regulatory capture is the process by which an agency comes to serve
the industry it is supposed to supervise. It is not an African
pathology. It is the default tendency of all regulation everywhere,
and the economist George Stigler set out its mechanics more than
fifty years ago. The mechanism is structural rather than moral.

The regulated industry is concentrated, informed, and permanently
interested. It employs people whose entire job is to influence the
regulator. It understands the technical detail better than the
regulator does, because it operates the equipment every day. And it
is present at every meeting, every rule making, and every hearing,
forever.

The public is scattered, technically outmatched, and interested only
after disasters. No single passenger has any reason to spend a week
of his life studying airworthiness directives. The rational
passenger does not read them, and never will.

That asymmetry never sleeps. It works in Tokyo and Toulouse in
exactly the way it works in Lagos.

If that seems too generous to Nigeria, consider the most
consequential capture scandal of the modern era, which happened in
Washington. The crashes of the Boeing 737 MAX in 2018 and 2019
killed 346 people in Indonesia and Ethiopia. The investigations that
followed exposed an American regulator that had delegated core
certification judgements to Boeing itself, under a long refined
system in which company engineers acted as the certifiers of the
government, while a commercial deadline pushed schedule pressure
down through the organization and internal warnings were filed and
buried. Change the nouns in that paragraph and the soft tires of
Chapter 4 inflate themselves.

Capture happens everywhere. What varies, and it is the only thing
that varies, is what happens next.

After the MAX there were congressional hearings with the power of
subpoena within months. Internal messages were extracted and
printed. Independent technical review boards were convened. A
criminal settlement ran into the billions of dollars. Legislation
rewrote the delegation system. The captured system audited its own
capture in public and repriced it.

The Nigerian version of that aftermath, for two decades, was a
filing cabinet.

Every country has these problems. Not every country has its problems
subpoenaed.

## Three regimes and one experiment

The history of Nigerian oversight is best read as a natural
experiment in the pricing of capture, run in three regimes on the
same population of airlines, the same weather, and largely the same
people.

The first regime was the directorate, and it ran from 1960 to 1999.
Oversight lived as a department inside the transport ministry. It
had no separate legal personality and no budget of its own. Its head
was a civil servant who could be moved at any time. Its inspectors
were paid on scales that an airline first officer could triple. In
that architecture, capture required no corruption, no conspiracy,
and not even a telephone call, because the regulator simply sat
inside the political structure whose interests conflicted with its
function. The waiver culture was not a distortion of that design. It
was the design, expressing itself. The audit trail of the first
regime is Chapters 4 through 8 of this book.

The second regime was the authority on paper, and it ran from 1999
to 2006. A Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority was created as a
formally distinct body. It was funded by annual allocation, led at
pleasure, and legally toothless against the ministry above it. It
was autonomy as letterhead. The record of that era, from Kano in
2002 to the two catastrophes of 2005, demonstrates the central
finding of the comparative literature on regulatory design, which
policy makers keep rediscovering the expensive way. Formal
independence without independence of money and tenure is capture
with better stationery.

The third regime is the architecture of 2006. Statutory levies.
Protected tenure at the top. A published rulebook. Imported
witnesses. Same country. Same industry. Same salary gaps. The same
generals with the same telephones. And a measurably different output
within two years.

The power of the experiment lies entirely in what it held constant.
No cultural transformation occurred between 2005 and 2008. No
generation turned over. The officials were substantially the same
individuals. Nigeria did not become more honest, more competent, or
more modern in eighteen months.

The architecture changed. And the Friday at the opening of this
chapter began to end differently, because the Monday overrule now
had to survive a paper trail that could be read in Montreal, a
director general who could not be quietly reassigned, and a revenue
stream that no minister could pinch.

## The four load bearing parts

Strip the reform of 2006 down to its mechanism and four parts carry
the structure. Each one can be exported to any weak regulator
anywhere in the world. Each one can also be reversed, which is why
Chapter 10 ended where it did.

The first is funding by formula. An agency financed by a statutory
levy on tickets and cargo can be disciplined only by changing the
law in public. Capture has to come out of hiding in order to
operate, and hiding is its whole comparative advantage.

The second is tenure at the top. An office that can be vacated at
pleasure will be filled by somebody whose planning horizon is that
pleasure. Tenure turns a posting into a position, and therefore
makes the job worth the reputational investment of a professional
who has other options.

The third is transparency working as a ratchet, and this one is more
subtle than the usual language about accountability suggests.
Publication does not mainly inform the public, most of whom will
never open a register of operating certificates. It creates third
party evidence that makes quiet reversal expensive. A waiver granted
against a published rule is a documented act. A waiver granted
against an internal circular is a conversation.

The fourth is imported witnesses. The deliberate coupling to outside
audit, through the programme of the International Civil Aviation
Organization, the assessment of the American regulator, and the
operational audit of the industry itself, gave the domestic
regulator something it had never had before. A constituency for its
own integrity that outranked its domestic predators.

That fourth part deserves a moment, because it inverts the colonial
theme of this book in a way reformers elsewhere should study.
Chapter 1 described oversight located abroad as the founding defect
of Nigerian aviation. The reform of 2006 chose foreign scrutiny,
sovereignly, as scaffolding. The difference between being audited by
an empire and hiring an auditor is the difference between
subordination and strategy. Where internal accountability has not
been built, external accountability can hold the structure up while
it grows. But only if somebody at home has both the authority and
the motive to invite it in.

## The problem of the ministry

One actor has escaped this chapter so far, and the history of the
regulator cannot be read without it. The federal ministry of
aviation is the political principal above the whole alphabet of
agencies, and it is the channel through which every pathology in
this book has at some point flowed.

The situation of the ministry invites its behaviour, and the
concentration of its portfolio is striking. Depending on the
statutory era, it controls, formally or informally, appointments to
the boards of the regulator, the airports authority, the airspace
agency, the meteorological agency, and the training college. It
sponsors the legislation of the sector and defends its budget. It
negotiates the bilateral air services agreements that allocate
international routes, which are the single richest discretionary
prize in the sector, transacted state to state, largely outside
public view, with enormous commercial consequences for identifiable
firms. And it owns the ceremonial surface, meaning the terminal
commissionings, the national carrier announcements, and the ribbon
cuttings from which political credit is harvested.

It is, in short, a machine for converting aviation into politics,
and it sits directly on top of the machine this chapter wants for
converting aviation into safety.

Now the tenure data. Nigerian aviation ministers since 1999 have
averaged barely two years in office. Each one arrives with a
roadmap, a slate of board nominees, and a flagship project. Each one
departs before the second budget cycle of the roadmap. The agencies
below have learned to metabolize that turnover the way Nigeria
Airways metabolized its chief executives, which is by treating every
minister as weather.

The reform proposals that follow from this diagnosis have converged
for decades on a quiet structural heresy. Shrink the ministry. Fold
aviation into a general transport portfolio and leave the agencies
as autonomous authorities, which is the pattern across much of the
developed world. Strip appointment and commercial powers back to a
policy mandate. Nominate professionally qualified boards through
public process, with staggered terms that no single administration
can sweep away.

No Nigerian government has yet found a reason to legislate away its
own patronage surface, and that sentence is the realistic prognosis
of the proposal.

But naming the apex predator accurately still matters. The deepest
dilemma of the regulator is not that it faces a powerful industry.
Every regulator on earth faces that. It is that its own hierarchy is
the most reliable advocate the industry has.

## The market for inspectors

Beneath the architecture lies a labour market, and no honest account
of regulatory capacity survives without it.

Consider what an airworthiness surveyor actually is. A licensed
aircraft maintenance engineer with a decade of seniority in a
hangar, retrained in regulation and audit, fluent in the
documentation systems of two or three manufacturers.

Now consider who else wants that person. The airlines want him to
run their quality departments. The leasing companies want him to
inspect aircraft on delivery and on return. The Gulf carriers want
him. The international agencies want him. The maintenance
organizations of Europe and the Middle East want him. All of them
pay in dollars and several of them add housing and school fees. The
flight operations inspectorate has the same problem in a sharper
form, because it needs current or recently current airline captains,
and the price of those is set in Dubai and Doha.

Every capable authority in the world manages that squeeze with some
combination of three tools.

The first is a pay parity mechanism that pegs technical salaries to
the industry rather than to the civil service scale. The levy
funding of 2006 made a version of this possible in Nigeria, and its
partial erosion is among the quietest dangers of the plateau.

The second is a culture of secondment that borrows industry
professionals for defined tours behind firewalls against conflict of
interest.

The third is prestige, meaning the intangible thing that lets the
great regulators keep people the market prices higher, because the
work confers a standing that money cannot buy.

That third tool is the interesting one, because it is manufactured
by exactly the things this book keeps recommending for other
reasons. Published work. Visible independence. International
respect. The name of the institution meaning something in a
technical conversation. A regulator whose findings are overruled by
telephone cannot pay anybody in prestige. A regulator whose Category
1 rating the whole industry cites can.

The recruitment from the diaspora in the Demuren years succeeded on
that currency as much as on salary. Engineers took real pay cuts to
join an institution that had started to mean something.

Which yields a conclusion that no budget office has ever written
down. Institutional dignity is a line in the compensation package.
Governments that spend it down discover the deficit in their vacancy
lists five years later, when the last inspector who remembers the
standard retires.

## What it costs to say no

Enforcement is not a decision. It is a budget. Follow a single
refusal through the accounts of a regulator and watch the costs
accumulate.

The finding costs the time of the inspector. Documentation that will
survive review costs more of it, in photographs, records, statements,
and a file assembled to be attacked. If the operator contests, the
authority pays lawyers through months or years of litigation against
firms whose counsel bill more in a week than an inspector earns in a
year, and in many jurisdictions, including Nigeria for much of this
period, the aircraft may keep flying under interim orders while the
case runs. If the operator escalates politically, the authority
spends its two scarcest currencies, which are the attention of its
leadership and political capital that could have bought something
else. And if the operator simply defies the order, because operating
while suspended is a documented Nigerian genre, the authority
discovers that its ultimate instruments run through police, courts,
and prosecutors that it does not control and must recruit case by
case.

So every refusal is an expenditure, and the enforcement posture of
an agency is exactly as strong as its enforcement budget.

This is why the comparative literature finds that effective
regulators are distinguished less by the quality of their rulebooks
than by three unglamorous features. They hold a litigation war
chest. They hold summary powers, meaning fines and groundings that
take effect before appeal rather than after it. And they hold
institutionalized working relationships with prosecutors.

The reform of 2006 funded the Nigerian version of this partially.
The levy stream paid for lawyers and the summary powers in the Act
survived their first tests in court. The plateau erodes it quietly.
Every raid on the war chest to cover overheads, every settlement
that rewards defiance, and every appeal that suspends a grounding
shifts the calculated price of non compliance downward. Operators
price regulatory risk as accurately as they price fuel, because they
have to.

Which means the question of whether the authority is strong is
answerable at any moment by a number that no audit publishes. What
does defying it actually cost, in naira, this year.

## What a rule is

There is a philosophical point underneath the waiver culture that
deserves to be argued rather than merely deplored, because it
explains why the reform had to take the specific shape it took.

A safety rule is not mainly an instruction. It is a commitment
device, and its value lies almost entirely in the fact that it is
unconditional. It binds at the exact moment when binding is most
expensive and least convenient, and that is the only moment at which
a safety rule does any work at all. A rule that binds when
compliance is easy has achieved nothing.

Which means that a negotiable rule is not a weaker rule. It is a
different kind of object. It is an opening bid.

And once a rule becomes an opening bid, its distributional
consequences follow immediately, because it now binds in inverse
proportion to power. It binds fully on the operator with no
telephone numbers. It binds partially on the connected one. It does
not bind at all on the general. This is the mechanism by which a
safety system silently turns into a class system, and it works
without anybody intending it and without a single corrupt payment,
purely through the different cost of obtaining an exception.

Now add the position of the passenger, which is the part that makes
the arrangement intolerable. The passenger in seat 14A cannot
observe which kind of rule was in force on the morning his aircraft
was released. He cannot audit the maintenance records. He cannot
know whether the finding was written narrowly. He is buying what
economists call a credence good, meaning a product whose quality he
cannot evaluate before he consumes it, after he consumes it, or in
most cases ever.

Credence goods are the classic case in which markets fail without
institutions. You cannot shop your way to safety. You can only be
governed into it.

And that is why the reform of 2006, stripped of everything else, was
at bottom the repricing of waivers. Not new rules, because the rules
had always existed. A new price for breaking them.

## Reflection

The men and women of the opening Friday deserve the last word in
this chapter. Every system anatomized in this book was, on its worst
days, staffed by some inspector who wrote the finding anyway,
knowing exactly what Monday would bring. Their memoranda are in the
archives and some of them are still working.

The reform of 2006 did not create Nigerian regulatory virtue. It
stopped taxing that virtue at confiscatory rates. That is all
institutional design has ever done anywhere. It arranges matters so
that ordinary conscientiousness, rather than heroism, is enough. And
it is enough, because the difference it makes can be counted in the
crashes that stopped.

The question posed by the plateau is whether a country that built
such architecture once, under compulsion, can learn to maintain it
without compulsion coming back. The next three chapters examine the
economic, operational, and cultural forces that will supply the
compulsion if the answer turns out to be no.
