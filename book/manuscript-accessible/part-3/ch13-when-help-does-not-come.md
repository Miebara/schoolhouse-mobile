# Chapter 13. When Help Does Not Come

Line up five photographs, because together they form a single image
taken five times.

Ejigbo in 1992. Residents wading into a canal with rope while the
military organizes a cordon.

The lagoon in 1996. Fishermen poling through a debris field the
state has not yet located.

Lisa in 2005. A Boeing missing for twelve hours inside the commuter
belt of Lagos, found by farmers walking toward smoke.

Port Harcourt in 2005. Fire tenders arriving at a burning and
survivable wreck without water.

Iju Ishaga in 2012. Ten thousand volunteers, and no crane.

Five different decades of aircraft, weather, and causation. One
constant, so reliable that it amounts to a national signature. In
the window between impact and institutional arrival, the Nigerian
state is represented at its own disasters by whoever happens to live
nearby.

The first disaster belongs to physics. The second belongs to the
state.

## Why this chapter carries so much weight

Emergency response is the purest test of state capacity that exists,
and the reason is worth stating precisely. It cannot be faked,
deferred, or performed selectively.

Every other function of a state can be simulated for an audience.
Statistics can be rebased. Buildings can be commissioned.
Legislation can be passed and never implemented. Attention can be
allocated to whatever is being measured this quarter.

Emergency response is the entire apparatus of a state, meaning its
communications, its logistics, its command, its medicine, and its
honesty, summoned without notice, against a clock, in front of
witnesses. The golden hour does not care what the roadmap said.

## The physics of survival

The survivability literature, built over decades from accident
forensics, establishes a fact that reorganizes how Part Two should
be read. A large share of transport aircraft accidents are
survivable, or partly survivable, at the moment of impact. Studies
of accident cohorts in the mature systems routinely find survival
rates above ninety percent across all accidents and well above half
in the serious ones.

The popular image of an air crash as instant annihilation with
nothing to be done describes some events in this book accurately.
The lagoon. Lisa. It does not describe the class.

Runway overruns. Undershoots like Sosoliso. Fires after impact like
Kano and Iju Ishaga. In those the impact distributes survival
unevenly through the cabin, and the toll is then negotiated. Between
fire and extinguishing agent. Between trauma and the clock. Between
the trapped and whoever arrives with cutting equipment. The
negotiation is over in roughly the time it takes to read this
chapter.

The international standards are calibrated to that negotiation.
Airport firefighting rules require response to any point of the
runway within about three minutes, delivering rated quantities of
water and foam, because an intact cabin next to a kerosene fire
resists for a survivable interval measured in low single digit
minutes.

Which produces the arithmetic Chapter 8 stated and this chapter
generalizes. The difference between a two minute response and a ten
minute response is not eight minutes of delay. It is everyone the
fire reaches second.

## The multiplication problem

An emergency system is a race run in stages. Detection. Dispatch.
Access. Extrication. Stabilization. Transport. Definitive care.

Its performance is not the sum of those stages. It is the product of
them, and that distinction is the most important operational idea in
this chapter.

Consider what it means. If each of seven stages performs at eighty
percent, which is a grade most managers would accept without
complaint, the end to end performance of the system is eighty
percent multiplied by itself seven times. That comes to twenty one
percent.

A failure at any single stage does not add to the delay. It
multiplies through everything downstream, because the later stages
cannot begin until the earlier ones finish.

Which is why the Nigerian record reads as it does. Every disaster in
Part Two failed at several stages at once. Twelve hours to find. No
water at the fire. No crane under the slab. No burn beds at the end.
Multiplication of failures is not addition. It is collapse.

## The stage nobody examines

The last link in the chain is the least discussed and, in Nigeria,
the most binding.

Severe burns are the signature injury of aviation, and they are
among the most resource hungry conditions in all of medicine. They
require dedicated units, sterile environments, skin banking, and
specialist teams working around the clock for weeks or months per
patient.

The burn capacity of Nigeria through the crisis decades was a
handful of units concentrated in two or three cities and chronically
short of everything, amounting to a few dozen dedicated beds for a
population that passed a hundred and forty million. A mass burn
event would have overwhelmed the national capability even if every
ambulance in the country had flown.

The medical odysseys of the survivors of Sosoliso, involving years
of reconstructive care sourced abroad and funded by family means and
public appeal, were not exceptional bad luck. They were the system
stating its capacity honestly. The stage of definitive care for the
injuries its airports were certified to produce did not exist inside
the country.

And the toll has never been counted. Burn and crush patients die in
the second week as reliably as in the first hour, through infection,
kidney failure, and the complications a mature trauma system exists
to prevent. No Nigerian study has ever enumerated those deaths.

Disaster mortality in Nigeria is counted at the scene or not at all,
which flatters every emergency response the statistics touch and
means the true cost of the second disaster in this book is
understated in every number it contains.

## The archipelago

Nigeria does not lack emergency institutions. It lacks an emergency
system. The distinction organizes everything here, and an inventory
makes it visible. Take a mass casualty aviation event and list who
responds.

The airport fire service, confident of its jurisdiction to the fence
line and legally uncertain beyond it, which matters enormously since
most of the crashes in this book came down outside the fence.

The state fire services, chronically the most starved agencies in
Nigerian federalism.

The national emergency management agency, created in 1999 with a
mandate whose breadth, covering floods, epidemics, displacement, and
crashes, has always exceeded a budget sized for press statements.

Its state counterparts, existing on paper in most states and in fact
in few.

The military, which holds the actual heavy capability of engineering
plant, airlift, and divers, but which is constitutionally and
culturally a guest at civilian disasters, arriving through liaison
channels that have to be improvised every time.

The police, who control scenes.

The Red Cross and its volunteer kin, who frequently outperform
everyone.

And the hospitals, federal and state and private, each admitting by
its own rules.

Every island is staffed by people who mostly try. No bridge between
the islands is load tested before the day it has to carry a
disaster. The finding at the top of every after action report in
this book, had anybody written after action reports, would have been
the same phrase. Interface failure. The catastrophe falls into the
water between agencies.

One island has grown notably since 2005 and earns its paragraph. The
emergency agency of Lagos State, built by a government wealthy and
embarrassed enough to invest, was fielding equipment and doctrine
visibly ahead of the federal layer by the time of the Dana response,
and has handled the grim portfolio of the state in building
collapses and tanker fires with accumulating competence since.

It matters analytically because it demonstrates the sub national
route. Response capacity, unlike regulatory sovereignty, can be
built at state level, where the incentives of a governor, meaning
visibility, credit, and a defined constituency who vote, can be made
to align with it.

## The offshore exception

One sector of Nigerian aviation has run, for decades, a response and
safety regime close to world standard. Its existence inside the same
country and under the same state is the most clarifying exhibit in
this chapter, and, once understood, the most damning.

The oil and gas fields of the Niger Delta are served by one of the
denser helicopter economies in the world, flying crew changes,
logistics, and medical evacuation under contract to the
international majors. The flying is objectively hazardous. Offshore
platforms. Salt corrosion. Night evacuations over water.

Yet the practices of that sector would be recognizable in Aberdeen
or in the Gulf of Mexico. Aircraft carry flotation gear and beacons
that deploy automatically. Passengers, who are the roughnecks of the
delta, are put through helicopter underwater escape training as a
condition of boarding. Flight data monitoring runs on every airframe,
years before the scheduled airlines adopted it. And a standing
search and rescue capability is guaranteed by contract, with
dedicated aircraft, trained crews, and response times measured
against the survival window of a man in tropical water.

None of it was provided by the Nigerian state. It was specified,
audited, and paid for by the customers, meaning the oil majors,
whose own liability and reputational machinery, tightened after each
industry disaster anywhere in the world, made safety performance a
condition of the contract and priced it accordingly.

Now extract the three theorems, because they are all important.

The geography was never the constraint. The same delta skies that
hosted the undefended passengers of Sosoliso hosted, at the same
time, world standard rescue.

The mechanism is the presence of a party who is punished by the
death. The roughneck flew safe because an institution existed that
was structurally penalized when he died. The passenger on the
domestic schedule had no such institution standing behind him.

And the capability was purchasable at Nigerian price levels, on
Nigerian soil, by organizations that decided they wanted it.

That third theorem removes every excuse. The offshore sector is a
privatized preview of the public system the final chapters of this
book specify. The scandal is not that it exists. The scandal is what
its existence certifies, which is that the Nigerian state has
watched, for forty years, a working demonstration of exactly what
the survival of its citizens would cost, and has so far declined to
place the order.

## Counting what rescue is worth

Public investment lives or dies by cost benefit arithmetic, and the
neglect of emergency response has always hidden inside a quiet
assumption that the arithmetic is unfavourable. Rescue capacity, the
assumption runs, is a luxury of rich countries.

The assumption collapses on contact.

Standard policy analysis prices life saving measures against each
other, using cost per life year saved as a common denominator. On
such a table, aviation disaster response looks at first glance like
a poor buy, because the events are rare and the costs are
concentrated. The first glance misprices three things.

It misprices the shared nature of the capacity. The firefighting
trucks, the trauma protocols, the mass casualty coordination, and
the family assistance machinery are not aviation assets. They are
all hazards assets, and the same investment answers the tanker
explosion, the market fire, the collapsed building, and the flood.
The Nigerian calendar supplies those at a rate that makes
utilization anything but rare. Aviation is merely the domain where
the standard happens to be written down.

It misprices the trust externality. Every failed response compounds
the civic distrust that taxes every other function of the state, and
Chapter 10 showed that distrust arriving physically at Iju Ishaga in
the form of a crowd that would not clear. Visible competence pays
into the same account in reverse. When the crew of a US Airways
aircraft ditched in the Hudson River in 2009 and all 155 people
aboard were recovered alive, ferries were alongside within four
minutes, because a city had drilled the scenario. What that response
was worth to American institutional confidence exceeded, by an
enormous margin, any budget line it consumed.

And it misprices the alternative. The casualties of the second
disaster. The uncounted dead of the second week. The decades of
litigation and grief work. The international reputation repriced,
and the lease rates and insurance premiums that follow the
repricing. All of these are costs. They are simply booked nowhere.

Run honestly, the package this chapter recommends prices out among
the cheapest life years a Nigerian budget can buy. Cheaper than most
health measures, and cheaper by orders of magnitude than the
terminal buildings that outrank it in every appropriation cycle.

The arithmetic was never unfavourable. It was never done. And the
reason it was never done is the one this book keeps returning to.
The constituency that would have demanded it, being the future
victims of the crash nobody answers, is by definition not yet
assembled. They do not know who they are.

## The volunteer nation

Every response narrative in this book has the same first responder.
The neighbour.

A serious national doctrine would stop treating that fact as an
embarrassment to be cordoned off and start treating it as the
largest unregistered asset the system owns.

The research literature converged long ago on what practitioners
call the myth of the first responder. In mass casualty events
everywhere, from Mexico City to Kobe and through the great urban
catastrophes of the record, the majority of live rescues are
performed not by professionals but by bystanders and neighbours in
the first hour, before any siren arrives.

This is not a failure of professional systems. It is a property of
geometry and time that cannot be removed. The people nearest the
event arrive first because they are nearest. Which means that the
highest leverage move available to the professional system is not to
replace the crowd but to equip it and interface with it.

The instruments exist and they are cheap.

Community emergency response training teaches light rescue, fire
suppression, and triage to ordinary residents in a weekend course,
on models that many countries run at national scale.

Interface protocols let an incident commander convert a crowd from
obstruction into organized labour, through cordon assistance, litter
bearing, supply chains, and traffic control.

And registration of standing community assets turns the fishing
fleets of the coastal chapters of this book, the neighbourhood
associations, and the traders unions and religious brigades that
already hold rosters, radios, and internal discipline into auxiliary
units with training days and call out lists.

Nigeria is, by civic structure, almost ideally suited to that
doctrine. Its neighbourhoods are organized to a density most rich
countries lost generations ago, and its crowds have proven, from
Ejigbo to Iju Ishaga, both willing and brave. What they have never
been offered is the weekend course, the interface protocol, or the
respect of being planned for.

A state that trained one percent of Lagos in light rescue would have
fielded, at Dana 992, ten thousand assets instead of ten thousand
obstacles, at the cost of roughly one annual conference line in one
ministry. Of all the cheap purchases in this chapter, that is the
cheapest, because the workforce has already volunteered. It has been
volunteering, unpaid and unthanked, since September 1992.

## The doctrine on one page

Since this chapter keeps demanding a national response doctrine, it
should sketch one, to demonstrate how little mystery the assignment
holds. Any competent drafting team could flesh out the following
skeleton in a month.

On command. One incident commander per event, designated by a rule
agreed in advance, meaning the airport authority inside the fence
and the metropolitan emergency authority outside it, with every
other agency subordinated for the duration by statute rather than by
negotiation. Dual command is no command. Every failed response in
this book had five commanders.

On communication. A national mutual aid radio protocol and a single
emergency coordination number for responders, with contact trees
tested monthly by actual calls rather than by annual memoranda.

On capability. Firefighting substance audited without warning,
covering agent stocks, pump function, and access routes rather than
the certificate on the wall. Every international airport paired by
written and exercised agreement with its municipal fire, ambulance,
and hospital network. And regional caches of the heavy kit, meaning
cranes, cutting gear, and lighting towers, mapped and contracted in
advance, so that the crane at Dana is never again sourced by
telephone on a Sunday afternoon.

On casualty flow. A regulating function that assigns patients to
hospitals by capability and load rather than by proximity and
rumour, and a single casualty register opened within the hour and
staffed to answer families.

On families. Notification standards. A family information centre at
every event. Management of remains under forensic protocol. And a
named liaison for every family, which is the machinery the United
States legislated after its own disasters of 1996, at almost no
cost, and which changed the civic meaning of American aviation
accidents by extending the competence of the state to grief itself.

On learning. The after action report made mandatory, published, and
answered.

One page. Nothing novel on it. Every line already paid for,
somewhere in this book, by somebody.

## The case against despair

There is a version of the argument in this book that counsels
hopelessness. If crashes are symptoms of institutional depth, and
institutional depth takes generations, then the dying continues
until history has been fixed.

This chapter is the rebuttal, and it rests on a distinction that
matters enormously to anybody trying to improve a weak state.

Some reforms are deep. They require constitutional arrangements,
protections of tenure, funding formulas, and the political
coalitions that will defend all three. The regulator of Chapter 11
is deep. The capital markets of Chapter 12 are deep. Reforms of that
kind take decades and can be reversed.

The second disaster is shallow. It is cheap, separable, and
technically undemanding. Filling a fire tender does not require an
autonomous regulator. Writing an incident command doctrine does not
require constitutional reform. Running a drill does not require a
change of political culture.

Which means a country can learn to rescue competently long before it
learns to regulate competently, and every life saved in the interval
is banked whatever happens to the deeper reforms. That is not a
small consolation. It is a strategy.

If Nigerian public life took a single sentence from this book into
its next budget cycle, it should be this one. Fill the trucks, write
the doctrine, run the drill.

The dead of Port Harcourt were failed twice. And the second failure
was the one that any state, in that year, with that budget, could
simply have declined.

## Reflection

What a state does after its disasters extends past the scene. The
trucks answer for the first hours. The report answers for the years.

And the report, in Nigeria, has its own long history of not
arriving. That silence is the most self inflicted of all the
patterns in this book and the cheapest to end, and it is the subject
of the next chapter.
