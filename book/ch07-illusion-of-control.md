## 7. What Prayer Is Actually For

### *The Illusion of Control*

Dobelli's chapter on the illusion of control opens with a man in a red hat
directing traffic — standing at a busy junction waving his arms with total
conviction, entirely unnoticed by the drivers, absolutely certain that the flow
is his doing. The chapter's core is the tendency to believe we influence
outcomes that are in fact independent of us. He cites the classic findings:
people throw dice harder when they want high numbers and gently when they want
low ones; they will pay more for a lottery ticket they chose themselves than for
one handed to them; put a button on a pedestrian crossing that does nothing and
people will press it and report that it worked. Most crossing buttons in
Manhattan have been disconnected for years. Everyone presses them. Everyone
feels the effect.

Prayer is the red hat.

Start with the cleanest available data, because this is one of the rare
supernatural claims that has actually been tested. Intercessory prayer — praying
for the recovery of someone who does not know they are being prayed for — is a
straightforwardly testable proposition, and it has been tested repeatedly. The
largest and best-designed trial, the STEP study led by Herbert Benson and
published in the *American Heart Journal* in 2006, followed 1,802 cardiac bypass
patients across six hospitals, randomised into groups that were prayed for or
not, with a third group told they were definitely being prayed for. The prayer
made no difference to complication rates. The one statistically notable finding
went the wrong way: patients who *knew* they were being prayed for had slightly
more complications, which researchers speculated might reflect anxiety — if the
church is praying this hard, how bad is it?

Francis Galton got there in 1872 with a simpler design, mentioned in Chapter 2:
the royal family, prayed for by name in every church in the empire every Sunday,
enjoyed no longevity advantage over comparable aristocrats. A century and a half
of measurement, and the effect size remains indistinguishable from zero.

The believer's reply is immediate and worth taking seriously, because it is the
most revealing sentence in the whole debate: *God is not a vending machine and
will not submit to a controlled trial.* Fine. But notice what has just happened.
The claim that prayer changes outcomes was doing real work — it is why people
pray at hospital bedsides, why prayer chains are organised, why the congregation
was told the surgery is Tuesday. The moment it is measured and fails, it is
reclassified as the kind of thing that cannot be measured. That manoeuvre is the
subject of the next chapter, and it is the central move in religious reasoning.
Keep count of how many times you see it.

So if prayer does not change outcomes, what does it do? It does something real,
and this is why it is not going away. It relieves the intolerable condition of
helplessness.

The anthropology on this is old and remarkably consistent. Bronisław Malinowski
observed in the Trobriand Islands that fishermen working the sheltered inner
lagoon, where technique reliably produces fish, used almost no ritual — while the
same men fishing the open ocean, where skill runs out and the sea decides,
performed elaborate magic. Same men, same beliefs, different exposure to
uncontrollable risk. The ritual tracked the helplessness, not the task.
Laboratory work has since converged on the same result: reduce people's sense of
control and their tendency to perceive patterns, agency and efficacy in random
stimuli goes up measurably.

This predicts the geography of prayer in your own life with uncomfortable
accuracy. People pray hardest about the things they can do least about:
metastatic cancer, a child at war, a plane in turbulence, exam results already
posted, the mind of a person who has stopped loving them. Nobody prays for their
tyre to be changed. The distribution of prayer across life's problems does not
map onto what matters most; it maps onto what is least under our control. That is
the signature of a coping mechanism, precisely and exactly, and it is what
Dobelli's chapter would predict before looking.

I want to be careful here, because "prayer is only a coping mechanism" is
sometimes said dismissively and it should not be. Coping mechanisms are
important. Structured attention to what you fear, in a calm posture, in
language, sometimes with others, does measurable good — it looks a great deal
like the mechanisms that make meditation and expressive writing effective. If
prayer gets you through the night before the biopsy, it is doing something
worth having. My objection is not that it fails to work. It is that it works
through you rather than through the world, and it is sold to you as the second
thing.

The cost of that mislabelling is not zero, and it is worth stating plainly. The
illusion of control substitutes for control. Every year there are children who
die of treatable conditions because their parents prayed instead of driving to a
hospital, and the parents were not monsters; they were people who had been taught
that the red hat directs the traffic. At a smaller scale the same substitution
runs through ordinary life: the marriage prayed over instead of discussed, the
depression prayed over instead of treated, the injustice prayed over instead of
organised against. Dobelli's warning about the crossing button is funny because
the stakes are nil. Change the stakes and it stops being funny.

**In conclusion:** ask what your prayer would look like if it had no external
effect at all. If the honest answer is *exactly the same* — same feeling of
having done something, same relief, same absence of any check on whether the
outcome differed — then you have no way to tell the two hypotheses apart, and you
should stop claiming the stronger one. Then ask the harder question. What is
the thing you have been praying about that you could actually act on? Take off
the hat and go and do that.
