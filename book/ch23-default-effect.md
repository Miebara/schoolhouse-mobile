## 23. The Sunday You Keep Not Skipping

### *The Default Effect, Status Quo Bias, Procrastination, and Omission Bias*

Dobelli's chapter on the default effect reports a finding with enormous
consequences and no glamour: **whatever the pre-set option is, most people will
take it.** Organ donation rates differ by fifty or sixty percentage points between
neighbouring countries with similar cultures, and the difference is not attitude —
it is whether the form requires you to opt in or opt out. Pension enrolment,
insurance excesses, software settings, privacy permissions: the default wins,
overwhelmingly, and people who took the default will explain their choice as a
considered preference.

Status quo bias is the general form: we prefer things to remain as they are, and
we require a much stronger argument to change than to continue. Dobelli's related
chapter on procrastination covers why the change never happens even once decided —
the cost of acting is now and the benefit is later, and hyperbolic discounting
makes later worth almost nothing.

This is the least dramatic chapter in the book and, for most readers, the true
explanation of their attendance.

Consider how you came to be in that building. You did not evaluate the world's
religions and select one. You were born to parents who held a particular
tradition, in a country where it was common, and you were taken there before you
could speak. The default was set for you at birth, by geography and family, and
essentially everyone who holds your faith received it the same way. This is the
observation from Chapter 8 that I consider unanswerable, and here it is in its
mechanical form: religious belief correlates with birthplace to a degree that
nothing tracking evidence ever could. A person born in Lahore is overwhelmingly
likely to be Muslim, in Bangkok Buddhist, in Alabama Baptist, in Salt Lake City
Mormon. If any one of these traditions had a genuine evidential advantage, that
advantage would show up as conversion flowing in one direction. It does not. What
flows is inheritance.

And then the default is maintained by nothing more than inertia. Ask an average
attender why they went last Sunday and the honest answer is rarely theological.
They went because they go. Because their spouse was going. Because they said they
would help with the coffee. Because their mother asks. Because the children's
programme is at the same time and it is genuinely useful. Because not going
requires a decision, an explanation, and a conversation, and going requires
nothing at all.

Dobelli's omission bias explains why the decision never gets made. We judge
harmful action more severely than equally harmful inaction, and so we default to
inaction whenever a choice is uncomfortable — his example is the parent who declines
a vaccine with a one-in-a-hundred-thousand risk, exposing the child to a much
larger risk from the disease, because the first harm would have been *caused* and
the second merely *allowed*. Applied here: actively leaving feels like doing
something, with consequences you would be responsible for. Continuing to attend
feels like doing nothing, and therefore feels safe. But you are not doing nothing.
You are spending two hours, every week, for the rest of your life, and paying for
it, and raising your children inside it. Continuing is a choice with a price; it
just doesn't present a bill.

Then the specifically religious version of procrastination, which is worth naming
because I suspect it describes a large fraction of anyone who has read this far.
*I'll sort this out eventually.* You have had serious doubts for three years, or
ten. You have not resolved them, and you have not left, and you have not
investigated. You have simply continued, with the question filed under things to
address later, while attending weekly in the meantime. Dobelli's remedy for
procrastination is the only one that works: a deadline, in writing, and a specific
next action rather than a vague intention. Chapter 26 gives you both.

There is one more, and Dobelli calls it the *inability to close doors*. We keep
options open at a cost, refusing to foreclose anything — his example is the
paralysed shopper, but the deeper version is a life in which nothing is ever
decided. Enormous numbers of people occupy a permanent middle position on
religion: not believing in any operative sense, not disbelieving out loud, still
attending at Christmas, still describing themselves as "sort of" something, still
raising children in a tradition they privately think is false. This is not
open-mindedness. Open-mindedness is a willingness to revise conclusions on
evidence, which requires having reached one. Holding a door open for forty years,
at the cost of two hours a week and a low continuous hum of unresolved
dishonesty, is not intellectual virtue. It is the default effect, wearing the
costume of humility.

**In conclusion:** find out whether you are a believer or a person who has never
opted out. There is a cheap test, and it is the practical recommendation of this
entire book: **skip four consecutive Sundays.** Not as a statement, not as a
resignation, not announced to anyone. Just do not go, for one month, and use one of
those mornings to write down what you actually think. If the belief is real, you
will find that out, and nothing has been lost. If what you find is relief, you
have learned something that fifty more years of attendance would never have told
you — because the default never gets tested by anyone who keeps taking it.
