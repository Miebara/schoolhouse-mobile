## 12. Pascal's Wager Is a Sales Technique

### *Loss Aversion, Framing, Ambiguity Aversion, and Fear of Regret*

Dobelli's chapter on loss aversion states the finding that underwrites most of
modern behavioural economics: **losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent
gains please.** Losing a hundred pounds is felt about twice as intensely as
finding a hundred. This asymmetry is not a quirk; it is one of the most robust
results in psychology, and it means that anyone who can *frame* an offer as
avoiding a loss rather than achieving a gain has doubled their persuasive power
without changing a single fact.

Dobelli's framing chapter follows immediately, and his point is that the same
information presented as a loss or a gain produces different decisions in the
same people. Ninety percent survival and ten percent mortality are the same
statement; surgeons who present the first get consent far more often. Marketing
runs on this. So does insurance. So does the most famous argument for religious
belief ever constructed.

Pascal's Wager, stripped to its skeleton: if you believe and God exists, you gain
infinite happiness. If you believe and there is no God, you lose almost nothing.
If you disbelieve and God exists, you lose infinitely. Therefore belief is the
rational bet under uncertainty.

It is a loss-aversion device with an infinity bolted on, and it is worth being
precise about why it fails, because it is the argument most likely to be doing
work on you emotionally even after you have stopped believing intellectually.

**It has the wrong number of options.** The wager is presented as a
two-by-two — believe or not, God or not — when the actual option space contains
every god that has ever been proposed and every one that hasn't. Yahweh as read
by Calvinists, who may damn you regardless. Allah, who regards the Trinity as the
gravest error possible. The god who rewards honest inquiry and despises hedged
belief. Once the grid has ten thousand cells, the infinite payoff appears in many
of them, mutually exclusively, and the expected-value calculation stops producing
an answer — it produces a division by nonsense. Dobelli's *alternative blindness*
chapter names this exactly: we compare an option against one alternative rather
than against the full set, and we choose badly because the set was rigged before
we started.

**Infinity breaks the arithmetic.** Multiply any non-zero probability by an
infinite payoff and you get infinity, which means the wager licenses literally
any claim that comes with an infinite promise attached. If I tell you there is a
one-in-a-trillion chance that donating to me yields eternal bliss, the same
reasoning obliges you to donate. An argument that proves too much has proved
nothing — this is Dobelli's *simple logic* problem, where a valid-looking
procedure produces absurdities and we accept the output because we cannot see the
error in the steps. Philosophers call it Pascal's Mugging, and it is not a
solved problem; it is a demonstration that the machinery is broken.

**The cost side is understated by a factor no one computes.** "You lose almost
nothing" is doing a lot of quiet work. What you actually spend is one day a week
for a lifetime — for a fifty-year adult believer, roughly two and a half years of
waking life — plus ten percent of gross income, plus, in many traditions,
constrained choices about who you marry, what you do with your sexuality, how you
raise your children, and what conclusions you are permitted to reach. That is not
a rounding error. It is one of the largest allocations of time and money you will
ever make, and Pascal gets to describe it as negligible only because the infinite
term on the other side is designed to make every finite term look like zero.

**And you cannot do what it asks.** This is the deepest objection. Belief is not
voluntary. Try, right now, to believe that there is a hippopotamus behind you. You
can say it, you can act as if, you can want to — you cannot believe it, because
belief is a response to evidence rather than a decision. Pascal knew this, which
is why his actual advice was behavioural: go to Mass, take the holy water, attend
to the rituals, and belief will follow. Read that again, because it is a
confession. The recommended path to faith is *to perform the practices until the
conviction arrives*. Chapter 20 explains precisely why this works — effort
justification and cognitive dissonance reduction — and that it works is not
evidence that the belief is true. It is evidence that humans come to believe what
they have paid for. Pascal, three centuries early, identified the mechanism this
book is about and recommended it as a method.

Two smaller devices finish the sales pitch. Dobelli's *ambiguity aversion*: we
strongly prefer known risks to unknown ones, and will pay a premium to convert
uncertainty into certainty even when the certainty is fake. Religion's core
product is the elimination of ambiguity about death — the one ambiguity nobody can
tolerate — and it is sold at exactly the price ambiguity aversion predicts. And
*fear of regret*, which produces the most common sentence in the whole apologetic
repertoire: "but what if I'm wrong?" That sentence is not an argument. It is the
anticipated pain of a loss, doing the work that evidence has failed to do. Note
that it is perfectly symmetrical and nobody ever runs it in the other direction:
what if you spent your one finite life on Sundays, guilt and tithes for a being
who was not there?

**In conclusion:** whenever an argument arrives framed as a catastrophic loss you
must insure against, translate it back into gains and see whether it still
persuades. Then count the options the frame omitted, and price the premium
honestly. Pascal's Wager, translated, says: *the punishment is so large that you
should stop asking whether the claim is true.* That is not a reason to believe.
It is a threat, and a threat is what a case looks like when it has no evidence
left.
