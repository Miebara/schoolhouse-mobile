## 14. "I Just Know He's Real"

### *The Forer Effect, the Introspection Illusion, and the Affect Heuristic*

Every argument in this book can be conceded by a believer without losing
anything, because the arguments were never the foundation. Press hard enough and
you reach bedrock, and bedrock is always the same sentence: *I have experienced
him.* Something happened to me. I felt a presence. I know it the way I know my
own mind.

Dobelli has three chapters that between them account for this, and they are the
ones I would most want a believer to read.

**The Forer effect.** In 1948 the psychologist Bertram Forer gave his students a
personality test and then handed each of them what he said was their individual
profile. Every student received the identical text, assembled from a newsstand
astrology column: *you have a great need for other people to like and admire
you… you tend to be critical of yourself… you have a great deal of unused
capacity which you have not turned to your advantage… at times you have serious
doubts as to whether you have made the right decision.* Students rated the
accuracy at 4.3 out of 5. They were not fools; the statements are true of nearly
everyone, which is the point, and the sensation they produce is not "this is
generic" but "this is *me*."

The Forer effect is the operating principle of horoscopes, cold reading, tarot,
mediums, and — I do not say this to be cruel — a great deal of what happens in
church. Take the most common form of personal religious confirmation: a sermon
that speaks directly to your situation. What actually happened is that a person
addressed four hundred people, for thirty minutes, on themes selected for near-
universal applicability — you are carrying something you have not told anyone, you
are tired, you are afraid you are not enough, there is a decision in front of
you, you have been running from something. In a room of four hundred adults,
every one of those is true of a large majority. The experience of being singled
out is manufactured by the same procedure Forer used, and it does not require
anyone to be dishonest. The preacher feels it too.

The same applies to the prophetic word given over you at a conference, and to
the Bible verse that answered your exact question, and to the words of knowledge
in a Pentecostal meeting ("someone here has pain in their lower back" — in a room
of two hundred adults, dozens do). This is not a debunking trick I am importing
from outside. It is the mechanism that any professional cold reader will explain
to you for free, because they consider it a technique rather than a gift.

**The introspection illusion.** Dobelli's chapter states the finding: we believe
we have privileged, accurate access to the causes of our own mental states, and we
do not. The research here — Nisbett and Wilson's classic work, and the whole
choice-blindness literature since — shows that people confidently report reasons
for their own preferences that demonstrably were not the causes. Subjects who
choose a stocking from an identical set explain their choice by texture and
sheen. Subjects whose choice is secretly swapped defend the option they *didn't*
pick with fluent reasons. We do not observe our own mental causation. We
confabulate it, immediately and convincingly, and the confabulation feels exactly
like memory.

This is why "I know from experience" is not the trump card it feels like. You did
have the experience — I fully believe you. What you did not have is access to its
cause. You felt an overwhelming presence, a certainty, a warmth, a sense of being
held. Those are real events in a nervous system. The label "God" was applied
afterwards, by the interpretive machinery, and the machinery is not a witness. It
is a press office.

And we know the labelling is culturally supplied, because the labels sort by
tradition with almost no leakage. Catholics have Marian visions; Protestants do
not. Hindus experience Krishna. Pentecostals speak in tongues and Anglicans, in
identical states of emotional intensity, do not. Sensed-presence experiences occur
reliably in conditions that have nothing to do with religion at all — sleep
paralysis, extreme altitude, solo sailing, exhaustion, bereavement, temporal-lobe
epilepsy, carbon monoxide exposure. The mountaineer's "third man" who walks
beside them at 8,000 metres is the same phenomenon that becomes the Holy Spirit in
a chapel and a dead spouse in a bedroom. The experience is universal. The
identification is local. That is precisely backwards for a genuine encounter and
precisely right for a physiological event being named by whoever is nearby.

(A caution, because Chapter 25 obliges me: Michael Persinger's "God helmet",
often cited here, failed replication in Granqvist's 2005 study, which found that
suggestibility rather than magnetic fields predicted the experience. I am not
leaning on it. The wider sensed-presence literature does not need it.)

**The affect heuristic.** Dobelli's version: we let emotional response stand in
for judgement of truth or risk. Something that feels good gets rated as safe and
likely; something that feels dreadful gets rated as dangerous and improbable. In
religious experience the affect *is* the evidence being offered — the feeling of
peace, of homecoming, of love — and no one is claiming otherwise. But intensity of
feeling is not a truth-detector, and we already know this in every other domain.
The certainty of new love is overwhelming and frequently wrong. The certainty of
3 a.m. despair is overwhelming and wrong. Converts to every religion on earth
report the identical feeling of finally having come home, and they cannot all be
right, and each of them experiences the feeling as self-authenticating.

**In conclusion:** the next time you are moved to certainty, run two checks. Was
the content specific enough that it could have failed — would it have applied to
the stranger three rows behind you? And would you accept the same report as
evidence from someone in a tradition you reject? If the content was universal and
the answer to the second is no, then what you have is a genuine experience with
a borrowed label. The feeling is yours. The explanation was handed to you before
you were old enough to ask for it.
