## 19. The Collection Plate and the Free Meal

### *Reciprocity, Liking Bias, and the Incentive Super-Response Tendency*

Dobelli's chapter on reciprocity carries the practical instruction: **do not accept
free drinks.** The reciprocity instinct is deep, automatic, and largely
insensitive to whether the initial gift was solicited, wanted, or trivial. Robert
Cialdini's field studies are the reference: a waiter who brings a sweet with the
bill raises the tip measurably; a charity that encloses free address labels raises
donation rates sharply; the Hare Krishnas discovered that pressing a flower on an
airport traveller who did not want it, and refusing to take it back, converted
into donations at a rate direct asking never achieved. The traveller usually
discarded the flower in the next bin. They donated anyway. The obligation does not
require that you value the gift.

Dobelli's second relevant chapter is liking bias: we are more easily persuaded by
people who are similar to us, who pay us compliments, and who cooperate with us
toward a shared goal. His summary is that salespeople are not trained to make you
like the product but to make you like *them*, and that it works even when you know
it is happening.

Now trace the actual sequence by which a person is brought into a congregation.
It is not an argument, and nobody sat down and designed it — it is what survived,
which is more interesting.

It begins with unconditional gifts. The free meal. The coffee, given by someone
who remembers your name at the second visit. The youth group with the games and
the pizza. The Alpha course, whose entire format is dinner first, discussion
second. Food and childcare when your baby is born. Help moving house, from six
men with a van who accept nothing. In the poorest parts of the world, the school,
the clinic, the well, the food distribution.

I want to be scrupulously fair here: most of this generosity is entirely sincere.
The people carrying the casseroles are not running a technique. They are being
kind, and they would be kind anyway, and the world is better for them. That is
what makes it effective in a way no cynical version could be. But the effect on
the recipient is the same whether or not anyone intended it. You have received,
repeatedly, at a moment of need, from people who asked for nothing — and the
reciprocity instinct now has a debt on its books. When the debt comes due, the
currency in which repayment is available is attendance, agreement, membership,
and money.

Watch how well this predicts the actual pattern of conversion. Overwhelmingly,
people do not convert after evaluating arguments; they convert after being
befriended, and the sociology of religious recruitment has been saying so since
Lofland and Stark's 1965 study of conversion, which found that affective ties to
group members predicted joining far better than any doctrinal factor. Missionary
strategy has understood this for centuries. Nobody argued anyone into anything.
They fed them.

Then the money, where Dobelli's incentive super-response tendency applies: **people
and institutions respond to incentives far more strongly than to their stated
values, and if you want to understand behaviour, look at what the system is paid
for.** Ask what a church, considered as an institution rather than as a
theological claim, is actually incentivised to produce. Attendance and giving. Not
accuracy — nothing in the structure rewards being right, because there is no
mechanism that could detect it. So over centuries you get selection for whatever
maximises the two things that are measurable, and the doctrines that survive are
the ones that pay.

Which is why the tithe is presented with such remarkable machinery around it.
Consider what has been built. It is framed as an obligation to God rather than a
subscription to an organisation. It is set as a percentage of income, which scales
automatically. Non-payment is a spiritual failing. And in prosperity traditions it
is sold as an *investment* — sow a seed, expect a harvest — an arrangement in which
the payment is offered as the mechanism for its own return, and any subsequent
good fortune is credited to it while any misfortune is attributed to insufficient
faith. Chapter 8 explained why that structure can never lose. The televangelist
with the private jet is the extreme case, but the mechanism is identical in a
village chapel, and in the village chapel the money at least buys a roof and a
food bank.

The full sequence is worth laying out as a single mechanism, because each stage
is documented in Dobelli's catalogue separately and their combination is what
actually holds people:

1. Unconditional gifts create obligation (reciprocity).
2. Warmth and similarity create trust that transfers to the claims (liking bias,
   and the halo effect of Chapter 17).
3. Small commitments are requested first — attend once, come to the group, help
   with the stall — and each one makes the next larger one easier (Cialdini's
   consistency principle, and Dobelli's *"because" justification*).
4. Costly commitments then generate belief retroactively rather than following
   from it (effort justification — Chapter 20).
5. The community becomes your entire social world, so exit becomes unaffordable
   (Chapter 18).

At no point in that sequence does anyone need to establish that God exists. The
question is never reached. That is not a flaw in the process; it is why the
process works.

**In conclusion:** separate the gift from the claim, deliberately and in writing
if necessary. The people who brought food when your father died were good to you,
and you owe them gratitude — a real debt, and you should pay it, in kindness and
in help and in showing up when they need you. What you do not owe them is
agreement about the origin of the universe. Repay generosity with generosity. Do
not repay it with your assent, because assent is not yours to give away; it
belongs to the evidence.
