# PART II — THE CRASHES (1990–2020)

# Chapter 4 — Fire Over Jeddah: Flight 2120

The pilgrims boarded in Jeddah wearing the white of ihram, the seamless
cloth of consecration. Most were farmers and traders from Nigeria's north
— from Sokoto and its hinterland — at the end of the journey of their
lives. The aircraft that would carry them home wore three flags at once:
built in America, owned and crewed in Canada, painted in the green and
white of Nigeria Airways, departing a Saudi runway. It was 11 July 1991,
the morning already furnace-hot, and Flight 2120 was one more rotation in
the great annual machinery of the Hajj airlift — the operation Chapter 2
described as the flying Nigeria's institutions oversaw least, performed
by strangers, under arrangements nobody fully owned.

The aircraft was a Douglas DC-8-61, twenty-three years old, registration
C-GMXQ, operated by the Canadian charter company Nationair under wet
lease — an arrangement, to define it once, in which an airline rents out
not just an aircraft but its crew, maintenance, and insurance, so that
the machine flies under one company's flag while answering, in every
operational particular, to another's. Nigeria Airways, by 1991 unable to
mount the Hajj surge from its own decaying fleet, had contracted the
lift; Nationair, a Montreal operator whose aggressive growth had
outrun its cash, needed the revenue badly. Two institutional
desperations had met in a marketplace, and 261 people now boarded the
result.

What happened next took twenty-nine minutes from pushback to impact, and
the sequence is among the most exactly reconstructed in African aviation
history, because the evidence began accumulating before the aircraft
left the ground.

## The operator, the contract, the economy of the surge

To understand why the tires were soft, begin with why the aircraft was
there.

Nationair was the creation of the deregulated 1980s — a Montreal charter
carrier, founded in 1984, that grew in seven years from nothing to one of
Canada's largest non-scheduled operators. Its business model was the
model of every ambitious charter airline of the era: buy the previous
generation's aircraft cheaply (DC-8s and 757s), fly them hard, chase the
world's seasonal demand spikes — Caribbean winters, European summers, and
the greatest spike of all, the Hajj. Growth of that speed is bought on
margin, in every sense. By 1991 the company's finances were strained, its
labor relations poisonous — its flight attendants were in a bitter
dispute that would later put the phrase "replacement crews" into the
coverage of the disaster — and its operational tempo relentless. None of
this was hidden; it was the ordinary visible profile of a company
running hot. The question the wreckage would pose was simply who, in the
world's aviation architecture, had been responsible for reading that
profile and acting on it. The answer — everyone slightly, no one
sufficiently — has already been given.

The Hajj contract itself deserves a paragraph, because its economics
explain the pressure that traveled down through the organization and
came to rest, finally, on two tires in Jeddah. Hajj lift is boom
economics compressed into six weeks: an operator commits aircraft, crews,
and insurance to a distant base; revenue arrives per rotation flown; and
every day an aircraft sits unserviceable, the fixed costs run on while
the revenue stops — and, beyond the money, thousands of pilgrims with
visas keyed to the pilgrimage calendar back up in terminals on two
continents. The Saudi authorities enforce the calendar mercilessly,
because the alternative is chaos at the holy places. The client — here
Nigeria Airways and, behind it, the Nigerian state's Hajj apparatus —
measures the contractor in departures achieved. Everything in the
arrangement's design rewards the decision to fly and penalizes the
decision to stop. Aviation calls the countervailing force
"airworthiness": the flat, non-negotiable rule that the machine meets
specification or it does not move. Airworthiness is precisely the kind
of rule — boring, expensive, painful at the margin — that survives only
inside institutions built to enforce it against money. In July 1991,
at the Jeddah ramp, no such institution was effectively present. The
maintenance personnel who found the soft tires had a nitrogen rig that
did not work, a schedule that did not pause, and a management, an ocean
from its regulator, for whom every hour of delay was counted in dollars
and diplomatic grief. The rule met the money alone, and lost.

One more figure belongs in this section: the passengers' own government,
in its capacity as charterer. Nigeria's Hajj operation of the era was
itself a study in institutional improvisation — pilgrim welfare boards of
uneven competence, state-level fiefdoms, agent networks, and a federal
apparatus for which the airlift's success was measured, like the
contractor's, in seats moved. The Nigerian state was, in this
transaction, a bulk buyer of the cheapest compliant lift the market
offered. It wrote no airworthiness standards into its chartering beyond
the paper minimum, conducted no independent technical vetting worth the
name, and stationed no oversight presence at the operation it had
bought. This was not exceptional malice; it was Chapter 3's regulator —
starved, subordinated, waiver-ridden — behaving abroad exactly as it
behaved at home. The wet lease did not defeat Nigerian oversight. There
was no Nigerian oversight to defeat; the lease merely gave the vacuum a
foreign address.

## Timeline

The DC-8 taxied out heavy — near its maximum takeoff weight, loaded with
247 passengers and their compressed belongings, fuel for Sokoto, and the
July heat, which thins air and lengthens takeoff rolls. What neither the
passengers nor, in any operationally meaningful sense, the flight crew
had been given the chance to weigh was the state of the aircraft's tires.
Tire pressure checks days earlier had found the number two and number
four main-gear tires significantly below specification. The nitrogen
servicing rig available to the ground crew was unserviceable; procuring
proper servicing would have delayed the rotation. The lease operation's
management, under schedule pressure — every Hajj day lost was revenue
lost and pilgrims stranded — allowed the aircraft to continue flying. By
the morning of 11 July, the underinflated tires had been carrying their
overload through days of hot-weather rotations.

An underinflated aircraft tire does not simply risk a flat. At taxi and
takeoff speeds under load, it flexes beyond its design, and flexing
rubber generates heat with terrible efficiency. During the long, hot,
heavy takeoff roll, the number one tire — overloaded by its
underinflated neighbor — failed; the adjacent tire followed, shedding
rubber, dropping the wheel rims onto the runway in a plume of friction
sparks. The takeoff continued; the crew, feeling vibration but reading
no engine anomaly, flew the aircraft off the runway and — following the
procedure of the age — retracted the landing gear.

Into the wheel wells, packed among hydraulic lines and adjacent to the
cabin floor, came wheels that were on fire.

The DC-8 has no fire detection or extinguishing in its main wheel wells;
the crew's first unambiguous warnings arrived as cascading system
failures — hydraulics dying, pressurization faltering — and then as
smoke in the cabin. The aircraft climbed toward two thousand feet while
the crew declared an emergency and turned back toward Jeddah. Behind
them, the fire was burning through the cabin floor into the passenger
compartment. In the aircraft's final minutes, as it descended toward the
runway it had left less than half an hour before, the floor structure
failed progressively; witnesses on the ground, and the distribution of
the evidence afterward, recorded that bodies and debris fell from the
burning aircraft along the final miles of its approach. Control was lost
2,875 meters short of the runway. There were no survivors: 247 pilgrims
and 14 crew.

It remains, three decades later, the deadliest accident in the history
of Canadian aviation, the deadliest involving a DC-8, and the deadliest
single disaster ever suffered by a Nigerian civil operation. It is
commemorated in almost no public place in any of the three countries
whose flags the aircraft wore.

## The investigation

Jurisdiction followed the Chicago Convention: Saudi Arabia, as the state
of occurrence, led the investigation, with Canadian and American
participation as the states of the operator and manufacturer. Nigeria,
whose citizens constituted nearly all of the dead and whose flag was
painted on the fin, participated at the margins — a fact to hold for the
analysis below.

The Saudi report's findings were unusually direct for the genre. The
aircraft had been dispatched with tires known to be below pressure
limits. Maintenance personnel had identified the deficiency;
serviceable equipment to correct it had not been available; and
management personnel of the lease operation, under commercial pressure,
had permitted continued operation rather than ground the aircraft. The
technical chain — underinflation, overdeflection, thermal failure,
rubber fire carried into the unprotected wheel well, hydraulic and floor
burn-through — was established in forensic detail. The report's
recommendations ranged from tire-pressure monitoring to wheel-well fire
protection, and the accident became a canonical case study, taught to
this day in maintenance human-factors courses worldwide, of how a
"minor" ground-handling economy becomes a hull loss: the textbook
specimen of the organizational accident, in which the fatal decision was
made not in a cockpit but at a desk, days earlier, by someone who never
boarded.

Nationair itself did not long survive its reputation. Grounded rotations,
labor conflict, and unpaid bills accumulated; the company collapsed into
bankruptcy in 1993, its founder's empire gone in a decade from startup
darling to cautionary tale. Canadian aviation absorbed its own lesson
about the oversight of aggressive charter operators — a lesson Canadian
regulators and journalists debated publicly, in hearings and print, in a
way that will contrast instructively with what followed in Nigeria.

## The reaction

In Nigeria, the disaster fell upon the north like a second Kano 1973 —
which is precisely the problem, that the sentence can be written that
way. The federal military government declared mourning; delegations
flew to Jeddah; compensation negotiations began their long, opaque
journey; and the Hajj operation, after a pause, resumed, because the
Hajj always resumes. What did not happen is the list that matters. No
Nigerian public inquiry examined how the national carrier had come to
place a quarter-thousand citizens on an aircraft whose operator's
maintenance economics were visibly strained. No Nigerian institution
published, for Nigerian readers, an account of the Saudi findings and
their implications for the charter system that had produced them. No
regulatory reform of wet-lease oversight followed in Lagos. The state's
posture, as after 1973, was that of a bereaved bystander — as if the
aircraft's Canadian registration had exported the accountability along
with the maintenance.

The families' experience followed the pattern that will recur in every
chapter of this Part: lists of the dead assembled slowly and imperfectly
(the pilgrim manifest system was approximate in the manner Chapter 3
described); compensation mediated through layers of insurers, lessors,
and governments, arriving in amounts and on schedules the bereaved
neither influenced nor understood; and remembrance privatized into
family and mosque, because the state built no memorial and appointed no
anniversary. Grief, like regulation, was outsourced.

## What the industry learned: the technical afterlife

Because the rest of this chapter concerns what institutions failed to
learn, the record should first show what the profession did learn,
globally, from Flight 2120 — a demonstration of the honesty machine
of Chapter 14 working as designed, for everyone except the country
of the victims.

The accident became a foundation stone of maintenance human-factors
education. It supplied the canonical answer to a question every
maintenance organization must decide in advance: what happens in our
company when the correct action is unavailable and the schedule is
waiting? The Jeddah answer — fly it anyway, it's only tires — entered
the textbooks precisely because tires had seemed so far down the
criticality ladder. The lesson generalized: there are no minor
systems on a transport aircraft at gross weight, only systems whose
failure modes have not been imagined vividly enough. Regulators and
manufacturers moved along the specific vectors the report drew:
tire-pressure monitoring systems migrated from luxury to standard on
subsequent designs; wheel-well fire detection, already present on
newer types, became an airworthiness talking point for the older
generation; and dispatch-with-defect governance — the Minimum
Equipment List culture that specifies exactly what may be broken and
for how long — tightened its language around landing gear. The crews
of today's aircraft, including every aircraft in Nigerian service,
inherit those changes without knowing their origin. This is how the
system is supposed to work: the dead of one operation purchasing
protection for all operations, through institutions that convert
findings into requirements.

Note, then, the asymmetry that defines this chapter. The
*international* learning loop closed: Montreal, Washington, and the
manufacturers metabolized Jeddah into worldwide practice within a
few years. The *national* loop — the one that should have
reformed Nigerian charter oversight, Hajj contracting, and manifest
integrity — never engaged. Nigeria received the global safety
dividend passively, as a customer of the world's aircraft, while
declining the specific institutional lessons addressed to it by
name. A state can free-ride on world aviation's honesty machine for
the physics; it cannot free-ride for the governance. The physics
travels in the airframe. The governance has to be built at home,
and Part II is what its absence cost.

## The dead

Before the analysis, the arithmetic of the loss, because numbers of this
size defeat the imagination unless they are unfolded.

Two hundred and forty-seven passengers: subtract the crew from 261 and
that is the pilgrim count, drawn overwhelmingly from Sokoto State and
its neighbors — the same savanna hinterland that had buried the dead of
Kano 1973 eighteen years earlier. The Hajj pilgrim of that place and
era was typically not young and not rich: the pilgrimage came, for most,
at the end of decades of saving, often financed by the sale of land or
cattle, often the single great journey of a life otherwise bounded by a
market radius. Whole village savings cohorts traveled together; the
manifest, had it been exact, would have read like the census of a rural
district's senior generation. Their deaths subtracted not individuals
but strata — the hajji as village elder, arbitrator, creditor, the
person whose title, once earned at Mecca, reorganized his standing at
home. The white of ihram, in which they died, is also, in Muslim burial
practice, the color of the shroud; the north understood the terrible
completeness of that symmetry at once, and the mourning of 1991 carried
an edge of theological consolation — they died as pilgrims, in a state
of grace — that the state, it must be said, found convenient. Grief that
resolves into God's will files no lawsuits and demands no hearings.

Fourteen crew: Canadians, mostly Montreal-based, several of them — this
detail lodged in the Canadian memory of the disaster — flight attendants
working the Hajj contract amid the airline's labor war at home. Their
names are recorded, their families' litigation traceable, their
memorials locatable: the ordinary forensic dignity of a country whose
institutions count its dead. The asymmetry between the two halves of the
manifest — fourteen names with dockets, two hundred forty-seven names
assembled approximately, later, from pilgrim board records — is not a
detail of the aftermath. It is the aftermath, in its purest expression,
and it will recur at Ejigbo, at the lagoon, at Lisa village: the
institutional weight of a death, in this story, depends on which state's
systems were responsible for remembering the life.

## Institutional analysis: the accountability triangle

Wet leasing is not a scandal; it is a tool, and world aviation could not
function without it. The scandal is a tool used without a jurisdiction
to own its risks — and Flight 2120 is the purest specimen in this book
of what might be called the accountability triangle, in which three
states each hold a corner of responsibility and the object at the center
belongs to none.

Consider the geometry. Canada licensed the operator, its crews, and its
maintenance system — but the operation ran nine time zones away, its
commercial pressure applied by a contract Canada had no reason to
scrutinize. Saudi Arabia owned the runway and the airspace — but wielded,
over a foreign-registered transient, only the blunt instruments of ramp
inspection. Nigeria owned the passengers, the brand on the fin, and the
contract — and had persuaded itself that ownership of the contract was
the opposite of responsibility: the whole point of chartering, in the
logic of a collapsing flag carrier, was to buy capacity *and shed
accountability* in a single transaction. Each corner of the triangle
could point, honestly, at the other two. The center — where 261 people
sat — was sovereign to nobody.

International aviation law has spent the decades since narrowing exactly
this gap. Article 83 bis of the Chicago Convention, which entered force
in 1997, created the machinery for transferring oversight duties
formally between states in lease arrangements; ICAO's standards now
require states to satisfy themselves about foreign operators serving
their territory, and the serious jurisdictions run foreign-carrier
assessment programs — the EU's SAFA ramp inspections, the FAA's
scrutiny of operators flying to America — that treat "not our registry"
as the beginning of oversight rather than its end. The reform, as usual
in aviation, is written in the blood of the uncompensated. But the
deeper Nigerian lesson of 2120 is not about leases. It is that a state
too weak to perform a function does not escape the function's risks by
contracting it out; it merely re-imports them, unregulated, marked up,
and denominated in other people's institutional quality. Nigeria in 1991
was buying safety oversight the way it bought aircraft — second-hand,
sight unseen, from whoever would extend credit. Flight 2120 was the
price discovery.

## The counterfactual protocol

It is fair to ask what, concretely, a competent Nigerian state would
have done differently — fair, because the answer demonstrates that
nothing in this chapter required wealth Nigeria lacked. The protocol
that would have interrupted the chain fits on an index card, and every
element of it existed somewhere in the world in 1991.

A charterer with functioning oversight writes technical conditions into
the contract: minimum maintenance standards, audit rights, the right to
inspect and reject specific airframes. It exercises those rights — a
two-person technical team stationed with the operation for six weeks,
with authority to ground. It requires that its regulator, not its
airline's commercial department, approve the operator — the distinction
between a customer's due diligence and a state's, which is the whole
difference between price and safety as the binding constraint. And it
prepares for the worst in advance: a manifest system that knows who
boarded, a family-assistance protocol, a standing arrangement for
participation in any foreign investigation touching its citizens. Cost
of the entire apparatus, per Hajj season, at 1991 prices: less than the
catering bill. India, running a pilgrim airlift an order of magnitude
larger through the same decades, built approximately this machinery —
imperfectly, with its own scandals, but with the state present inside
the operation rather than outside it, and its Hajj safety record,
across vastly greater volumes, reflects it.

The index card was not expensive. It was institutionally impossible —
because each line of it presumes an agency with the authority to impose
costs on politically connected commerce, and Chapter 3 has already
described what stood in that place in 1991: a ministry directorate that
could not ground a general's schedule, let alone a national Hajj. The
counterfactual is worth stating not to sharpen the hindsight but to fix
the book's unit of analysis. The difference between the disaster that
happened and the routine season that could have happened was not money,
technology, or expertise. It was the existence of one institution able
to say no and make it stick — the cheapest and rarest component in the
whole machinery of flight.

## Comparative note: two countries read one report

The most instructive comparison for Flight 2120 is not another crash but
another readership — what Canada and Nigeria each did with the same
document.

In Canada, the disaster prompted press investigation, regulatory
soul-searching about charter oversight and the economics of the
"low-cost long-haul" model, civil litigation, and a public record —
books, documentaries, journalism — in which the dead of Jeddah, though
foreigners, became part of the national aviation conscience. The
operator was destroyed by the exposure. The lesson entered the
bloodstream of the profession: ask a Canadian aircraft maintenance
engineer about Nationair and the answer, a generation later, is a
short course on dispatch pressure and the duty to say no.

In Nigeria, the state of the victims, the report went substantially
unread. It was not translated into policy, testimony, curriculum, or
memorial. The Hajj airlift continued under successor arrangements with
successor charter operators of varying quality; the underlying
architecture — surge lift, leased metal, diffused oversight — persisted
essentially unreformed into the 2000s, when Nigerian Hajj charters would
again feature in safety controversies. One document; two national
digestive systems; and the difference between them is nearly the whole
subject of this book. Aviation safety is often described as a technology
or a budget. It is more fundamentally a reading practice — the
institutional habit of treating every report as addressed to oneself.
Canada, which lost fourteen citizens, read the report as an indictment
of Canada. Nigeria, which lost two hundred and forty-seven, read it as
news about a Canadian airline.

## Reflection

Every July, the anniversary passes with little notice in the country the
pilgrims came from. There is a bitter symmetry in that silence: they
died, in the largest sense, of arrangements designed to be nobody's to
notice — flags borrowed, oversight assumed away, responsibility priced
out of the contract line by line. The white garments of ihram signify,
Muslim scholars teach, the shedding of worldly distinction: all pilgrims
equal before God. The system that carried them had performed a darker
shedding — of jurisdiction, of accountability, of the state duties that
distinguish citizens from cargo.

Flight 2120 stands first among this book's seven crashes because it
establishes the pattern at its most naked, before any complication of
weather or piloting can blur the sightline: a known deficiency, a
commercial pressure, a decision to defer, an institutional vacuum where
the refusal should have come from — and then physics, which negotiates
with no one. Every disaster that follows in this Part will be more
Nigerian in its mechanics: Nigerian airlines, Nigerian runways, Nigerian
regulators. None will be different in kind. The wheel well of C-GMXQ is
this book's argument in miniature: the fire always starts in the part of
the machine nobody can see, that everybody deferred, that somebody,
somewhere, decided could wait — and by the time it is visible from the
cabin, the only questions left are for the investigators. The work of
safety, like the work of institutions, is done or not done in the dark,
before the doors close.

Fourteen months after Jeddah, the pattern would repeat with the
institutional vacuum in a different costume — not a leased airliner this
time but a military transport, not pilgrims but the officer corps of the
Nigerian armed forces, and not a foreign investigation report unread but
a domestic one never written for the public at all.
