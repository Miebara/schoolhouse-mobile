# Epilogue — The Names

At the Loyola Jesuit College campus in Abuja there is a memorial
garden with sixty names. At Ejigbo there is a canal the neighborhood
renamed with its grief. At Lisa village, a clearing; at Iju-Ishaga, a
rebuilt street; at Gwammaja, houses whose newer brick marks the old
scar line. Nigeria's aviation dead are memorialized the way this book
found everything else — privately, locally, improvisationally, by the
communities the state left holding the memory as it once left them
holding the rope at the canal's edge.

Other countries build the other kind of memorial. The point of a
national memorial is not marble; it is the state placing itself on
record — these were citizens, this was our failure, this stone is our
receipt. Nigeria owes that receipt at a dozen sites named in these
pages, and the debt is not sentimental. A state that will not
memorialize its failures is declaring its intention to be surprised
by them again.

But the truer memorial, this book has argued from its first chapter,
was never stone at all. It is the published report, the tracked
recommendation, the drilled airport, the funded inspector, the
protected confession, the rule that holds on Friday against the
Monday phone call. Every name in the appendix that follows is a
person to whom that memorial was owed in advance and delivered, if
ever, too late. The names are the argument. Everything else in this
book has merely been their arrangement.

# Appendix B — The Institutions

**NCAA — Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority.** The economic and safety
regulator: licensing, airworthiness, operations oversight, consumer
protection. Created 1999; given fiscal and legal autonomy by the Civil
Aviation Act 2006; re-chartered 2022. The book's central institution
(Chapters 9, 11).

**NSIB — Nigerian Safety Investigation Bureau** (until 2022 the
**AIB**, Accident Investigation Bureau). Independent accident
investigator, Annex 13 authority; multimodal mandate since 2022.
Separated from the regulator by the 2006 Act (Chapters 9, 14).

**NAMA — Nigerian Airspace Management Agency.** Air navigation
services: ATC, radar (TRACON, 2010), navaids (Chapter 6).

**FAAN — Federal Airports Authority of Nigeria.** Airport operator:
terminals, runways, ARFF fire services (Chapters 8, 13).

**NiMet — Nigerian Meteorological Agency.** Aviation weather
services (Chapters 8, 9).

**NEMA — National Emergency Management Agency.** National disaster
response coordination (Chapter 13).

**NCAT — Nigerian College of Aviation Technology, Zaria.** The
training college, founded 1964 with ICAO/UNDP support (Chapter 2).

**The Ministry.** The Federal Ministry of Aviation in its various
namings — the political principal whose relationship to all of the
above is the subject of Chapter 11.

**International:** **ICAO** (UN standard-setter; USOAP audits),
**FAA** (US regulator; IASA Category 1/2 assessments), **EASA/EU**
(air safety list), **IATA** (trade body; IOSA audit).

# Appendix C — Glossary and Abbreviations

- **AOC** — Air Operator Certificate; an airline's license to operate.
- **ARFF** — Aircraft Rescue and Firefighting; airport fire service.
- **Annex 13** — Chicago Convention annex governing accident
  investigation; mandates independence and prevention-only purpose.
- **Annex 14** — Annex governing aerodromes, including ARFF standards.
- **C-check** — Major scheduled maintenance inspection, weeks-long.
- **Category 1 / 2 (IASA)** — FAA rating of a *country's* oversight
  (not its airlines); Category 1 permits direct US service.
- **CVR / FDR** — Cockpit voice recorder / flight data recorder
  ("black boxes").
- **Density altitude** — Effective altitude for aircraft performance;
  high heat = thin air = degraded performance.
- **EU Air Safety List** — The European ban list of carriers/states.
- **IOSA** — IATA Operational Safety Audit of airlines.
- **Just culture** — The bargain protecting honest error reports from
  punishment to keep safety information flowing.
- **SAATM** — Single African Air Transport Market (AU open-skies).
- **SARPs** — ICAO Standards and Recommended Practices.
- **SMS** — Safety Management System; an operator's formal risk
  machinery.
- **TRACON** — Total Radar Coverage of Nigeria (commissioned 2010).
- **USOAP** — ICAO's Universal Safety Oversight Audit Programme.
- **Wet lease** — Renting aircraft *with* crew/maintenance/insurance
  (vs. **dry lease**, aircraft only).
- **Windshear** — Abrupt wind change with altitude/position; lethal
  near the ground; thunderstorm signature.

# A Note on Sources

This book stands on four strata of evidence, in descending order of
authority, and the reader deserves to know which claims rest where.

**Official investigation records.** Where a final report exists —
Saudi Arabia's on Flight 2120; the AIB's on ADC 086, EAS 4226,
Sosoliso 1145, ADC 053, Dana 992, and Associated 2013; the partial
Bellview record — this book follows its findings, timelines, and
casualty figures, and says so in the chapter. Where none was ever
published (Kano 1973 in any Nigerian form; Ejigbo 1992; the NAF
losses of 2021), the text marks the absence explicitly: it is part
of the argument (Chapter 14). The NSIB's online library, the ICAO
accident digests, and the Aviation Safety Network's database were
the working spine for cross-checking dates, registrations, and
figures; every number must be re-verified against the primary
documents in the pre-publication pass.

**Audit and regulatory records.** ICAO USOAP results, FAA IASA
determinations, EU air safety list decisions, the Civil Aviation
Acts of 2006 and 2022, the NSIB Establishment Act, NCAA regulations
and public registers, and National Assembly committee reports
(including on Nigeria Air).

**Contemporaneous journalism and memoir.** The Nigerian press — the
Daily Times and West Africa for the early era; the Guardian,
PUNCH, ThisDay, Daily Trust, BusinessDay, Premium Times, and the
wire services (Reuters, AP, AFP) and BBC for 1990 onward — supplies
the scenes, reactions, and public record of promises made. The
industry journalism of Wole Shadare and colleagues, the memoir
literature of Nigeria Airways veterans and military officers, and
the Loyola Jesuit community's memorial publications inform Chapters
2–3, 5, and 8 respectively. Journalism is treated as evidence of
what was said and seen, not of technical causation.

**Scholarship and comparative literature.** James Reason's
organizational-accident framework; Sidney Dekker on just culture;
Stigler and the Carpenter–Moss school on regulatory capture; the
histories of the FAA/NTSB, Ethiopian Airlines, Indonesian reform,
and the Cape Town Convention compliance literature; ICAO Annexes 6,
13, and 14 as normative benchmarks; and the airline economics
literature on deregulation and safety. Colonial-era material draws
on the UK National Archives' AIR and CO series and the
historiography of imperial aviation (Pirie and others), and on
Nigerian archival holdings at Ibadan and Kaduna.

Composite figures — the inspector of Chapter 11's thought
experiment, the keeper of the file in Chapter 14 — are identified
as composites where they appear and invent no facts: every element
of their situations is drawn from documented cases. No dialogue has
been invented anywhere in the book. Where sources conflict on a
number, the chapter says so or the conservative figure is used.
All remaining errors are the author's.
