# Epilogue — The Names

At the Loyola Jesuit College campus in Abuja there is a memorial
garden with sixty names. At Ejigbo there is a canal the neighborhood
renamed with its grief. At Lisa village, a clearing; at Iju-Ishaga, a
rebuilt street; at Gwammaja, houses whose newer brick marks the old
scar line. Nigeria's aviation dead are memorialized the way this book
found everything else — privately, locally, improvisationally, by the
communities the state left holding the memory as it once left them
holding the rope at the canal's edge.

Other countries build the other kind of memorial. The point of a
national memorial is not marble; it is the state placing itself on
record — these were citizens, this was our failure, this stone is our
receipt. Nigeria owes that receipt at a dozen sites named in these
pages, and the debt is not sentimental. A state that will not
memorialize its failures is declaring its intention to be surprised
by them again.

But the truer memorial, this book has argued from its first chapter,
was never stone at all. It is the published report, the tracked
recommendation, the drilled airport, the funded inspector, the
protected confession, the rule that holds on Friday against the
Monday phone call. Every name in the appendix that follows is a
person to whom that memorial was owed in advance and delivered, if
ever, too late. The names are the argument. Everything else in this
book has merely been their arrangement.

# Appendix B — The Institutions

**NCAA — Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority.** The economic and safety
regulator: licensing, airworthiness, operations oversight, consumer
protection. Created 1999; given fiscal and legal autonomy by the Civil
Aviation Act 2006; re-chartered 2022. The book's central institution
(Chapters 9, 11).

**NSIB — Nigerian Safety Investigation Bureau** (until 2022 the
**AIB**, Accident Investigation Bureau). Independent accident
investigator, Annex 13 authority; multimodal mandate since 2022.
Separated from the regulator by the 2006 Act (Chapters 9, 14).

**NAMA — Nigerian Airspace Management Agency.** Air navigation
services: ATC, radar (TRACON, 2010), navaids (Chapter 6).

**FAAN — Federal Airports Authority of Nigeria.** Airport operator:
terminals, runways, ARFF fire services (Chapters 8, 13).

**NiMet — Nigerian Meteorological Agency.** Aviation weather
services (Chapters 8, 9).

**NEMA — National Emergency Management Agency.** National disaster
response coordination (Chapter 13).

**NCAT — Nigerian College of Aviation Technology, Zaria.** The
training college, founded 1964 with ICAO/UNDP support (Chapter 2).

**The Ministry.** The Federal Ministry of Aviation in its various
namings — the political principal whose relationship to all of the
above is the subject of Chapter 11.

**International:** **ICAO** (UN standard-setter; USOAP audits),
**FAA** (US regulator; IASA Category 1/2 assessments), **EASA/EU**
(air safety list), **IATA** (trade body; IOSA audit).

# Appendix C — Glossary and Abbreviations

- **AOC** — Air Operator Certificate; an airline's license to operate.
- **ARFF** — Aircraft Rescue and Firefighting; airport fire service.
- **Annex 13** — Chicago Convention annex governing accident
  investigation; mandates independence and prevention-only purpose.
- **Annex 14** — Annex governing aerodromes, including ARFF standards.
- **C-check** — Major scheduled maintenance inspection, weeks-long.
- **Category 1 / 2 (IASA)** — FAA rating of a *country's* oversight
  (not its airlines); Category 1 permits direct US service.
- **CVR / FDR** — Cockpit voice recorder / flight data recorder
  ("black boxes").
- **Density altitude** — Effective altitude for aircraft performance;
  high heat = thin air = degraded performance.
- **EU Air Safety List** — The European ban list of carriers/states.
- **IOSA** — IATA Operational Safety Audit of airlines.
- **Just culture** — The bargain protecting honest error reports from
  punishment to keep safety information flowing.
- **SAATM** — Single African Air Transport Market (AU open-skies).
- **SARPs** — ICAO Standards and Recommended Practices.
- **SMS** — Safety Management System; an operator's formal risk
  machinery.
- **TRACON** — Total Radar Coverage of Nigeria (commissioned 2010).
- **USOAP** — ICAO's Universal Safety Oversight Audit Programme.
- **Wet lease** — Renting aircraft *with* crew/maintenance/insurance
  (vs. **dry lease**, aircraft only).
- **Windshear** — Abrupt wind change with altitude/position; lethal
  near the ground; thunderstorm signature.

# A Note on Sources

This book stands on four strata of evidence, in descending order of
authority, and the reader deserves to know which claims rest where.

**Official investigation records.** Where a final report exists —
Saudi Arabia's on Flight 2120; the AIB's on ADC 086, EAS 4226,
Sosoliso 1145, ADC 053, Dana 992, and Associated 2013; the partial
Bellview record — this book follows its findings, timelines, and
casualty figures, and says so in the chapter. Where none was ever
published (Kano 1973 in any Nigerian form; Ejigbo 1992; the NAF
losses of 2021), the text marks the absence explicitly: it is part
of the argument (Chapter 14). The NSIB's online library, the ICAO
accident digests, and the Aviation Safety Network's database were
the working spine for cross-checking dates, registrations, and
figures; every number must be re-verified against the primary
documents in the pre-publication pass.

**Audit and regulatory records.** ICAO USOAP results, FAA IASA
determinations, EU air safety list decisions, the Civil Aviation
Acts of 2006 and 2022, the NSIB Establishment Act, NCAA regulations
and public registers, and National Assembly committee reports
(including on Nigeria Air).

**Contemporaneous journalism and memoir.** The Nigerian press — the
Daily Times and West Africa for the early era; the Guardian,
PUNCH, ThisDay, Daily Trust, BusinessDay, Premium Times, and the
wire services (Reuters, AP, AFP) and BBC for 1990 onward — supplies
the scenes, reactions, and public record of promises made. The
industry journalism of Wole Shadare and colleagues, the memoir
literature of Nigeria Airways veterans and military officers, and
the Loyola Jesuit community's memorial publications inform Chapters
2–3, 5, and 8 respectively. Journalism is treated as evidence of
what was said and seen, not of technical causation.

**Scholarship and comparative literature.** James Reason's
organizational-accident framework; Sidney Dekker on just culture;
Stigler and the Carpenter–Moss school on regulatory capture; the
histories of the FAA/NTSB, Ethiopian Airlines, Indonesian reform,
and the Cape Town Convention compliance literature; ICAO Annexes 6,
13, and 14 as normative benchmarks; and the airline economics
literature on deregulation and safety. Colonial-era material draws
on the UK National Archives' AIR and CO series and the
historiography of imperial aviation (Pirie and others), and on
Nigerian archival holdings at Ibadan and Kaduna.

Composite figures — the inspector of Chapter 11's thought
experiment, the keeper of the file in Chapter 14 — are identified
as composites where they appear and invent no facts: every element
of their situations is drawn from documented cases. No dialogue has
been invented anywhere in the book. Where sources conflict on a
number, the chapter says so or the conservative figure is used.
All remaining errors are the author's.

# Appendix E — Institutional Milestones, 1919–2024

A chronology of the governance events this book treats as the
true plot, for the reader who wants the argument's spine in
dates.

- **1919** — Paris Convention establishes state sovereignty over
  airspace; colonial Nigeria's skies vest, legally, in Britain.
- **1925** — RAF Cairo–Kano flights; aviation arrives as imperial
  demonstration.
- **1935–36** — Kano established as trans-African civil staging
  post; scheduled services reach Lagos.
- **1940–45** — West African Reinforcement Route; wartime runway
  and radio infrastructure built.
- **1944** — Chicago Convention signed; ICAO system created.
- **1946** — West African Airways Corporation founded (four-
  territory public corporation).
- **1958** — WAAC (Nigeria) Ltd formed; becomes Nigeria Airways.
- **1960–61** — Independence; Nigeria joins ICAO in its own right;
  oversight functions repatriated without matching capacity.
- **1964** — Nigerian Civil Aviation Training Centre, Zaria,
  founded with ICAO/UNDP support.
- **1969** — Nigeria Airways VC10 lost at Lagos; the national
  grammar of unexplained disaster set.
- **1973** — Kano Hajj charter disaster (176 dead); no public
  Nigerian reckoning.
- **1976** — Murtala Muhammed Airport commissioned; FAAN's
  predecessor established; aviation as monument era peaks.
- **1985–86** — Oil collapse; SAP; the dollar famine reaches the
  hangar.
- **1988–95** — Deregulation licensing wave; private carriers
  multiply against a starved directorate.
- **1991** — Flight 2120 burns at Jeddah (261 dead); wet-lease
  accountability vacuum exposed.
- **1992** — NAF C-130 falls at Ejigbo (~158 dead); report never
  published.
- **1995–96** — FAA and international bodies flag Lagos
  deficiencies; ADC 086 lost over the lagoon (144 dead).
- **1999** — Democracy restored; NCAA created on paper; NEMA
  established; ICAO USOAP begins auditing sovereign oversight.
- **2002** — EAS 4226 falls into Kano (~148 dead); aircraft age
  limits enter policy.
- **2003–04** — Nigeria Airways liquidated; Ghana Airways
  parallel collapse; both flags grounded permanently.
- **2005** — Bellview 210 (117) and Sosoliso 1145 (108); the
  Loyola parents organize; national aviation summit convened.
- **2006** — ADC 053 (96 dead, the Sultan among them); Civil
  Aviation Act 2006: NCAA autonomy, statutory funding, AIB
  separated; Demuren era begins; re-certification purge.
- **2007** — Democratic succession survives; purge completes;
  AOC count roughly halved.
- **2010** — FAA IASA Category 1 attained; TRACON radar
  commissioned; the reform's trophy year.
- **2011** — Freedom of Information Act; the secrecy default
  formally renounced.
- **2012** — Dana 992 falls at Iju-Ishaga (163 dead incl.
  ground); the reformed system's first great test; suspensions
  and restorations begin their oscillation.
- **2013** — Associated Airlines charter crash; Demuren removed;
  the plateau era opens.
- **2016–17** — Naira crisis; Arik and Aero into AMCON care;
  fleet-safety floors hold.
- **2018–23** — Nigeria Air saga, from Farnborough livery to
  parliamentary verdict; symbolic politics resurgent.
- **2021** — NAF Beechcraft losses at Abuja and Kaduna (Chief of
  Army Staff killed); military opacity intact at 29 years from
  Ejigbo.
- **2022** — Civil Aviation Act renewed; NSIB created with
  multimodal mandate; publication tempo improves.
- **2024** — The quiet decade continues in scheduled aviation;
  the constituency question — this book's closing question —
  remains open.

# A Bibliographic Essay: Reading Further

For the reader who wishes to go deeper, the literature sorts
usefully by the book's own parts.

**On the colonial and early national era (Part I).** Gordon
Pirie's *Air Empire: British Imperial Civil Aviation, 1919–39*
and its sequel on cultures of imperial flying are the standard
account of the system that brought aviation to Nigeria; they
reward reading alongside the Nigerian nationalist press of the
1940s–50s, digitized in part by the great newspaper archives.
On WAAC and the transition, the corporate and administrative
records in the UK National Archives (AIR and CO series) remain
underexploited by scholars — a dissertation waits there. On
Ethiopian Airlines' founding bargain, the airline's own
commissioned histories should be read critically alongside
independent business-history treatments. On the political
economy of early Nigerian parastatals generally, Peter Lewis's
*Growing Apart: Oil, Politics, and Economic Change in Indonesia
and Nigeria* is this book's silent companion — its
Indonesia–Nigeria pairing anticipates Chapter 15's by two
decades and at monograph depth.

**On the crashes (Part II).** The primary documents, where they
exist, are cited in the Note on Sources; the Aviation Safety
Network's database is the indispensable finding aid. Nigerian
journalism is the era's true archive: the aviation desks of the
Guardian, PUNCH, ThisDay, and later BusinessDay and Premium
Times did, for decades, the documentation the state neglected,
and any future scholar of the period will live in their
morgues. Wole Shadare's collected industry journalism provides
the insider chronicle. On the Hajj airlift's strange economics,
the scholarly literature on pilgrimage logistics (notably work
on the Saudi aviation system's Hajj operations) frames Chapter
4; the Canadian journalism on Nationair's collapse frames its
operator. For the military dimension, the Haddon-Cave Nimrod
Review (2009) is the essential text — nominally about Britain,
actually about every air force — and its Nigerian application
is this book's Chapter 5.

**On the patterns (Part III).** James Reason's *Managing the
Risks of Organizational Accidents* remains the field's
foundation; Sidney Dekker's *Just Culture* and *The Field Guide
to Understanding 'Human Error'* carry the argument into law and
practice. On regulatory capture, Carpenter and Moss's edited
*Preventing Regulatory Capture* supplies the modern framework;
Stigler's 1971 article the classic statement. On the FAA/MAX
episode, the House Transportation Committee's final report and
Peter Robison's *Flying Blind* give the documentary and
narrative accounts respectively. On disaster response, Lee
Clarke's *Mission Improbable* (on fantasy planning documents)
reads as if written about the exercises of Chapter 13; the
NTSB's family-assistance materials document the institution
Chapter 13 urges. On the economics, the aircraft leasing and
Cape Town Convention literature (AWG's compliance indices) and
the AFRAA/IATA annual African aviation reports supply the data
spine.

**On the future (Part IV).** The manufacturers' twenty-year
forecasts (Airbus Global Market Forecast; Boeing Commercial
Market Outlook) and the UN's *World Population Prospects*
underlie Chapter 17's arithmetic; the AU/AFCAC documentation on
SAATM and the single market its politics. On Indonesia's
recovery, the DGCA's published corrective action plans and the
EU air-safety-list decision notices constitute an
under-appreciated reform archive — the rare case where a
country's entire remediation is legible in public documents.
No comparable Nigerian archive yet exists. Assembling one —
the documentary history of the 2006–2010 reform, its memoranda
and audit responses and internal battles, gathered while its
protagonists live — is the most urgent unwritten book in
African aviation, and this author hopes some Nigerian scholar
is already at work on it.
