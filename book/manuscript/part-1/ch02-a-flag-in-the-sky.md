# Chapter 2 — A Flag in the Sky

Every new nation buys three things first: a flag, an anthem, and an
airline. The flag is cheap, the anthem is free, and the airline is the most
expensive symbol a poor country can own.

On the morning of 1 October 1960, as the Union Jack came down over Lagos,
the aircraft of the new national carrier were part of the ceremony — the
green-and-white livery overhead a promise that the country below would
rise with them. Nigeria Airways had been created two years earlier out of
the dissolving West African Airways Corporation, with the government in
partnership, initially, with the British shipping line Elder Dempster and
BOAC — a transitional arrangement, everyone understood, until the airline
could be fully and gloriously Nigerian. Its aircraft would carry the
green-white-green to London, to New York, to the capitals of the new
Africa. Its logo, in time, would be a flying elephant — a creature chosen,
one assumes, for majesty rather than aerodynamics, though history would
have its joke either way.

It is easy, from the wreckage of what came later, to condescend to the
founders' airline dream, and this chapter will try not to. The dream had
hard logic beneath the sentiment. A country of Nigeria's size — fifty-five
million people at independence, spread across a territory twice the size
of California with three great population centers separated by poor roads
and a seasonal river system — was a natural aviation market if anywhere in
Africa was. A national carrier promised connectivity the railways could
not, foreign exchange earnings, a training ladder into modern technical
employment, and presence: in 1960, an airline was how a new state
introduced itself to the world, the way a website would be four decades
later. Nor was the state-owned model eccentric. In 1960 nearly every
airline on earth outside the United States was state-owned; Air France,
BOAC, KLM, and Lufthansa were all creatures of their treasuries. Nigeria
was doing what everyone did.

What everyone did, however, concealed a fork in the road that few noticed
at the time, because it ran not through ownership but through governance.
A state airline could be owned by the nation and run as an enterprise —
disciplined by its books, its schedules, and an external standard of
professionalism, with the government as shareholder rather than manager.
Or it could be run as an annex of the state itself: its jobs part of the
patronage economy, its routes part of diplomacy, its cash flow part of the
budget's gray zones, its management appointed the way permanent
secretaries are appointed and removed the way ministers are removed.
Ethiopian Airlines, as we saw, was deliberately built on the first model
and defended, by successive Ethiopian regimes of every ideology, as too
valuable to raid. Nigeria Airways drifted — not immediately, and never
irreversibly, but steadily — onto the second. The drift, not any single
decision, is the story of this chapter.

## The boom

For its first decade, the airline could plausibly claim to be succeeding.
The 1960s fleet — F27 Friendships on domestic trunks, a Comet and then
Boeing 707s on the London run — was modern by the standards of the
continent. Nigerian pilots came up through cadetships in Britain and
Canada; Nigerian engineers earned licenses recognized abroad. The airline
survived even the catastrophe of the decade — the civil war of 1967–70,
during which civil aviation was militarized, airports became strategic
targets, and the Biafran airlift wrote its own strange chapter of aviation
history in the night skies over Uli. When the war ended, the airline
resumed growth as if the interruption had been weather.

Then came the boom. The oil price explosions of 1973–74 and 1979
transformed Nigeria's fiscal reality with a speed few societies have ever
absorbed well. Federal revenue multiplied; the naira was strong; the
country embarked on the greatest spending expansion in African history —
new capital city, new universities, new steel mills, new everything. FESTAC
'77, the great festival of Black and African arts, announced Lagos as a
capital of the Black world. Nigeria Airways expanded with the mood: wide-
body DC-10s and 747s for the intercontinental routes, 737s for a thickening
domestic network, orders and options and route announcements in every
year's headlines. By the late 1970s the airline was carrying over a
million passengers annually, flying to four continents, and employing a
workforce measured in the tens of thousands. Lagos's Ikeja airport, renamed
in 1976 for the assassinated head of state Murtala Muhammed, gained a new
international terminal modeled explicitly on Amsterdam's Schiphol — the
most modern in Africa, it was said, at the ribbon-cutting.

The reader who has absorbed Chapter 1's lesson — distinguish the artifacts
of aviation from its institutions — will already be asking the right
question. The fleet was growing; was the system? Three numbers from the
era, assembled from the airline's own records and the recollections of its
veterans, answer it.

The first is the ratio of aircraft types to fleet size. By 1980 Nigeria
Airways operated roughly a dozen distinct aircraft types in a fleet of
about thirty — Boeing and Douglas and Fokker and Airbus, long-haul and
short-haul, a maintenance and training nightmare that no commercially
governed airline would tolerate. Fleets grow that way when acquisitions
are decided as diplomacy and finance ministry theater — a 747 because
flags need one; an Airbus because Europe called — rather than as fleet
planning. Every additional type meant separate spares inventories,
separate simulator arrangements abroad, separate engineering
certifications: complexity purchased at boom prices, to be maintained
later at bust prices.

The second is management tenure. The airline ran through chief executives
at a rate that eventually exceeded one per year — across the whole of its
existence it would have more than twenty, many lasting months. Each
arrived owing his appointment to a patron, each froze or reversed his
predecessor's arrangements, and each learned that the position's real
constituency was not the passenger but the appointing power. No
maintenance program, no training pipeline, no route rationalization
survives management churn of that speed. Churn is what patronage looks
like on an organization chart.

The third is the payroll. By the airline's later years its
employee-per-aircraft ratio would reach levels several times industry
norms — a workforce that had become, in effect, a federal employment
program with a flying division. This is worth stating carefully, because
the individuals were not the problem: the airline contained, to its end,
engineers and pilots of world caliber, as its diaspora would prove. The
problem was arithmetic. An airline that exists partly to distribute jobs
cannot shrink to its revenue, cannot pay competitively at the top of its
technical grades, and cannot say no — and an airline that cannot say no
is accumulating risk somewhere on its balance sheet, usually in the
hangar.

What did the boom look like from inside the uniform? The
first generation of Nigeria Airways flight crew — the cadets of
the 1960s Britain-and-Canada schemes, captains by the mid-1970s —
worked a career that their successors would remember as a golden
age and their contemporaries experienced as a daily argument
with entropy. The flying itself was world-standard: London and
New York in the 707, the Hajj rotations, type courses at Boeing's
schoolhouse, salaries that made an airline captain one of the
most prosperous professionals in Lagos. But the veterans'
memoirs and interviews return, with striking consistency, to the
argument: the trip that operated with a deferred-defect list
that would have grounded the aircraft in the airline of a
neighbor's registry; the roster built and rebuilt overnight as
management's VIP charters commandeered aircraft; the simulator
recurrency postponed for budget seasons; the quiet professional
arithmetic — every senior pilot of the era describes some
version of it — of deciding personally, at the head of the
jetway, which of the airline's accumulating compromises one
would carry into the air and which one would refuse at the cost
of a summons to headquarters. The company's safety, by the
late boom, was increasingly a private subscription service run
by its professionals against their employer's drift — Chapter
3's heroism-as-symptom, already pricing itself into the
system's margins while the annual reports still photographed
new aircraft. When the diaspora chapter of this story opens in
the 1980s, its first recruits were exactly these people: not
failures fleeing a collapse, but professionals accepting, at
last, a standing offer to work where the argument was not
daily. Institutions do not only lose their people to better
pay. They lose them to the exhaustion of winning the same
argument ten thousand times.

None of this was secret, and none of it was uniquely Nigerian. It was the
standard pathology of the era's state enterprises, from Lagos to Lima. The
difference is that when a state grain board fails, the grain rots. When a
state airline fails, the failure eventually arrives at several hundred
miles per hour. Aviation's unforgivingness, which Chapter 1 called its
analytic virtue, was about to demonstrate itself.

## War in the air, and the first jet grave

Two events of the airline's first decade complicate the boom
narrative and deserve rescue from the footnotes.

The first is the civil war. From 1967 to 1970, while Nigeria Airways'
timetable shrank to the federal side's needs, the country hosted one
of the strangest air operations of the century: the Biafran airlift.
With the secessionist enclave blockaded, its supply line became a
nightly shuttle of chartered and improvised transports — church-
organized relief on one channel, arms on another — flying blacked-out
into a stretch of reinforced road at Uli, landing lights flicked on
for seconds, mercenary and missionary pilots sharing a traffic
pattern under federal guns. The airlift's moral history belongs to
other books. Its institutional deposit belongs here: the war
normalized, at scale and under the highest sanction, precisely the
aviation the rules forbid — unregistered aircraft, falsified
manifests, night operations into unlicensed strips, the whole
repertoire of flight as improvisation. Some of that repertoire's
veterans, machines and men both, flowed after 1970 into the region's
gray charter markets, and the gray markets never fully closed. When
Chapter 10 finds a funeral charter falling out of the Lagos sky in
2013 with paperwork that should have grounded it, the lineage traces,
by more steps than one but without a break, to the normalizations of
the war years. Wars end; their exemptions retire more slowly.

The second event is the crash of November 1969 — Nigeria's first
great jet-age loss, and the most completely forgotten disaster in
this book. Nigeria Airways' VC10, inbound to Lagos at the end of the
London service in the early morning of 20 November, descended into
forest short of the runway and was destroyed with all 87 aboard. The
VC10 was the airline's flagship, the London run its showcase; the
dead included prominent Nigerians and foreigners; and the event has
left almost no trace in national memory — no memorial, no annual
journalism, no public report that the ordinary Nigerian reader could
ever have consulted. The investigation's threads (approach procedure,
navigational aids, crew factors) ran into the era's institutional
sand. It was 1969: the war was ending, the state's attention was
entirely elsewhere, and the machinery for public technical
accounting — a machinery Britain would have supplied automatically a
decade earlier — had been repatriated without replacement, exactly as
this chapter's handover section described. The VC10's significance is
thus not its causation, which remains thin in the public record, but
its reception: it established, at the first full-scale test, the
national grammar for aviation catastrophe that Part II will conjugate
seven times — mourn intensely, investigate opaquely, publish nothing,
memorialize privately, proceed. Every element of the grammar was
already in place before the boom began. The boom merely bought bigger
aircraft to apply it to.

## Zaria: the institution that worked

Fairness — and analytic honesty — requires this section, because the
independence generation did build one aviation institution on the right
model, and its fate under the flag's shadow is as instructive as the
airline's.

In 1964, with support from the United Nations Development Programme and
ICAO, Nigeria established the Nigerian Civil Aviation Training Centre at
Zaria, in the savanna north of Kaduna — a school for pilots, air traffic
controllers, aircraft engineers, and the other trades of flight. Zaria was
everything the airline was not: technically governed, internationally
supervised in its founding decade, selective, and oriented toward a
standard rather than a spectacle. It trained not only Nigerians but
students from across anglophone Africa; for a generation of African
controllers and engineers, "Zaria" meant what "Sandhurst" meant in another
profession. The center — later renamed the Nigerian College of Aviation
Technology — survives to this day, and its alumni network runs through
every institution in this book, usually on the creditable side of the
ledger.

Zaria matters to the argument in two ways. First, it falsifies in advance
the lazy explanation of everything that follows — that Nigeria somehow
could not do aviation. Nigeria could, and did, wherever the institutional
design insulated competence from politics: an admissions exam rather than
a patron, a curriculum audited from Montreal rather than a budget
negotiated in Lagos. Second, Zaria's later history — decades of
underfunding, obsolete training aircraft, salaries that could not hold
instructors against airline poaching, periodic rescues, and renewed decay
— demonstrates the other half of the theorem. An institution's founding
design protects it for a while; only a political constituency protects it
forever. The airline had a constituency (everyone who could extract from
it). The regulator had none. The training college had none. Where the
extraction was, the money went; where the competence was, the
appropriations bill went thin. A country's budget is its actual aviation
policy, whatever its gazette says.

The pattern deserves one more sentence before we return to the narrative,
because it will recur in every decade of this book: in Nigerian aviation,
the institutions that produced excellence were consistently the ones
designed to be boring — examined, audited, unspectacular — and the
institutions that produced disaster were consistently the ones designed to
be seen. The country did not lack the capacity for the first kind. It
lacked, for a very long time, any political reason to prefer them.

## Kano, January 1973

The Hajj is the largest annually recurring movement of people on earth,
and by the early 1970s Nigeria — home to one of the world's great Muslim
populations — was among its largest sources. Hajj transport was aviation's
strangest market: a colossal demand spike, a few weeks long, in one
direction at a time, to a single destination, made of passengers many of
whom had never flown and would never fly again. No airline could keep a
fleet sized for it. The solution, universal then as now, was the charter:
national carriers and Hajj boards hired aircraft and crews from wherever
capacity could be found — a Boeing here, a stretched DC-8 there, operators
reputable and otherwise — to surge pilgrims across the Red Sea and home.

On 22 January 1973, a Boeing 707 chartered from the Jordanian carrier Alia
for the Nigerian Hajj operation approached Kano at the end of a flight
from Jeddah, carrying pilgrims home to the north. The winter Harmattan
does to Kano what fog does to London; visibility that morning was poor.
The approach went wrong — the aircraft came down hard short of and onto
the runway environment, broke, and burned. One hundred and seventy-six
people died. A handful survived. It was, at that moment, the deadliest
crash in the history of civil aviation.

Sit with that fact. The deadliest crash the world had yet seen involved
Nigerian passengers, on Nigerian soil, in the Nigerian boom's fifth year —
and it is barely remembered today outside Nigeria, and imperfectly within
it. Part of the amnesia is general: the 1970s were aviation's bloodiest
decade worldwide, and records set in January were broken by other horrors
soon enough. But part of it is specific, and diagnostic. The aircraft was
Jordanian, the crew was foreign, the charter had been arranged through the
apparatus of the Hajj rather than the airline proper; investigation
belonged formally to Nigeria as the state of occurrence, with Jordan and
the manufacturer's state in attendance. In the seams between all those
jurisdictions, accountability quietly bled out. There was grief — Kano
buried the dead in long trenches, and the north mourned through the
spring — but there was no reckoning: no public report that Nigerians could
read, no named institutional failure, no reform with a date on it. The
state's operational conclusion, as far as the record shows, was that a
foreign airline had crashed a foreign plane, regrettably, in bad weather —
an act of God with a Jordanian registration.

The alternative conclusion, available even then, was that Nigeria had
built a system in which the most dangerous flying its citizens did — mass
charters, surge operations, unfamiliar crews, minimal continuity — was
precisely the flying over which its institutions exercised the least
control; and that a state whose citizens board aircraft it does not
oversee has not avoided responsibility but only deferred it. That
conclusion was not drawn. Eighteen years later, at Jeddah, in an almost
perfectly recurved echo — a chartered foreign aircraft, a Hajj operation,
Nigerian pilgrims, institutional responsibility dissolving across three
countries — the deferred bill would arrive, with 261 names on it, and
Chapter 4 of this book will read it line by line. Institutions that do
not digest their first warning are not granted gentler second ones.

## The routes as foreign policy

One boom-era pattern deserves separate notice because its costs
compounded quietly for fifty years: the use of the route
network as diplomatic currency.

An international air route is, commercially, a franchise; the
bilateral air services agreements that govern them are, for a
flag carrier, the deed book. The Nigeria of the boom negotiated
its bilaterals as an instrument of presence — routes to fellow
African capitals as pan-African statement, frequencies granted
to partners as summit courtesy, the network drawn by the
foreign ministry's map rather than the airline's spreadsheet.
The airline flew the statements at a loss, which the treasury
absorbed, which was the point: the network was purchasing
standing, not revenue. The practice's true price emerged
later, in the lean decades, in two forms. Routes flown for
diplomacy could not be cut without diplomatic incident, so the
dying airline hemorrhaged longest exactly where it earned
least. And the bilateral deed book, negotiated from symbolism
rather than strategy, systematically underpriced the asset
that mattered: access to Nigeria's own market. Foreign
carriers accumulated frequencies and capacity into Lagos on
terms a commercially minded negotiator would never have
granted — terms that outlived the airline whose interests
they were traded against, and that later Nigerian carriers,
attempting international operations from the 2000s onward,
found arrayed against them like a mortgage their grandparents
had signed. The lesson recurs throughout aviation's political
economy and beyond it: when a state spends an institution's
commercial assets as diplomatic currency, the expenditure is
invisible until the institution must live on what remains.
Chapter 17's hub question will be fought, in part, clause by
clause, through the renegotiation of exactly this inheritance.

## The terminal and the mirror

Before the reckoning, one more scene from the boom, because it captures
the era's self-understanding better than any statistic.

In the late 1970s Nigeria opened the new international terminal at Lagos —
named, with the airport, for Murtala Muhammed, the military head of state
assassinated in 1976 and canonized by the manner of his death. The
terminal was consciously copied from Schiphol, then the most admired
passenger building in Europe: the same finger piers, the same flowing
levels, a piece of Amsterdam assembled on the Ikeja plain. The opening was
a national event. Here was the boom made walkable — proof, in glass and
terrazzo, that Nigeria had arrived at the world's departure level.

Anyone who transited both buildings over the following twenty years
received a tutorial in the difference between a structure and a system.
Schiphol remained Schiphol because a dense, dull web of institutions —
maintenance contracts, performance audits, an airport authority governed
like a company — renewed it continuously. Its Lagos twin began dying on
its opening day: air conditioning that failed and stayed failed,
escalators that became staircases, a roof that leaked onto immigration
queues, generators coughing through the power cuts, and around it all an
ecosystem of touts and rent-seekers filling the vacuum where management
should have been. By the 1990s, Murtala Muhammed had become a byword among
travelers for dysfunction, and the FAA and international carriers would
periodically single out Lagos for security deficiencies. Same blueprint,
same decade, same equipment: divergent institutions. Two buildings,
identical at birth, had run the book's whole argument as an experiment in
concrete.

The terminal was a mirror, and it foretold. What happened to the building
was about to happen to the airline that used it, the regulator that
inhabited it, and the whole boom-bought apparatus of Nigerian flight — the
same slow subtraction, deferral by deferral. It required only that the
money stop. In 1981, it stopped.

## The airline as mirror of the state, 1958–1979

Before the chapter's catastrophe, one more analytical layer: the
airline's first two decades track the Nigerian state's own
constitutional history with a fidelity that makes it usable as a
political seismograph, and reading it that way explains features
the business history alone cannot.

The First Republic airline (1958–66) was the federation's
airline: its board seats and management posts balanced across
regions with the same care as the federal cabinet, its route
map — Lagos, Kano, Enugu, the regional capitals — a diagram of
the tripodal federation, its recruitment a patronage lattice in
which each region monitored its share. When the federation
broke — the 1966 coups, the pogroms, the war — the airline
broke along the identical seams: eastern staff withdrew or were
withdrawn; aircraft became war assets; the "national" carrier
became, for three years, a belligerent's. The post-war airline
of the 1970s was the unitary military state's airline: run from
the center, its federal character now meaning federal
*character* — an instrument of the oil-funded, Lagos-
headquartered, decree-governed state that the war had built.
The oil boom's fleet expansion was that state's expansion; the
type-count chaos was its procurement politics; the payroll
growth was its employment politics; and the airline's
management churn tracked, coup by coup and reshuffle by
reshuffle, the center's own instability. Nothing about this
correspondence is accidental. A wholly state-owned enterprise
in a personalized state *cannot* have a corporate character
distinct from the state's; its governance is the state's
governance, applied to aircraft. This is why the Ethiopian
comparison keeps its force — the founding bargain there built,
deliberately, a firewall between the state's character and the
airline's — and why every proposal to revive a Nigerian flag
carrier without first reforming the state's grip (a proposal
renewed, Chapter 16 records, as recently as 2023) is a
proposal to re-run this chapter with newer aircraft. The
airline was never the patient. It was the thermometer.

## After Kano: the anatomy of a non-response

Because Chapter 4 will lean on the claim that 1973's lessons went
unlearned, the non-response deserves its own forensic paragraph
rather than an aside.

What a functioning system does after a disaster of Kano's scale
has a checklist, and the checklist existed in 1973 — ICAO's Annex
13 was already two decades old. State of occurrence opens an
investigation with international participation; findings are
published; recommendations are tracked to closure; and the
policy layer asks the second-order question: what class of
operation produced this, and does the class need redesign? Jordan
and the manufacturer's state had standing; Nigeria, as state of
occurrence, held the pen. The public record of what followed is
thin to the point of transparency — no published Nigerian report,
no gazetted findings, no traceable reform of the charter
apparatus. The Gowon government, months from the oil embargo
windfall that would transform its budget and attention, treated
the event through the era's two available frames: religious
(martyrdom in pilgrimage, a consolation with deep legitimacy)
and diplomatic (a Jordanian aircraft, handled state-to-state).
The third frame — systemic, regulatory, checklist-driven — had
no domestic constituency to insist on it. The Hajj apparatus
itself, answering to religious-affairs authorities rather than
aviation ones, continued under successive arrangements that
each subsequent decade found wanting: the pattern of surge
charters at minimum price under maximum schedule pressure
persisted into the 1990s, when Chapter 4 collects its
consequence, and into controversies well beyond.

The non-response of 1973 thus establishes the baseline against
which 2006's response must be measured — and the measurement is
the book's spine. Same country, same class of shock; thirty-three
years apart; opposite institutional outcomes. Whatever changed
between Gowon's Nigeria and Obasanjo's was not the people, the
religion, or the "culture" — it was the presence, by 2006, of a
democratic information market, an organized victims'
constituency, and an installable institutional design waiting on
the shelf. History rarely runs so clean a controlled trial on a
single nation. This book is, among other things, that trial's
report.

## What the flag concealed

Here the comparative lens earns its keep, because the 1970s offered a
live-action controlled experiment that nobody at the time understood they
were running.

In Addis Ababa, Ethiopian Airlines spent the decade doing unglamorous
things. Its American management contract had ended in 1971 with the
Ethiopianization it had always been designed to produce; the airline kept
the systems, the training academy, the maintenance culture. When
revolution came in 1974 and a Marxist military junta seized the state,
the Derg — which nationalized everything and shot much of the old elite —
left the airline's management substantially alone, having grasped that it
was one of the only reliable hard-currency machines the state owned. The
airline flew through the terror, the famine, and the civil war. It made
money. It trained pilots other African carriers borrowed. The lesson,
seldom stated because it is uncomfortable: institutional protection is a
political choice, available even to poor states, even to bad states, the
moment the state decides an institution is worth more functioning than
raided.

Nigeria in the same decade ran the opposite protocol with ten times the
money. The airline was raided — not spectacularly, but continuously, in
the form of jobs, tickets, contracts, and appointments extracted by the
political system as a matter of routine. The regulator, meanwhile,
scarcely existed as an independent force: civil aviation was a directorate
within the transport ministry, budgeted as an afterthought, staffed as a
posting rather than a profession, and structurally incapable of saying no
to a national carrier that outranked it in every currency that mattered.
Oversight of the boom — the dozen aircraft types, the charter economy,
the training shortfalls — would have required a referee standing outside
the game. Nigeria had put the referee on the home team's payroll.

The 1970s were the years in which this arrangement was affordable,
because oil paid for the slack: when maintenance was expensive, one
bought spares; when a route lost money, the treasury absorbed it; when
the Hajj needed lift, one chartered it. Boom-era institutions are never
tested by booms. The test comes when the money stops — when the deferred
maintenance, the deferred training, the deferred regulation, all the
quiet deficits accumulated behind the flag, must suddenly be paid from an
empty account. In 1981 the oil price broke, and Nigeria's long emergency
began. What it did to the country's aviation system — slowly, invisibly,
one unordered spare part at a time — is the subject of the next chapter,
and the reason the crashes of Part II, when they come, should be read not
as lightning strikes but as invoices.

The flag, through all of it, kept flying. That was the trouble with the
flag: it flew whether or not anything beneath it worked. Symbols are
load-bearing in politics precisely because they do not depend on
performance — and a symbol that cannot fail is the perfect roof under
which everything else can.
