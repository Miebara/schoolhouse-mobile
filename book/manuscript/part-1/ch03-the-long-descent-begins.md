# Chapter 3 — The Long Descent Begins

Decay is rarely dramatic. It is a spare part not ordered, a simulator
session not renewed, a runway light that fails and is not replaced, a
salary paid two months late to a man whose signature certifies that an
aircraft is safe to fly.

By the end of the 1980s, visitors to the Nigeria Airways maintenance base
at Ikeja could see the decade summarized in a single exhibit. Along the
apron sat the airline's grounded wide-bodies — among them, for a period, a
Boeing 747 that had become what engineers call a "Christmas tree": an
aircraft kept unflyable so that its parts could keep others flying. A
hydraulic pump gone to one sister ship, instruments to another, seats and
slides and valves fanned out across the fleet — the airline consuming its
own body to postpone the amputation of its schedule. Aircraft
cannibalization is a legitimate, tightly controlled practice at
well-run airlines. As a way of life, it is the aviation equivalent of a
family burning the furniture through a winter: rational each cold night,
catastrophic as a strategy, and diagnostic — always — of an income that
has stopped matching a way of living.

Nigeria's income had stopped matching everything. The oil price that had
financed the boom broke in 1981 and collapsed in 1986; federal revenue
fell by more than half in real terms; the naira, defended for a while and
then devalued in stages, lost more than nine-tenths of its dollar value
over the decade. For aviation this was not one problem among many — it
was close to an extinction event, because aviation is, in a way few
industries are, a dollar industry that happens to earn local currency.
Aircraft are priced in dollars. Spare parts, insurance, simulator time,
manufacturer support, navigation databases, even the charts in the
cockpit: dollars. A Nigerian airline in 1988 sold naira tickets at
politically supervised fares and owed dollars to everyone who kept its
aircraft legal. Every devaluation silently multiplied the real cost of
airworthiness. No press release announced this; no coup plotter mentioned
it among his grievances. But when the historians of Part II ask why
Nigerian aircraft in the 1990s were old, why their spares were scarce,
why their crews' recurrent training was deferred and their insurance
threadbare — the answer begins here, in the exchange rate, the least
dramatic villain in this book and among the most lethal.

## The politics of the descent

The money stopped; the politics then determined where the shortage would
be felt, and the politics of Nigeria in the 1980s were the politics of
military rule — a coup in 1983, another in 1985, and, beneath the
successive juntas, the progressive fusion of state and barracks that
Nigerians simply call "the military era."

Military government did three specific things to aviation, beyond the
general damage it did to every institution it touched.

First, it completed the destruction of management continuity. The Second
Republic (1979–83) had already treated Nigeria Airways as electoral
furniture; the juntas treated it as a command. Chief executives — some of
them serving or retired officers — rotated through on the coup cycle. Each
change of government at the top produced a change of management at the
airline, each new management suspended its predecessor's contracts and
launched its own, and the procurement pipeline — that dull artery through
which an airline's future flows — was severed and re-plumbed every two or
three years. An airline can survive poverty with patience; Ethiopian
proved it through the same decade with less money and a worse war. What
no airline can survive is the abolition of its own memory.

Second, the military era normalized the exemption. Soldiers do not queue,
and regimes born of emergency govern by exception: this flight will carry
whom it is told; this aircraft will be released regardless; this
inspection finding will be waived, this time, because the general's
schedule requires it. Each exemption was small. Their sum was a culture —
the "waiver culture," aviation professionals of the era named it — in
which the rules were understood by everyone to be the opening position in
a negotiation with power. The reader should hold that phrase, because
Part II is largely a record of its price, and Part III will argue that
dismantling it, rather than any technology, was the substance of the
post-2006 reform. Safety rules work only when they are unnegotiable; a
rule negotiable for a general is negotiable, eventually, for anyone with
his phone number.

Third, and least visibly, the military era starved the referee. Civil
aviation oversight remained, through the 1980s, a directorate of the
federal ministry — its inspectors civil servants on civil service pay, its
budget a rounding error, its independence a legal fiction under a
government in which the transport minister was an air commodore and the
airline's chairman outranked the director of civil aviation in every
register Nigerian life recognizes. Inspector positions went unfilled;
inspector training abroad, a dollar expense, was among the first casualties
of austerity; and the technical library, the airworthiness records, the
whole administrative sediment of oversight thinned toward transparency.
By decade's end Nigeria possessed a regulator the way the grounded 747
possessed engines: the housing was there, and everyone continued to speak
as if the function were, too.

## Structural adjustment and the strange birth of the private airline

In 1986, under General Ibrahim Babangida, Nigeria adopted a Structural
Adjustment Programme — the IMF-flavored package of devaluation,
deregulation, and privatization that swept the indebted world in that
decade. SAP's social history is bitter and contested; its aviation
consequence was specific and, at first glance, paradoxical: it created
the Nigerian private airline.

The logic was orthodox. Nigeria Airways was a fiscal hemorrhage and a
monopoly; deregulation would let private capital supply the capacity the
flag carrier no longer could. Licenses flowed from 1988 onward, and by the
early 1990s a swarm of private carriers had risen — Okada Air, Kabo Air,
ADC Airlines, Gas Air, Concord, Triax, dozens more across the decade,
their names a roll call of the era's entrepreneurial energy. Some were
serious enterprises. Okada, at its peak, operated a fleet of BAC 1-11s
that outnumbered many flag carriers'; ADC was building something
recognizable as a modern airline, with ambitions matching Chapter 6's
tragedy. The traveling public, offered an alternative to the airline that
canceled by habit, voted with its feet. Domestic aviation grew even as the
economy shrank — one of SAP's few unambiguous supply responses.

But observe what had actually been done, because the sequence is the
lesson. Deregulation had multiplied the number of operators by ten at the
precise historical moment when the capacity to oversee even one had been
starved to the bone. The aircraft the new airlines flew were the aircraft
SAP-era dollars could buy: elderly BAC 1-11s and 707s and 727s, second-
and third-hand, cheap to acquire and voracious to maintain — the geriatric
fleet whose economics Chapter 12 will dissect. The pilots and engineers
were, in the main, alumni of Nigeria Airways and Zaria — competent men
and women, now scattered across a dozen thin balance sheets, working for
owners for whom a grounded aircraft was a bleeding artery. And above the
whole new ecosystem stood the same ministry directorate, with the same
handful of inspectors, now responsible for certifying, auditing, and
disciplining an industry ten times the size — in dollars it did not have,
with staff it could not train, under governments for which aviation
policy meant the presidential fleet.

The United States had run this experiment first, and the comparison is
exact enough to be useful. American deregulation in 1978 also unleashed
entry, price competition, and marginal operators; the safety professionals
predicted carnage. Carnage did not come — accident rates continued their
long fall — and the reason is now textbook: deregulation of the *market*
was paired with reinforcement of the *referee*. The FAA's oversight
apparatus, the NTSB's investigations, the litigation system, the insurance
market's discipline — the institutional immune system was mature before
the pathogens multiplied. Nigeria ran the same experiment with the immune
system switched off. The 1990s would supply the laboratory results, and
they are the subject of the next seven chapters.

## What flying felt like

Statistics anesthetize; it is worth descending, briefly, to the departure
lounge, because the texture of late-1980s Nigerian domestic aviation is
itself evidence — and because millions of Nigerians carry versions of
this memory.

You bought your ticket, but the ticket was the beginning of a
negotiation, not the end. Flights were "confirmed" in a system where
confirmation was social, not computational: the airline's schedule was
aspirational, the aircraft's departure depended on its arrival from
somewhere else, and boarding, when it came, could be a physical
competition in which the manifest was reconciled to reality afterward, if
at all. The reader should note that word — *manifest* — and keep it. In
November 1996, and again in October 2005, the country would discover at
the worst possible moments that knowing exactly who is aboard a Nigerian
airliner was not a routine certainty but an investigative task. A society
learns its record-keeping habits in ordinary time and pays for them in
extraordinary time.

Around the terminal, the state's aviation presence had meanwhile
reorganized itself into an alphabet that Nigerians would come to know
well. The Federal Airports Authority of Nigeria — FAAN — ran the airports
from 1976 onward (its earlier incarnation, the Nigeria Airports
Authority, having been another child of the boom). The navigation aids
and airspace would eventually pass to a dedicated agency, NAMA, in the
1990s; the weather service to NiMet; the regulatory directorate,
ultimately, to the NCAA. The alphabet matters less than the pattern: each
crisis of the system was answered institutionally by *subdivision* —
another agency, another acronym, another board and headquarters and
budget line — rather than by empowerment. By the century's end Nigerian
aviation would have more institutions than at independence and less
institutional capacity, a proliferation of bodies sharing an anemia of
function. Organograms are cheap; inspectors are expensive; governments
under fiscal siege consistently buy the first and defer the second.

And yet — the other half of the truth — the system worked, daily, in the
sense that mattered to its passengers: aircraft mostly arrived. The
pilots were often superb, precisely because the environment demanded it;
veterans of that era describe hand-flown VOR approaches into airports
whose instrument landing systems had died years before, fuel decisions
made against forecasts that were folklore, radio calls relayed
pilot-to-pilot across gaps in coverage. A generation of aviators became
virtuosos of the workaround. Virtuosity of that kind is magnificent and
corrosive at once: it keeps the schedule alive, and by keeping the
schedule alive it hides the decay from the public and the treasury,
converting infrastructure failure into private professional burden. The
workaround is the enemy of the work order. Systems that run on heroism
are systems whose margins are being spent invisibly, every day, until the
day the heroism is one contingency short — and no one can say in advance
which day that will be.

## The airline dies slowly

Nigeria Airways' final act ran through the 1990s toward a conclusion in
2003, but its death was legible by 1990, and its manner deserves recording
because the corpse would poison the water table for decades.

The fleet contracted from its boom-era thirty toward single digits — by
the end, famously, to almost nothing flyable at all. The routes retreated:
the New York run, the pride of the boom, ended in the early 1980s;
European stations closed one by one; the airline that had flown four
continents finished as an unreliable domestic operator with a London slot
it leased out. What remained vigorous to the end was the airline's
function as an extraction site. Its liquidation in 2003–04 revealed the
expected archaeology: debts in the hundreds of millions of dollars,
pension obligations to tens of thousands of employees paid partially and
late over years of agony, assets — slots, properties, receivables —
whose disposal generated its own genre of scandal. The employees, as
usual, were the last creditors standing: men who had certified 747s
queued for pensions that arrived, when they arrived, in fractions.

Two consequences of this long-drawn death matter to everything that
follows. The first is human capital flight, in the exact sense: the
airline's best pilots and engineers went abroad — to the Gulf carriers
then rising on the same oil that had once financed Lagos, to British and
American operators, to the African airlines that still functioned. The
diaspora would become one of Nigeria's exports of distinction, and one of
its standing rebukes: the individual excellence was real, and it was now
improving other countries' safety statistics. The second consequence was
reputational and institutional. For the international system — lessors,
insurers, alliance auditors, foreign regulators — "Nigerian aviation"
came to mean the flag carrier's unpaid bills and the ministry's unanswered
letters. The private airlines of the 1990s, whatever their individual
merits, would buy aircraft, insurance, and credibility at prices marked
up by the flag's default. Nations, like people, pay compound interest on
their reputations.

The comparative note here is Ghana, whose flag carrier ran the same
trajectory to the same terminus — Ghana Airways, mismanaged and raided in
close parallel, banned from US airspace in 2004 and liquidated the next
year. Same colonial parent, same nationalist founding, same patronage
capture, same death: the parallelism is close enough to constitute
replication of the experiment. And the control case, once more, is
Ethiopian — which entered the 1990s having survived a regime change more
violent than anything Nigeria experienced, and left the decade profitable,
expanding, and about to order aircraft American airlines had not yet
flown. The variable was never African-ness, poverty, or turbulence. It
was whether the state treated the airline as an institution or as a
carcass.

## 1990

Set the stage, then, as the ninth decade of the century closes, for the
seven chapters that follow.

A country of roughly ninety million people, its treasury gutted, its
currency a fraction of itself, governed by a military regime entering its
own baroque late period. A flag carrier in terminal decline, hemorrhaging
its competence into the world's cockpits. A new private aviation sector,
vigorous and undercapitalized, flying the developed world's retired
aircraft into airports built for the boom and maintained by the bust —
navigation aids failing, radar partial, fire services notional, power
intermittent. A regulator that existed chiefly on paper, inside a ministry,
under soldiers, in a culture where every rule had its waiver. A training
college running on fumes. An accident investigation function that had
never once, in thirty years of independence, given the Nigerian public a
full account of why their dead were dead.

No single person intended this arrangement. That is the point of these
first three chapters, and the reason they precede the wreckage rather than
follow it. The system that would kill several thousand people over the
next two decades was not built by villains; it was accumulated by
deferral — colonial defaults never revised, boom purchases never
institutionalized, bust economies never confronted, each decade handing
the next a slightly hollower inheritance and a slightly more practiced
vocabulary of denial. By 1990 the holes in the layers of defense, to
return to James Reason's image, were no longer scattered. They were
drifting into alignment. All the system now required was traffic, time,
and weather — and the 1990s, generous in every cruelty, would supply all
three.

The first invoice arrived in July 1991, at a runway in Saudi Arabia, in
the oldest costume in Nigerian aviation: a chartered aircraft, a foreign
crew, and the pilgrims of the Hajj.
