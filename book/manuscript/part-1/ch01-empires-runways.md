# PART I — THE ASCENT (1925–1990)

# Chapter 1 — Empire's Runways

The airplane arrived in Nigeria before the idea of Nigeria had settled.

On the first day of November 1925, a formation of Royal Air Force biplanes
droned out of the Harmattan haze and circled the walls of Kano. The city
below them was already a thousand years old. Its walls had seen caravans
from Tripoli and Timbuktu, armies from Sokoto, and — only twenty-two years
earlier — the Maxim guns of Frederick Lugard's West African Frontier Force.
Now it acquired a new kind of gate. The aircraft, DH.9As of the RAF's Cairo
command, had flown across the continent in stages, from the Nile to Lake
Chad and on to the Sahel, to demonstrate that the British Empire could move
through African skies as confidently as it moved on African rivers and
rails. Crowds gathered outside the walls to watch the machines land on a
cleared strip of laterite. The emir received the airmen with ceremony. The
colonial Resident wrote enthusiastic dispatches. And somewhere in the files
of the Colonial Office, a precedent was set that would shape Nigerian
aviation for a century: the airplane had come to Nigeria as an instrument
of somebody else's system, landing on Nigerian soil but answering to
institutions an ocean away.

It is worth pausing on that image, because this book will return to it in
forms its 1925 spectators could not have imagined — a Canadian-crewed
charter burning over Saudi Arabia with Nigerian pilgrims aboard; a
British-built jet, past its prime, falling into a Kano neighborhood
seventy-seven years almost to the month after those first biplanes landed.
The history of aviation in Nigeria is, to a degree unusual even among
postcolonial states, a history of imported systems: machines, rules,
routes, and expectations designed elsewhere, grafted onto local
institutions that were themselves imported, improvised, or starved. Where
the graft took, Nigerians flew safely. Where it did not, they fell. The
purpose of this first chapter is to understand what, exactly, was planted
in those early decades — because the shape of the root system explains a
great deal about the later shape of the tree.

## An empire discovers the shortcut

Aviation came to Africa in the 1920s for the same reason railways had come
in the 1890s: imperial logistics. The British Empire after the First World
War was larger than ever and more expensive than ever, and its
administrators were seized by a technology that promised to compress it.
The journey from London to Lagos by sea took two weeks; the mails, the
administrators, the currency shipments and the crisis telegrams all moved
at the speed of the steamer. Aircraft promised to collapse those distances
— and, not incidentally, to police the empire cheaply. The RAF had already
demonstrated in Iraq that a squadron of biplanes could substitute for
brigades of infantry, a doctrine politely called "air control."

The Cairo–Kano flights of 1925 were the West African application of this
logic, and their route tells you everything about their purpose. They did
not begin in Lagos, the commercial capital of British West Africa, or
follow the population. They ran laterally across the Sahel, linking
Britain's Sudanese and Nigerian possessions along the old caravan latitudes
— an administrative corridor, drawn for the convenience of the colonizer's
map. When regular civil aviation followed in the 1930s, it, too, followed
imperial rather than Nigerian logic. Imperial Airways' flying boats and
landplanes worked their way down the continent's edges on the great trunk
routes to Cape Town, with West Africa as a spur: Khartoum to Kano, Kano to
Lagos, feeder services to the coast. Kano's airfield — the strip those
crowds had watched in 1925, progressively improved — became one of the
busiest aviation crossroads in Africa not because northern Nigeria
generated traffic but because it sat conveniently on the way to somewhere
else. By the Second World War, when the Allies built up the West African
Reinforcement Route to ferry aircraft from Takoradi and Lagos across the
continent to the Middle East theater, Nigeria found itself hosting some of
the most strategically important runways on earth, built to specifications
its own government had not written, for purposes its own people had not
chosen.

None of this was unusual; it was how empires worked, and every colonized
territory got some version of it. What matters for our story is the
institutional residue. Three features of colonial aviation would prove
remarkably durable.

First, **the network pointed outward.** The routes that received investment
were the ones connecting Nigeria to London and to the imperial system;
internal connectivity was an afterthought. At independence, Nigeria
inherited a handful of well-found international aerodromes and a thin
scatter of domestic strips — an aviation system shaped like a funnel, wide
toward Europe and narrow at home. Sixty years later, the funnel persists:
the Lagos–London route remains among the most valuable in Africa, while
domestic aviation — where nearly all of Nigeria's crashes would occur —
remained for decades the system's neglected child.

Second, **regulation lived abroad.** Airworthiness standards, pilot
licensing, accident investigation — all of it was administered from
Britain, under British law, by British personnel, as an extension of
British institutions. This was, in the short run, a kind of gift: colonial
Nigeria's aviation was overseen by one of the world's most competent
systems. But it meant that when the flag came down, Nigeria inherited the
*activity* of aviation without the *organs* of aviation. The regulator, the
investigator, the training pipeline — these had been branch offices of
institutions headquartered elsewhere. Independence would require growing
them from cuttings, quickly, in political soil that was itself unsettled.
Compare the railways, which for all their troubles were at least run from
Lagos by a Nigerian administration with decades of accumulated local
practice. Aviation's institutional memory sailed home with the expatriates.

Third, **aviation was fused with prestige from the beginning.** The 1925
landings were staged as theater — the emir's reception, the crowds, the
dispatches. Flight arrived in Nigeria not as a utility but as a
demonstration of power, and it never entirely lost that character. Every
subsequent Nigerian government, colonial and sovereign, civilian and
military, would treat aviation partly as a stage. The consequences of
treating a safety-critical industry as a stage will occupy a substantial
portion of this book.

## The war that built the runways

If the 1920s gave Nigeria its first aircraft and the 1930s its first
scheduled services, it was the Second World War that gave it an aviation
infrastructure — and, characteristically, the war built it for reasons that
had nothing to do with Nigeria.

By late 1940, with the Mediterranean closed to convoys and Rommel across
the Egyptian frontier, Britain faced a logistics problem of imperial scale:
how to deliver aircraft to its armies in Egypt and the Middle East without
sailing them past Axis airpower. The answer ran through West Africa.
Crated fighters and bombers were shipped to Takoradi on the Gold Coast and
to Lagos, assembled on the coast, and flown in stages across the width of
Africa — Lagos to Kano, Kano to Maiduguri, on across Chad and Sudan to
Cairo. At its peak the West African Reinforcement Route moved hundreds of
aircraft a month. Kano, that convenient waypoint of 1925, became one of
the busiest military staging posts on the continent. The Americans arrived
after 1941 and enlarged everything again; Pan American Airways, under
contract, ran transatlantic ferry and supply operations through Lagos, and
for a period the lagoon city was a node in the largest air transport
system the world had yet seen.

The war left three legacies. The first was concrete, literally: extended
runways, hangars, fuel farms, radio installations — a physical plant that
would carry Nigerian civil aviation for a generation. The second was
human. Thousands of Nigerians worked the route as riggers, fitters,
loaders, clerks, and drivers; a small number flew. Peter Thomas, a Lagosian,
became the first Nigerian commissioned into the Royal Air Force, and his
example — celebrated in the Lagos press of the day — planted the idea that
the cockpit, too, could be Nigerianized. The aviation trades entered the
Nigerian labor market a full generation before the Nigerian state would
build any institution to train them systematically.

The third legacy was subtler and worth naming precisely, because it
recurs. The wartime system worked — brilliantly — because it was run as a
closed loop by organizations (the RAF, Pan Am) that owned every link:
their aircraft, their mechanics, their fuel testers, their radio operators,
their standards, their discipline. When the war ended, the organizations
left and took the loop with them. What remained was the hardware. Nigeria's
experience of aviation excellence, from the very beginning, was the
experience of hosting somebody else's system — of watching world-class
performance happen on Nigerian soil without the institutional machinery
that produced it ever passing into Nigerian hands. The distinction between
possessing aviation's artifacts and possessing its institutions is the
oldest theme in this story, and the runways of 1945 are its first
monument.

## The corporation years

After the Second World War, Britain rationalized its West African air
activities into a single entity that deserves more attention than history
has given it: the West African Airways Corporation, established in 1946 to
serve Nigeria, the Gold Coast (Ghana), Sierra Leone, and the Gambia. WAAC
was a classic creature of late-colonial developmentalism — a public
corporation on the model of the BBC or BOAC, jointly owned by the four
territorial governments, headquartered in Lagos, and intended to knit the
region together by air.

Judged on its own terms, WAAC was a modest success. It flew de Havilland
Doves and later Herons on a lattice of West African routes; it trained the
first generation of Nigerian ground staff and, eventually, aircrew; it ran
scheduled services with respectable regularity; and it operated under the
full apparatus of British civil aviation regulation. Nigerians of a certain
generation remember it with an affection tinged by what came later. It was
also, structurally, everything its successors would not be: a technocratic
organization, deliberately insulated from any single government, run by
aviation professionals on multi-year plans, with its safety oversight
anchored in an external system too distant to be lobbied.

The insulation was, of course, colonial — accountability to four
governments meant, in practice, accountability to the Colonial Office and
to nobody Nigerian. That was its vice and its virtue at once, and the
tension is one this book will meet repeatedly in other forms: the
arrangements that protect technical institutions from politics are often,
in a young democracy, indistinguishable from the arrangements that protect
them from the public. What is not in doubt is what happened when the
insulation was removed. As the Gold Coast moved toward independence in
1957, Kwame Nkrumah pulled Ghana out of WAAC to found a national carrier of
his own — Ghana Airways, a flag for the first flag of independent Africa.
The corporation's logic collapsed; Nigeria followed suit, and WAAC's
Nigerian operations were reorganized in 1958, on the eve of independence,
into a company that would carry the new nation's name, its hopes, and
eventually its pathologies: WAAC Nigeria, soon rebranded Nigeria Airways.

The corporation's daily work deserves a page of texture, because
"technocratic" is an abstraction until one watches it operate. A
WAAC Dove's morning rotation out of Lagos in 1952 — Ikeja to
Benin to Enugu, or the coastal run toward Accra — began the
previous evening, in the fabric of small disciplines the
corporation had imported whole: the engineer's daily inspection
signed in a bound log; the load sheet computed and countersigned;
the met briefing from the colonial meteorological service; the
radio schedule with stations that answered; the fuel drawn
through filters that were themselves inspected on a calendar. If
a magneto check failed at run-up, the flight canceled — and here
is the institutional point — *nobody called anybody*. There was
no one to call. The corporation's remoteness from each
territorial government, its officers' career insulation, and the
Air Registration Board's distant, impersonal authority over its
airworthiness meant that the decision to cancel belonged to the
system, not to a negotiation. Passengers grumbled at the notice
board and waited for the afternoon Heron. The airplane did not
crash. Multiply that uncancelled phone call by every marginal
weather day and doubtful magneto of a decade, and WAAC's safety
record — unglamorous, unremembered, real — assembles itself out
of pure governance, on 1950s technology, in the same skies and
storms that fill Part II with wreckage. The reader will not
encounter a cleaner demonstration that the variable in this
book was never the continent, the weather, or the century.

Who flew, in those years? The passenger cabins of the Doves and Herons
held colonial officers on transfer, expatriate businessmen, and — in
steadily growing numbers through the 1950s — the Nigerian elite of the
independence generation: lawyers to constitutional conferences, politicians
to Lagos, traders to Accra and Freetown, students connecting toward London.
Nnamdi Azikiwe and Obafemi Awolowo and the men who negotiated Nigeria into
being were among the first generation of Nigerians for whom air travel was
an ordinary tool of public life, and the experience marked the political
imagination. The airplane was where the future felt physically real: you
boarded in a colony and disembarked, hours later, somewhere your grievances
could be argued before power. It is not incidental that the nationalist
press covered aviation with the intensity other countries reserved for
sport, or that "one day Nigerians will fly these planes" became a stock
line of the platform speeches of the era. Flight had been staged as
imperial theater in 1925; by 1955 the nationalists had claimed the stage.
The aspiration was genuine and honorable. But note its object: the
aspiration was to *fly the planes* — to occupy the visible seats, the
cockpit, the boardroom, the ownership. Nobody campaigns to inherit the
airworthiness inspectorate. The invisible seats would go unclaimed for
decades, and the visible ones would be filled at once, and that asymmetry
— glory nationalized immediately, drudgery indefinitely deferred — is as
close as this book comes to a unified theory of what followed.

The regional corporation dissolved into national fragments in the space of
two years. It is one of decolonization's small, forgotten counterfactuals.
A West African air corporation jointly owned by four sovereign states —
professionally run, too big for any one government to raid, its routes
rationalized across the region — was precisely the kind of institution that
West Africa would spend the next half-century failing to build, in aviation
as in currency, power, and trade. It had existed. It was dismantled in the
name of the flag, and every successor state got a smaller, weaker, more
politically exposed airline in exchange. Ghana Airways would die in 2004,
bankrupt and blacklisted from American skies. Nigeria Airways would die in
2003, in circumstances Chapter 3 will describe. The Doves of WAAC, in
retrospect, were carrying more than passengers.

## The aerodrome at the water's edge

Lagos entered the aviation age from the sea, which is fitting for a
city the Portuguese named for a lagoon. The first regular air services
to reach it in the 1930s included flying boats working the coastal
legs, alighting on the same waters that had carried the palm-oil
trade; the land aerodrome grew up at Ikeja, then a sandy expanse of
scrub north of the city, chosen — like airports everywhere in that
era — for emptiness rather than access. Travelers' accounts of the
early Lagos air station describe a corrugated-iron and whitewash world:
a windsock, a radio hut, fuel in drums rolled by hand, arrival times
estimated in half-days, and the mail — always the mail, the true
paying passenger of 1930s African aviation — sorted under an awning
for the launch into town. It seems quaint until one registers what it
represented: for the first time in the history of a city built on
tide and season, a schedule existed that connected Lagos to London in
days rather than weeks. Commerce reorganized around it quietly. The
colonial bank could move signatures; the trading houses could move
samples and contracts; the government could move itself. Aviation's
first Nigerian constituency was, in short, the colonial paperwork
economy — and its geography (Lagos, Kano, the mining town of Jos, the
administrative stations) was the paperwork's geography, a map of
extraction and administration onto which no Nigerian community's own
priorities were ever consulted.

The labor that built and ran these stations deserves its paragraph,
because the standard histories leave it in the passive voice.
Runways were cleared and graded by Nigerian hands, at wages and under
conditions set by the Public Works Department; the fuel drums, the
baggage, the radio batteries, the endless grass-cutting that a
tropical airfield demands — all of it Nigerian labor, supervised by a
thin expatriate technical layer that monopolized every certified
trade. The apprenticeship structures that might have transferred
those certifications — the licensing ladders, the examinations —
existed in Britain and were, in practice, closed: a Nigerian could
fuel the aircraft but not sign for it, fix the engine under
supervision but not certify the fix. The distinction between labor
and license — between doing aviation work and owning aviation
authority — was the colonial system's deepest transfer to its
successor, more durable than any runway. Decades later, Nigerian
aviation would still be organized around it: the expatriate
consultant's signature, the foreign audit, the license that
validates elsewhere. Chapter 9 will describe the moment in 2010 when
the FAA, in effect, finally countersigned Nigeria's own signature —
and the reader will appreciate by then why that bureaucratic event
carried, for the profession, the emotional charge of a second
independence.

## What the first accidents taught — and whom

Colonial aviation in Africa was dangerous, and nobody pretended otherwise.
The machines were primitive, the weather was unmapped, the Harmattan filled
the sky with dust for months at a stretch, and the distances between
airfields left little margin for the era's unreliable engines. Imperial
Airways lost aircraft along its African routes with grim regularity in the
1930s; forced landings in the bush were common enough to be a genre of
after-dinner story. The West African colonies saw their share of wrecks,
and the response to each followed a fixed ritual whose details are more
interesting than they first appear.

When an aircraft crashed in colonial Nigeria, an inspector investigated —
under the United Kingdom's Air Navigation regulations, on procedures
written by the Air Ministry in London, with the findings dispatched to
London, filed in London, and applied, where they changed anything, to
standards maintained in London. The system was, for its time, genuinely
good: Britain had been investigating aviation accidents systematically
since 1915 and understood earlier than most that the point of an
investigation was prevention, not punishment. But observe what it meant
for Nigeria. The colony experienced the crashes; the metropole
accumulated the learning. The feedback loop that turns accidents into
safety — the institutional digestion of failure that this book will argue
is the whole secret of aviation's safety revolution — ran through Nigeria
without ever touching down there. For thirty-five years, aviation learning
was one more raw material the territory exported unprocessed.

The deeper habit formed in these years was about publicity. Colonial
accident findings were administrative documents, circulated within
government, released to the public — a public of colonial subjects — at
the administration's discretion, which is to say rarely and partially. The
notion that an accident report is owed to the public, that the state must
explain a disaster to the people it fell upon, was not part of the
inheritance. When, decades later, independent Nigeria's governments sat on
accident reports for years or forever — a practice Chapter 14 will
quantify — they were not innovating. They were continuing an
administrative tradition the empire had modeled: investigation as internal
correspondence, the bereaved as an audience the state may address or not,
as it finds convenient. Institutions transmit their reflexes to their
successors, and secrecy is a reflex.

## The world that was watching

Zoom out, as this book will do once in every chapter, to the
panorama: what was aviation, in the world of 1925, when the
biplanes circled Kano?

It was barely a technology and mostly a promise. The Atlantic had
been flown nonstop only six years earlier; scheduled passenger
aviation was a novelty of the European capitals, flown in
converted bombers by veterans of the war that had built them;
navigation was by railway line, river bend, and prayer. And yet
every serious government on earth had already concluded that the
promise was strategic. The 1919 Paris Convention — aviation's
first international law — had established the principle that
would govern everything after: every state possesses complete
and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory.
Sovereignty over the air, decided in Paris while the colonial
map was being redrawn at the same conferences, meant that
Africa's skies belonged, in law, to the empires that held its
soil. The Chicago Convention of 1944, which replaced Paris and
still governs, kept the sovereignty principle intact; what
changed in 1960 was merely the flag to which Nigerian air
sovereignty attached. The deep grammar of aviation governance —
territorial sovereignty, national registries, state
responsibility for oversight — was written in and for a world of
strong states. It has never been revised for the world of weak
ones, and a large fraction of this book can be read as the
account of what happens when a state-shaped legal architecture
is inherited by a state still assembling itself: the
responsibilities are sovereign, immediate, and non-delegable;
the capacities are colonial residue. International aviation law
assigns Nigeria the duties of France without asking whether the
assignment is funded. Nobody designed that mismatch. Everybody
in this book lives inside it.

The panorama also fixes the era's honest baseline: aviation in
1925 was mortally dangerous everywhere. Imperial Airways' own
loss rates would horrify a modern budget carrier; the American
airmail service consumed pilots at a rate that made its name a
byword; the profession's culture of workaround-heroism —
celebrated then, lethal always — was global. What distinguished
the next century was not that some countries started safe and
others dangerous; everyone started dangerous. It is that some
polities built, decade by decade, the institutional machinery
that converted a stunt into a utility — and the building was
cumulative, unglamorous, and constant. The divergence between a
2 a.m. maintenance hangar in Atlanta and one in Lagos in 1995
was manufactured, year by year, by choices this book will
watch being made. In 1925, over Kano, the ledger still stood
at zero everywhere. That is worth holding, against every
temptation to read what follows as destiny.

## The comparison worth making

It is tempting to treat all of this as simply the standard colonial
inheritance, common to the continent and therefore explanatory of nothing.
The temptation should be resisted, because the variation within the common
inheritance is where the lessons live.

Consider Kenya, whose settler colony produced an aviation culture oriented
around private flying and the Nairobi hub, and whose post-independence
carrier, Kenya Airways, would eventually be part-privatized into a
partnership with KLM — the first African flag carrier to submit itself to
that kind of external discipline. Consider Ethiopia, never colonized, which
in 1945 did something remarkable: Emperor Haile Selassie contracted with
the American airline TWA to build and manage Ethiopian Air Lines as a
commercial enterprise, with an explicit mandate to train Ethiopians into
every cockpit and every management office — an arrangement that fused
foreign expertise with national ownership and, crucially, kept the airline
outside the patronage economy of the imperial court. Ethiopian Airlines has
been consistently profitable, consistently safe by African standards, and
continuously respected for three-quarters of a century, through revolution,
Marxist dictatorship, famine, and war. Its founding bargain — sovereignty
over symbolism, competence over control — is the precise inversion of the
bargain Nigeria would strike, and the divergence between the two carriers
is as close to a controlled experiment as institutional history offers.

And consider India, where aviation arrived under the same British empire
but where an indigenous commercial class got into the cockpit early: J.R.D.
Tata flew the mail from Karachi to Bombay in 1932 and built Tata Airlines
into a disciplined private company long before independence. When India
nationalized it into Air India, it nationalized a functioning airline with
an internal culture — and even so, politicization nearly destroyed it over
the following decades. Nigeria, whose colonial economy had reserved
aviation's commanding heights for British capital, had no Tata to
nationalize. It would build its flag carrier from a colonial branch office,
under political pressure to Nigerianize faster than it could train, in a
state whose own institutions were three years old. The wonder, in
retrospect, is not that Nigerian aviation struggled; it is that it worked
as well as it did, for as long as it did.

## The Kano crossroads

Between the wars, Kano became something no other Nigerian city has
been before or since: a genuine node of the world's traffic. The
trans-African routes of the 1930s — Imperial Airways' England–
Africa services, the French lines working across the Sahara, the
Italian connections to their East African possessions — funneled
through a small set of viable Sahelian stops, and Kano, with its
established aerodrome, its rest house, its fuel stocks, and its
colonial administrative depth, captured the traffic. The
passenger lists of the Kano rest house in 1936 read like a
gazette of the imperial world in motion: colonial governors and
mining engineers, newspaper correspondents heading for
Abyssinian wars, aristocrats on shooting itineraries, the
occasional royal. For the city's traders and the emirate's
administration, the aerodrome became an economic fact — provisioning
contracts, employment, the beginnings of a local aviation service
economy — and for the city's imagination it became something
larger. Kano had been a terminus of the desert for a millennium;
the caravanserai now had a runway. Northern Nigerian memory
retains this era with a particularity that southern accounts of
Nigerian aviation history often miss: aviation was not introduced
to the north as a Lagos phenomenon that traveled up-country. It
arrived in the north first, embedded itself in the north's
oldest commercial city, and the north's relationship with flight
— the Hajj traffic above all — developed its own lineage, its
own expectations, and eventually its own tragedies, at Kano in
1973, at Jeddah in 1991, at Kano again in 2002. When this book's
later chapters find the northern establishment aboard the
aircraft of catastrophe — the Sultan on 053, the emirs' sons on
the Hajj charters — the density is not coincidence. It is the
century-old geography of who, in Nigeria, has needed the sky.

The crossroads era also planted Nigeria's first aviation
economics lesson, promptly forgotten and relearned expensively
ever after: hub traffic is rented, never owned. Kano's position
depended entirely on the technology of the moment — aircraft
whose range demanded a Sahelian stop. The long-range airliners
of the postwar years overflew the Sahel without stopping;
Kano's transit traffic evaporated within a decade; and the
city's aviation economy contracted to its organic base, the
Hajj and the north's own demand. Nothing about the loss was
avoidable — technology moves — but the pattern (a windfall
position mistaken for a permanent endowment, no investment made
in the underlying system while the rent flowed) would repeat at
national scale with oil, and at sectoral scale with every
bilateral route concession Nigeria later treated as a birthright
rather than a competitive position. Chapter 17's hub question —
whether Lagos can capture the twenty-first century's West
African traffic — is Kano's question, returned after a hundred
years, with the same answer waiting: the traffic goes where the
system is, and stays exactly as long as the system deserves it.

## The decade of handover

The 1950s, Nigerian aviation's decade of transition, rewards a closer
look than the sweep of this chapter has yet allowed, because the
texture of the handover explains the hollowness Chapter 2 inherits.

Constitutional decolonization ran on a timetable that accelerated
past everyone's plans — self-government for the regions in 1957,
independence visible by 1958, achieved in 1960. Institutions that had
assumed a generation to "Nigerianize" were given five years. In
aviation the arithmetic was brutal and specific. A pilot's progression
from cadetship to airline command took, in the systems of the day,
roughly a decade; an aircraft maintenance engineer's licensing ladder,
comparably long; an airworthiness surveyor — the regulator's core
professional, the person who inspects the inspectors — longer still,
because the role presumes years of industry experience before the
oversight training begins. There was no Nigerian cohort in the
pipeline in 1950 because the pipeline had been closed to Nigerians;
there could therefore be no Nigerian cohort emerging from it by 1960
regardless of goodwill, money, or talent. The new state faced the
standard postcolonial menu: retain expatriates (politically corrosive,
financially costly, and self-liquidating as careers pulled them home);
hire international agencies and contractors (the ICAO technical-
assistance route that built Zaria); or promote the available Nigerians
faster than the ladders were designed to bear and hope experience
would catch up with authority. Nigeria, like most of its peers, did
all three at once, in proportions set by budget crises rather than
strategy.

The oversight function fared worst in this scramble, for the reason
this book has already made structural: it was invisible. Political
attention concentrated ferociously on the visible transfers — the
first Nigerian captain (celebrated in the press like a boxing title),
the first Nigerian station manager, the airline's ownership itself,
bought out from BOAC and Elder Dempster by 1961 so that the flag
would be sole and clean. The Department of Civil Aviation's
expatriate surveyors, meanwhile, ran down their contracts and were
replaced slowly, partially, or nominally; the UK's Air Registration
Board stopped being the de facto airworthiness authority for
Nigerian-registered aircraft without any equivalent capability being
built in Lagos at matching depth. Nobody decided this in a memo. It
happened the way invisible things happen — by the sum of attention
paid elsewhere — and it set the pattern this book will trace to 2006:
in Nigerian aviation, operational capability (pilots, engineers,
airlines) always Nigerianized faster and better than oversight
capability (surveyors, inspectors, investigators), because operations
had glamour, constituency, and a wage market, and oversight had none
of the three. States, like people, staff what they love.

## What was planted

By Independence Day — 1 October 1960, when a green-and-white flag rose over
a stadium in Lagos and the Nigeria Airways fleet performed a ceremonial
flypast — the deep structure of the next sixty years was largely in place,
though no one could see it.

The physical inheritance was real: runways at Kano, Lagos, Port Harcourt,
and a dozen lesser fields; a cadre of trained ground personnel; membership,
via Britain, in the postwar international system codified at Chicago in
1944, which Nigeria would join in its own right in 1961. The institutional
inheritance was hollower than it looked. Regulation had been an imported
service, now withdrawn. The airline was three parts symbol to one part
system. The network faced outward. And aviation's political meaning — as
spectacle, as sovereignty, as the state showing itself to its people from
above — had been established by the RAF in 1925 and embraced without
revision by the nationalists who replaced them.

None of this made disaster inevitable. Inheritance is not destiny;
Ethiopia's story alone proves that the institutions of flight can be built
deliberately, even in poverty, when a state decides to want them. But
inheritances set the default, and defaults govern whenever attention
lapses. Nigeria began its independent aviation history with defaults set
by an empire that had needed runways, not institutions — and with a new
political class that needed, above all, a flag in the sky. The next
chapter is about that flag: how brightly it flew in the boom years, and
what was quietly not being built beneath it while everyone watched it
wave.
