# Chapter 16 — Rebuilding Trust

The reform agenda for Nigerian aviation fits on one page. It has fit
on one page for thirty years. The page is not the problem.

Every element of what follows has appeared in some ministerial
roadmap, ICAO audit response, or national aviation policy since the
1990s; several elements exist in law today. This chapter's purpose is
therefore not discovery but specification — each item stated with its
mechanism, its cost class, its owner, and its predictable enemy,
because an agenda without named enemies is a communiqué. And it opens,
deliberately, with the era's great cautionary tale, the project that
consumed a decade of the ministry's attention while the page went
unexecuted.

## The parable of Nigeria Air

In 2018, at the Farnborough Air Show — the industry's global stage —
the Nigerian government unveiled a livery, a name, and a promise:
Nigeria Air, the flag carrier reborn, fifteen years after Nigeria
Airways' liquidation. What followed instead of an airline was five
years of procurement opera: suspensions, relaunches, a contested
partnership with Ethiopian Airlines (the mirror of Chapter 15,
summoned now as vendor), court challenges from domestic carriers, a
parliamentary investigation — and, in 2023, days before a change of
government, a ceremonial "launch" at Abuja featuring an Ethiopian
Airlines 737 temporarily repainted in Nigeria Air colors, flown in
for the cameras and flown home after them. The House of
Representatives committee's verdict was unsparing; the incoming
administration shelved the project; the livery survives as a
photograph.

The episode earns its place here not as farce but as diagnosis. A
state with a plateaued regulator, an unfinished investigative
reform, dry fire tenders on its audit findings, and a training
college losing instructors chose to spend its scarcest resources —
ministerial attention, political capital, credibility — on the one
aviation asset with no safety yield whatsoever: the flag. It is
Chapter 2's founding error, re-committed in high definition sixty
years later, by officials with every chapter of the intervening
history available to them. The lesson for everything below is about
the *order of operations*: symbols are what functioning systems earn
last, not what aspiring ones buy first. The reader should apply a
simple test to every future Nigerian aviation announcement — does
this spend attention on the system, or on its portrait? — and the
rest of this chapter is confined, item by item, to the system.

## The agenda

**1. Finish the investigator.** The NSIB has the statute; give it the
substance: a statutory publication clock (interim statement within
thirty days, published docket within a year, on the public web);
a recommendation-tracking register, also public, in which every
addressee must answer within ninety days — accepted, funded, dated,
or refused with reasons; and a just-culture provision shielding
safety statements from routine prosecution and discipline. Cost
class: trivial. Owner: NSIB board, National Assembly for the shield.
Enemy: the blame system of Chapter 14, i.e., everyone with something
pending. This is the agenda's keystone — it is cheap, it compounds,
and every other item's honesty depends on it.

**2. Ring-fence the regulator — again, permanently.** The 2006
settlement (statutory levies, tenure) has been eroded at the margins
by treasury consolidation drives and appointment politics. Restore
and entrench: levies outside the consolidated fund by constitutional-
grade drafting; DG appointment from a professionally qualified
shortlist with staggered board tenure; and NCAA technical salaries
benchmarked to the market that actually bids for its inspectors —
the Gulf carriers and ICAO, not the civil service scale. Cost class:
moderate, self-funding from existing levies. Enemy: every future
minister of aviation, structurally; the item exists to outlive them.

**3. Fill the trucks, run the drills.** Chapter 13's package,
verbatim: ARFF substance (agent stocks, pump maintenance, access
planning) audited *unannounced* at every certified airport; one
full-scale mass-casualty exercise per international airport per
year, NEMA and hospitals in the loop, results published; a national
SAR coordination center with a tested contact tree; manifest
integrity enforced digitally; and a family-assistance act on the
American model. Cost class: pocket change against any terminal
project. Owner: FAAN, NEMA, NCAA jointly — which is the enemy:
jointness. Assign a single accountable coordinator by statute.

**4. Keep the fleet young by keeping capital cheap.** Full Cape Town
Convention performance — the practice, not the ratification:
repossession timelines guaranteed through a designated commercial
court list, registry operations at world standard, and the treaty's
compliance discount thereby recaptured for every Nigerian lessee.
Add transparent, reviewable slot and route allocation so that
carriers compete on operations rather than access. Cost class:
administrative. Enemy: the litigation culture and every debtor with
a grounded asset; the payoff — measured in basis points on every
aircraft financing — is the cheapest fleet-safety money available.

**5. Consolidate without cartelizing.** The post-reform market's
thin capitalization (Chapter 12's residual risk) argues for higher
AOC capital floors and honest mergers — but paired with SAATM (the
Single African Air Transport Market) implementation, so that
consolidation's pricing power is disciplined by continental
competition rather than by a regulator who will lose that fight.
Owner: NCAA, ministry, AU machinery. Enemy: the consolidated
carriers themselves, five years hence.

**6. Bring the military inside the airworthiness perimeter.** The
unfinished chapter from Ejigbo to Attahiru: an independent military
airworthiness authority on the UK MAA model — inside the defence
establishment but outside the operating chain of command — with
published mishap summaries as its minimum transparency floor. Cost
class: modest. Enemy: the deepest in the book, the informational
sovereignty of the barracks; realistic sequencing starts with the
transparency floor and builds. No Nigerian aviation reform is
complete while the state's own aircraft answer to nobody.

**7. Rebuild the pipeline.** Zaria (NCAT) refunded and re-equipped
against a dated fleet-and-simulator plan; instructor pay unhooked
from the civil service scale; and a national cadetship financing
scheme — the airlines' training levy matched against guaranteed
bonds — so that the pilot pipeline stops depending on family wealth
and diaspora remittance. Cost class: the agenda's largest, still
small against one year of fuel subsidy. Enemy: nobody — which,
Chapter 2 taught, is exactly why it starves; pipelines have no
constituency until they are empty.

**8. Publish everything.** The cross-cutting item: AOC registers,
audit outcomes, ARFF inspection results, recommendation registers,
delay statistics — machine-readable, on the web, by default.
Transparency is the only regulator that works weekends. Cost class:
negligible. Enemy: reflex.

## The precondition

The agenda's items are cheap severally and achievable jointly; the
binding constraint, as every chapter since the Sultan's funeral has
argued, is the absence of a constituency to demand them between
funerals. So the last item is the political one, and it cannot be
delegated to the ministry it disciplines:

**9. Manufacture the constituency.** Concretely: an annual, statutory
*State of Aviation Safety* report to the National Assembly, debated
in public session, with the NCAA, NSIB, FAAN, and NAMA heads on the
record — importing, on a fixed calendar, the scrutiny that currently
arrives only by catastrophe; a standing consumer-and-victims'
advocate with docket standing before the agencies (the
institutionalized descendant of the Loyola parents); and continued,
deliberate coupling to the external witnesses — ICAO, FAA, EU, IOSA
— whose audits remain, until domestic demand matures, the system's
rented spine. Interest must be built where grief once stood. That
is what "rebuilding trust" means in operational terms: not a
communications strategy, but the construction of permanent,
self-interested watchers.

## Reflection

Skeptics will note, correctly, that Nigeria has seen agendas before.
The rebuttal is the book's own Part II: the 2006 settlement was
exactly such an agenda, enacted under compulsion, and it worked —
measurably, quickly, for years. The question was never capability;
it was whether the enactment energy could be found without the
coffins. This chapter's wager is that specification helps: that an
agenda with owners, costs, clocks, and named enemies is harder to
dissolve into communiqué than a roadmap of aspirations. The page,
as promised, remains one page. What stands between the page and
the sky is not knowledge, and never was. It is the arrangement of
power — and arrangements, this book's whole history testifies, are
the one thing Nigerians have repeatedly proven able to change,
when they finally decide the price of not changing them. What that
decision would be worth across the coming half-century — the
largest aviation growth market left on earth — is the final
chapter's subject.
