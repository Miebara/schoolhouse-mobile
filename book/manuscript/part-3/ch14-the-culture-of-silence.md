# Chapter 14 — The Culture of Silence

Aviation became the safest way to move because it became the most
honest industry on earth — not the most virtuous; the most honest.

Somewhere in Lagos, as this book is written, a woman in her sixties
keeps a file. It holds a boarding-pass stub, a laminated photograph, a
death certificate whose cause-of-death line reads "air crash," and
three decades of correspondence — with an airline that no longer
exists, a ministry that has been renamed twice, and an investigation
bureau that has been renamed once — asking one question the file has
never answered: *why*. She is a composite, assembled from the
documented histories of the bereaved of Ejigbo, the lagoon, Lisa, and
Kano; and her file is the Nigerian aviation archive in miniature —
grief fully documented, causation forever pending. What she was owed,
under a treaty her country signed before she was born, was a report.
This chapter is about the reports: the ones never written, the ones
never published, the ones published a decade late — and about the
discovery, which is the whole secret of modern aviation, that a
society's safety is a function of what it permits itself to say.

## The honesty machine

Begin with how the system is supposed to work, because its design is
genuinely one of civilization's quiet masterpieces. Annex 13 to the
Chicago Convention obliges every state to investigate accidents on its
territory — with a stated purpose the treaty text takes care to make
exclusive: prevention, "not to apportion blame or liability." From
that single sentence unfolds the entire apparatus this book has
invoked chapter by chapter: independent investigation bodies, walled
off from regulators (who may be implicated) and prosecutors (whose
presence silences witnesses); protected safety information — cockpit
voice recordings, crew statements — shielded from routine legal use so
that the system can afford to be told the truth; public findings and
recommendations, addressed to the world; and, layered above the
accident system, the voluntary-reporting regimes through which pilots,
controllers, and engineers confess hazards and near-misses by the
hundred thousand, un-punished, so that the system can see its failures
*before* the wreckage stage. The scholars of the field — Sidney Dekker
foremost — call the underlying bargain "just culture": the deal in
which the professional trades candor for protection from blame, and
frontline honesty becomes the system's sensory organ. Every element
is counterintuitive to the punitive instinct, and every element is
empirically vindicated: the industries and countries that punish
error into hiding fly blind; the ones that pay for truth with
immunity see everything, and their accident curves fall for decades.

Aviation safety, in other words, is not primarily a technology. It is
an epistemology with a budget — a set of institutions for making
failure speakable, fast, in public, protected. Which is why the state
of a country's accident reports is not a bureaucratic detail but the
X-ray of its whole safety system, and why this chapter's ledger
matters more than any single crash in Part II.

## The Nigerian ledger

Compile it, as the source appendix to this book does in full, and the
Nigerian record sorts into three strata.

**The unwritten and the buried.** The colonial habit (Chapter 1:
investigation as internal correspondence) passed seamlessly to the
successor state. The 1973 Kano disaster: no public Nigerian account.
Ejigbo, 1992, and the military line generally, through 2021: boards
of inquiry convened, findings sealed, permanently. For roughly the
first four decades of independence, the default disposition of a
Nigerian accident investigation was the file, not the press.

**The late.** The AIB era brought real investigations — and a tempo
that defeated their purpose. ADC 086's report: years. Bellview's
inconclusive findings: most of a decade. Dana 992: five years, for an
accident essentially understood in months. Recall what a report is
for — grounding fleets, rewriting procedures, informing the identical
aircraft still flying — and the arithmetic of delay becomes a safety
deficit in itself: a five-year report protects nobody for five years.
Annex 13's twelve-month norm is missed worldwide, but the strong
systems bridge the gap with loud interim publication; the Nigerian
practice bridged it with silence, which converted even eventual
publication into archaeology.

**The reformed and partial present.** The NSIB era shows the curve
bending: reports now appear, faster, in public, on a website, in
Annex 13 format — a genuine institutional achievement that this
chapter's argument requires acknowledging as such. What has bent
less is everything around the reports: recommendations issued but
untracked (the follow-up function — who verified that the
recommended windshear system was installed? — remains the system's
weakest organ); no national voluntary-reporting scheme with credible
protections at scale; and a legal environment in which the just-
culture bargain has never been statutorily struck, leaving every
frontline confession discoverable, and therefore unspoken.

The consequences of the ledger compound in two directions. Downward,
into operations: a system that does not publish its failures cannot
standardize their lessons — each airline, each crew, learns only its
own near-misses, and the national curriculum of error (windshear
decision-making, fuel-system anomalies, the recurring departure-
into-CB pattern of 2005–06) is taught only by catastrophe, the most
expensive instructor there is. Upward, into the civic atmosphere:
where official causation is absent or late, explanation is supplied
by the market — rumor, sabotage theories, spiritual attribution,
elite conspiracy — and each unexplained disaster deposits another
stratum of the reflexive distrust that Nigerian institutions now
meet everywhere, including when they tell the truth. The woman with
the file does not believe the government's account of anything. Why
would she? On the subject she knows best, it never gave her one.

## What silence costs, itemized

Because "transparency" tends to float free as a virtue-word, pin
the costs of its absence to cases already in evidence, item by
item.

**Operational cost.** The windshear pattern: Sosoliso (December
2005) and ADC 053 (October 2006) were, at the level of decision
architecture, the *same accident* — a crew continuing into
convective weather that forecasts and eyes had flagged, under
schedule and prestige pressure. Ten months separated them. In a
system with a live reporting culture and rapid interim
publication, December's lesson — its dispatch-pressure findings,
its go-around arithmetic — would have been circulating in every
crew room, regulator bulletin, and simulator syllabus by the
following rainy season. The interval was instead filled with
mourning and process, and the lesson arrived in Sokoto's
funeral procession. Silence's operational cost is measured in
repetitions, and Nigerian history bills them by the hundred
seats.

**Commercial cost.** Unexplained accidents price into everything:
insurance loading (Chapter 12's flat country premium is, in
part, an opacity premium — underwriters charge for what they
cannot read); lease terms; bilateral negotiations (the EU list
and FAA categories weigh investigative performance directly);
and passenger demand itself, which after every unexplained
Nigerian event has swung toward road transport — whose fatality
rate per kilometer exceeds aviation's by orders of magnitude.
Opacity, by displacing travelers onto the highways, kills
statistically even when the fleet flies clean.

**Civic cost.** The conspiracy ecology. Every unpublished report
franchises an alternative explanation: sabotage theories after
1992 (a military transport, a hated regime); spiritual and
political readings of 2005's clustered grief; durable rumors
around every VIP death in the appendix. These are not Nigerian
credulity; they are rational inference under informational
famine — where the state explains nothing, the explanation
market clears at whatever price rumor sets. The cost compounds
politically: a public trained by aviation (and elections, and
budgets) to assume concealment extends the assumption to the
occasions when the state tells the truth, and the state,
finding truth-telling unrewarded, economizes on it further. The
spiral is general, but aviation — high-salience, technically
adjudicable, internationally witnessed — is where a state could
most cheaply reverse it, which is what makes the NSIB's
publication rate a civic indicator far beyond its sector.

## The blame reflex

Why is silence so stable? The economical answer — secrecy protects
the guilty — is true and insufficient. The deeper mechanism is that
the *alternative* to the honesty machine is not neutrality but an
older, intuitive system: blame. In the blame system, an accident is
a crime scene; the question is not "what failed" but "who pays";
information is ammunition, so every actor — pilot, controller,
airline, ministry — rationally armors up and shuts up; and the
inquiry's real function is the allocation of punishment and the
protection of hierarchy. Most of human history has run on the blame
system; most institutions revert to it under stress; and a state
built on patronage runs on it structurally, because in a patronage
system responsibility *is* the currency and its assignment is far
too valuable to delegate to engineers. Nigeria's silences, read this
way, were not lapses of administration but the blame system
defending itself: every unpublished report was a favor owed, a
faction shielded, a hierarchy preserved. The French experience shows
the tension is universal — the BEA's investigations have collided
for decades with the juge d'instruction's criminal machinery, each
Concorde and Rio–Paris file contested between the epistemology of
prevention and the epistemology of guilt. The difference is that
France's collision happens in public, between strong institutions,
and the safety findings emerge regardless. Where the blame system
meets no institutional counterparty, it simply wins, and the file
stays closed.

The NTSB's public docket — the comparative benchmark here — is best
understood as the blame reflex's engineered defeat: hearings on
camera, dockets of raw evidence online, findings barred from use as
legal proof of liability, the entire process designed on the wager
that a democracy can be trusted to watch its failures being
understood. The wager has paid for eight decades. It is also,
Chapter 10 noted, the cheapest reassurance technology ever
invented: the state that explains publicly does not need to ask to
be believed.

## The recorder and the courtroom

One frontier of the silence problem is sharp enough for its own
section: the legal status of the safety data itself.

The cockpit voice recorder is the honesty machine's most invasive
instrument — a microphone on working professionals' last (or
worst) minutes — and its bargain is correspondingly explicit in
international standards: the recording exists for prevention, its
use for discipline or prosecution restricted, precisely because
crews who expect their words to convict them will fly silent
cockpits and lobby against every extension of monitoring. The
bargain's modern descendants go further: flight-data monitoring
programs, in which airlines continuously download and analyze
their own operations for exceedances, run on negotiated
protections — de-identified data, no punishment for what the
program reveals — that turn every routine flight into safety
telemetry. Where the protections are credible (the American,
European, and best Asian systems), the data flows in oceans and
the accident curves show it. Where they are not, the programs
exist on paper and the telemetry is curated.

Nigeria's practice sits, characteristically, between statute and
habit. The modern Civil Aviation Acts import Annex 13's
protective language; the NSIB's regulations gesture at
non-punitive reporting. But the surrounding legal ecology — police
investigations opened reflexively after crashes, recorders and
documents seized as exhibits, the periodic ministerial demand
that "those responsible be brought to book" before the
investigation has established what the book is — has never been
reconciled to the bargain, and no Nigerian court has yet built
the case law that would make a crew's protected statement
actually protected. The result is a system whose frontline
knows the protections are unlitigated, and prices its candor
accordingly. The fix is legislative and small: an explicit
statutory shield for safety information, with narrow, defined
carve-outs for willful misconduct — the "just culture" statute
that New Zealand, Denmark, and others have modeled for decades.
It would cost nothing, offend no budget, and require only that
the state formally renounce a power it has historically used
mainly to perform severity after failing to prevent harm. That
renunciation — power traded for information — is the entire
philosophy of aviation safety in one clause, which is perhaps
why it has waited so long in Abuja: it is the clause by which a
state admits what kind of state it intends to be.

## Reflection

Of all the patterns in Part III, this one costs least to break and
resists longest — because it is not a budget line but a reflex, and
reflexes are governance's deepest layer. Yet the record offers its
usual Nigerian consolation: the reflex has been broken before, in
this very country, in this very industry. The 2006 settlement that
separated the AIB from its regulator, and the NSIB statute that
deepened it, were exactly such breaks — the blame system conceding
territory to the honesty machine, under funerary compulsion, one
statute at a time. What remains is the last mile: the just-culture
law that makes candor safe, the recommendation-tracking that makes
findings binding in fact, the publication tempo that makes truth
arrive while it can still protect someone. None of it requires a
naira the system does not have. It requires only the thing the
woman with the file has been requesting for thirty years, which is
that the state address its citizens as an adult addresses adults:
with an account of what happened.

Part III's anatomy is complete: a regulator whose strength is
architectural and therefore maintainable; an economics that sorts
risk toward weakness and can be re-sorted; a response deficit
purchasable for pocket change; a silence that costs nothing to end
but pride. Four patterns, none of them fate. What a country might
actually do with that knowledge — and what four other countries
have done with it — is Part IV.
