# Chapter 13 — When Help Doesn't Come

The first disaster belongs to physics. The second belongs to the state.

Line up the scenes this book has already witnessed. Ejigbo, 1992:
residents wading into a canal with ropes while the military organizes a
cordon. The lagoon, 1996: fishermen poling through debris the state
has not yet located. Lisa village, 2005: a Boeing missing for twelve
hours within thirty kilometers of Africa's largest metropolis, found
by farmers. Port Harcourt, 2005: fire tenders arriving at a burning,
survivable wreck without water. Iju-Ishaga, 2012: ten thousand
volunteers and no crane. Five different decades' worth of aircraft,
weather, and causation — and one constant, so reliable it amounts to a
national signature: in the window between impact and institutional
arrival, the Nigerian state is represented at its own disasters by
whoever happens to live nearby.

Emergency response is the purest test of state capacity that exists,
because it cannot be faked, deferred, or performed selectively. It is
the state's whole apparatus — communications, logistics, command,
medicine, honesty — summoned without notice, against a clock,
before witnesses. Elections can be managed and statistics can be
launched; the golden hour cannot. And this is why the present chapter,
though it contains the book's least complicated failures, carries a
disproportionate share of its argument: response capacity is where
institutional quality becomes, literally, countable — in the persons
alive at the scene and dead at the morgue.

## The physics of the second disaster

The survivability literature — built over decades from accident
forensics, most systematically by the American and European
investigation agencies — establishes a fact that reorganizes how one
reads Part II: a large fraction of transport-aircraft accidents are
survivable or partially survivable at impact. The image of the crash
as instant annihilation describes some events (the lagoon; Lisa) but
not the class. Runway excursions, undershoots like Sosoliso,
post-impact fires like Kano and Iju-Ishaga: in these, the impact
distributes survival unevenly through the cabin, and the toll is then
*negotiated* — between fire and extinguishing agent, between trauma
and the golden hour, between the trapped and whoever arrives with
cutting equipment. The negotiation is over in roughly the time it
takes to read this chapter. ICAO's Annex 14 ARFF standards — the
three-minute response, the rated agent quantities, by airport
category — are that negotiation's opening bid, calibrated to fuel-fire
physics: an intact cabin resists a kerosene fire for a survivable
interval measured in low single-digit minutes. Every minute of
response delay is therefore not a linear cost but a threshold one:
the difference between two minutes and ten is not eight minutes, it
is everyone the fire reaches second.

Against that physics, tabulate the Nigerian record as Part II
documented it: response by neighbors (always first, never equipped);
airport fire services delayed, under-agented, or — the Sosoliso
finding that became a national synecdoche — dry; no drilled interface
between airport services and municipal ones; hospitals receiving mass
burns without burn units, mass trauma without triage protocols,
learning of the influx from the influx. Then note the detail that
turns description into indictment: every airport in this ledger was
*certified*, on paper, at an ARFF category adequate to its traffic.
The vocabulary of the international standard had been purchased —
the trucks procured (some), the categories declared, the boxes
inked — without the substance: agent stocks, pump maintenance,
access-road planning, and above all drills, the cheap, dull,
repeated rehearsals that convert equipment into capability. The
pattern is Chapter 8's "certification as costume," and response is
where the costume meets its only honest audience.

## The golden hour and the hospital at the end of it

Trauma medicine's organizing insight — formalized from battlefield
data and etched into every developed emergency system — is that
survival after severe injury decays on a curve whose steepest
segment is the first hour. The "golden hour" is shorthand, not
scripture; the literature debates its edges. But the operational
consequence is not debated: an emergency system is a race
machine, and its performance is the *product* of its stages —
detection, dispatch, access, extrication, stabilization,
transport, and definitive care. A failure at any stage multiplies
through the rest. This is why the Nigerian record reads as it
does: each disaster in Part II failed at *every* stage
simultaneously, and multiplication of failures is not addition.
Twelve hours to find (Bellview) times no water at the fire
(Sosoliso) times no crane under the slab (Dana) times — the stage
this section adds — nowhere adequate to take the saved.

The hospital is the response chain's terminal link and its least
examined. Severe burns — aviation's signature injury — are among
the most resource-intensive conditions in all of medicine:
dedicated units, sterile environments, skin banking,
round-the-clock specialist teams. Nigeria's burn capacity through
the crisis decades was a handful of units, concentrated in two or
three cities, chronically short of everything; mass-burn events
like Kano or Sosoliso would have overwhelmed the national
capability even had every ambulance flown. The Sosoliso survivors'
medical odysseys — treatment abroad, funded by family means and
public appeal — were not exceptions but the system stating its
capacity honestly: the definitive-care stage for the injuries its
airports were certified to produce did not exist in-country. A
state that certifies an international airport is, by that
signature, promising a trauma system scaled to the airport's worst
day. The promise is written nowhere on the certificate, which is
precisely why this chapter exists.

## Why response is different — and why that is hopeful

Prevention, this book has argued, is institutionally expensive: it
requires decades of regulatory architecture, capital-market depth,
political constituency. Response is cheap, and the cheapness is the
chapter's central, practical claim. A competent national aviation
response posture consists of: functioning ARFF at a dozen airports
(equipment already largely owned; the deficit is maintenance and
drills); a written, exercised mass-casualty doctrine binding the
alphabet agencies (FAAN, NEMA, military, police, states, hospitals)
into one command structure; a search-and-rescue coordination center
with current contact trees and a duty officer who answers; manifest
integrity, so the state knows within the hour who it is looking for;
and a family-assistance protocol, so that the bereaved meet a
process instead of a scrum. The entire package prices in the low
millions of dollars and one uncomfortable annual exercise per
airport. It is, by an order of magnitude, the highest-yield
investment in this book — and it is the *most reform-tractable*,
because unlike regulatory independence it threatens no one's rents:
there is no constituency for the fire truck being empty. Its
neglect is not capture but pure entropy — the unglamorous decay of
things nobody powerful ever needs until the day everybody does.

The comparative record confirms both the cheapness and the yield.
The Hudson River ditching of 2009 — 155 alive — is remembered as a
pilot's masterpiece, which it was; it was equally a *response*
masterpiece: ferries alongside in under four minutes, a harbor's
crews drilled for exactly this, a city whose emergency doctrine had
been rehearsed into reflex. Japan's airport disaster exercises are
municipal events with casts of hundreds. South Africa's ARFF
services — under a state with fiscal troubles Nigerians would
recognize — have maintained drill discipline that has repeatedly
held real events to their survivable size. None of these is a
wealth story; they are habit stories. Rehearsal is a technology
available at every income level — which is precisely what makes its
absence diagnostic. A state that will not drill is publishing its
discount rate on its citizens' marginal lives, and no development
statistic contradicts it so efficiently.

## The anatomy of a drill

Because this chapter's remedy is "rehearse," the rehearsal itself
should be described, so that no reader files it under ceremony.

A full-scale airport emergency exercise — ICAO requires one at
least every two years at certified international airports, with
partial exercises between — is a production: a scripted "crash"
with a declared aircraft type and passenger load; volunteer
casualties by the hundred, moulaged and tagged with injury cards;
the alarm cascading in real time through the actual chains — tower
to ARFF, ARFF to municipal fire, medical dispatch, police
perimeter, NEMA, the airline's own emergency center, the
hospitals' mass-casualty plans; and, crucially, umpires with
stopwatches and clipboards logging every interval against the
plan: minutes to first foam, to triage established, to first
ambulance departure, to family-information center open. The
exercise's product is the *after-action report* — the honest,
written ledger of what failed (the gate that wouldn't open, the
frequency nobody monitored, the hospital that never picked up) —
and the corrective log that the next exercise re-tests. Run
seriously, the cycle converts an emergency plan from a binder into
a reflex, and its cost is a weekend, some paint, and institutional
humility.

The humility is the actual price, and the reason drills decay.
An exercise that logs failures embarrasses agencies in front of
each other; a state whose operating currency is face will
therefore drift, without ever deciding to, toward the exercise as
pageant — the pre-briefed scenario, the fire out in record time,
the group photograph, the report unwritten. Veterans of Nigerian
airport exercises across the decades describe exactly that drift,
punctuated by earnest post-disaster seasons (after 2005, after
2012) when the drills briefly acquired teeth. The diagnostic for
any reader who ever inspects one is simple: ask to see the last
after-action report and the corrective log. A drill without a
published failure list is a play. And a country's willingness to
let its agencies fail visibly, on schedule, in rehearsal — small
failures, cheaply, before witnesses — is the exact institutional
muscle whose absence this book has been anatomizing at
catastrophe scale since Jeddah. The drill is the honesty machine
in miniature. That is why it works, and that is why it is
resisted.

## The morgue and the manifest

One more institution belongs to the second disaster, though it is
rarely counted in it: the machinery of identity and grief. The
recurring Nigerian sequence — approximate manifests, unidentified
remains, mass burial, compensation labyrinths, no family-assistance
function — is not a footnote to response failure but its longest-
lasting component, because it is the part the survivors inhabit for
years. The comparative benchmark here is legislative: after TWA 800
(1996), the United States passed the Aviation Disaster Family
Assistance Act, statutorily obliging carrier and state to a named,
rehearsed apparatus — notification, care, information, remains,
records — with the NTSB as guarantor. The act cost almost nothing
and changed the civic meaning of American aviation disasters: the
state's competence now extends to grief. Nigeria's bereaved, from
Jeddah to Iju-Ishaga, met instead the unreformed core of the old
state — opacity, improvisation, and the quiet assumption that
mourning is the family's problem. Chapter 8 argued that 2005's
political rupture came when grief organized; a state with a family-
assistance act metabolizes that energy into procedure. A state
without one leaves it to compound into the civic distrust that
Chapter 14 will measure. How a country treats the manifest is how
it treats the citizen: as a record worth keeping, or a crowd worth
estimating.

## Reflection

There is a version of this book's argument that counsels despair:
if crashes are symptoms of institutional depth, and institutional
depth takes generations, then the dying continues until history is
fixed. This chapter is the rebuttal. The second disaster — the dry
tender, the twelve-hour search, the undrilled airport — is not
deep. It is shallow, cheap, and separable: a country can rescue
competently long before it regulates competently, and every life so
saved is banked regardless of the deeper reforms' fate. If Nigerian
public life took a single sentence from this book into the next
budget cycle, it should be this one: fill the trucks, write the
doctrine, run the drill. The dead of Port Harcourt were failed
twice, and the second failure was the one any state, that year,
could have declined.

What a state does after its disasters, though, extends beyond the
scene. The trucks answer for the first hours; the *report* answers
for the years — and the report, in Nigeria, has its own history of
not arriving. That silence, the most self-inflicted of all the
patterns, is the last of the anatomy, and the next chapter's
subject.
