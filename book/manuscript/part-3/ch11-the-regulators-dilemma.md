# PART III — THE PATTERNS

# Chapter 11 — The Regulator's Dilemma

No regulator anywhere is loved. The good ones settle for being feared
by the right people.

Consider a thought experiment assembled from the sworn recollections of
men who lived it. It is a Friday in Lagos, some year between 1988 and
2005 — the year hardly matters, which is the point. An inspector of the
civil aviation authority, trained at Zaria and seasoned abroad, walks
the ramp and finds an aircraft he cannot legally release: a defect
beyond deferral, a certification lapsed, a crew out of time. He grounds
it. The airline's owner — a retired general, a party financier, a
friend of the minister; the portfolio varies, the physics does not —
makes two telephone calls. By Monday, one of three things has happened.
The finding has been overruled from above the inspector's head; or the
inspector has been reassigned to cargo statistics in a distant state;
or — the commonest outcome, because systems teach faster than they
punish — nothing has happened at all, because the inspector, knowing
the first two outcomes by heart, wrote the finding narrowly enough to
be waivable. The aircraft flies Monday evening. Nothing about the
weekend appears in any record ICAO will ever audit. Multiply that
weekend by twenty years and you have Part II.

The question this chapter asks is not why individual Nigerians failed
to stop such weekends — many tried; some paid for it — but why the
*institution* was built so that trying was heroic. Heroism, this book
said at Chapter 3, is the signature of a system spending margins it
does not own. A regulatory system that requires brave inspectors is
already broken; the entire art of institutional design is to make the
safe finding the cheap one.

## The capture problem, stated honestly

Begin with the universal, because Nigerian exceptionalism is the enemy
of understanding. Regulatory capture — the process by which an agency
comes to serve the industry or interests it oversees — is not an
African pathology; it is the default tendency of all regulation,
described by George Stigler in 1971 in an American context and
observable from Tokyo to Toulouse. The regulated party is concentrated,
informed, and permanently interested; the public is diffuse, ignorant
of the technical stakes, and interested only after disasters. The
asymmetry never sleeps. The FAA itself — this book's recurring
benchmark — was revealed by the 737 MAX catastrophes of 2018–19 to have
delegated core certification judgments to Boeing under a
decades-refined doctrine of "designee" oversight, with results that
killed 346 people and humiliated American aviation governance before
Congress and the world. Capture is universal. What varies — the only
thing that varies, and therefore the thing this chapter is about — is
the *architecture that prices it*: how expensive it is, for whom, and
who is positioned to notice.

The Nigerian regulator's history is best read as a natural experiment
in that pricing, run in three regimes.

**Regime one: the directorate (1960–1999).** Oversight as a ministry
department — no legal personality, no budget of its own, its head a
civil servant removable at pleasure, its inspectors on scales that a
first officer's salary tripled. In this architecture capture required
no corruption, no conspiracy, not even a telephone call: the regulator
was simply *inside* the political structure whose interests conflicted
with its function. The waiver culture was not a distortion of the
design; it was the design, expressing itself. The directorate era's
audit trail is Part II, chapters 4 through 8.

**Regime two: the NCAA on paper (1999–2006).** The Authority created
as a formally distinct body, but funded by allocation, led at
pleasure, and legally toothless against the ministry — autonomy as
letterhead. The era's record (Kano 2002, the 2005 catastrophes)
demonstrates the central finding of the comparative literature:
*formal* independence without fiscal and tenure independence is
capture with better stationery. Political scientists who study
regulatory design — the Carpenter–Moss school — emphasize precisely
this distinction between de jure and de facto independence, and
Nigeria 1999–2006 is as clean a demonstration as the literature owns.

**Regime three: the 2006 architecture.** Statutory levies, tenured
DG, published rulebook, imported witnesses (ICAO, FAA, IOSA). Same
country, same industry, same salary differentials, same generals'
telephones — and a measurably different output within two years. The
experiment's power lies in everything it held constant: no cultural
transformation occurred between 2005 and 2008; no generation turned
over; the officials were substantially the same people. The
architecture changed, and the weekend in the thought experiment
started ending differently, because the Monday overrule now had to
survive a paper trail auditable from Montreal, a DG who could not be
quietly reassigned, and a levy stream the minister could not pinch.

## The anatomy of the architecture

What exactly did the 2006 design change? Four load-bearing members,
each worth specifying because each is exportable — and each, as the
plateau of Chapter 10 showed, reversible.

**Funding by formula.** The pre-2006 regulator begged annually; the
post-2006 regulator collects a statutory percentage of every ticket.
The distinction is not about amounts but about *who can punish*. An
agency funded by allocation can be disciplined by the budget process
for unwelcome diligence — the starvation is deniable, attributed to
austerity, and Chapter 3 showed the mechanism at national scale. An
agency funded by formula can only be disciplined by changing the law
in public. Capture must come out of hiding to operate, and capture's
whole comparative advantage is operating in the dark.

**Tenure at the top.** The Act's protections for the
Director-General's term converted the office from a posting into a
position — occupiable by someone planning beyond the next reshuffle,
and therefore worth the reputational investment of an internationally
credentialed professional. The Demuren tenure was the provision's
proof: eight years, spanning two presidencies, terminable only at
visible political cost. Compare the pre-reform pattern of NCAA and
directorate heads — a churn nearly matching Nigeria Airways' CEOs —
and the point requires no elaboration. Institutions cannot remember
through heads that change annually.

**Transparency as a ratchet.** Publication — of regulations, AOC
registries, directives, audit outcomes — performs a function subtler
than accountability theater: it creates *third-party evidence* that
makes quiet reversal expensive. A waiver granted against a published
rule is a documented act; a waiver granted against an internal
circular is a conversation. The reform's genius was less in writing
better rules than in moving the rulebook where everyone, including
foreign auditors and opposing counsel, could see it.

**Imported witnesses.** The deliberate coupling to external audit —
ICAO's USOAP, the FAA's IASA, mandatory IOSA for carriers — gave the
domestic regulator what it had never had: a constituency for its own
integrity that outranked its domestic predators. A minister can
overrule an inspector; he cannot overrule the FAA's category
assessment, and after 2010 every act of domestic interference carried
a legible price tag denominated in Category 1 — direct flights,
lease rates, insurance premia, national prestige. The reform, in
effect, took the country's aviation reputation hostage against its
own political class. It is the single most sophisticated
institutional maneuver in this book, and its author-era officials
understood it exactly.

## The dilemma that remains

If the architecture works, why does Chapter 10 end on a plateau? Because
the 2006 design solved the regulator's *independence* problem and left
its *constituency* problem standing — and constituency, in the long
run, is the binding constraint.

An agency can be structured to withstand pressure, but structures are
maintained by politics: levies can be raided or "consolidated" (as
Nigerian treasury reforms have periodically threatened); tenure
provisions protect only officials someone was willing to appoint;
external audits sample the system every few years, not every Friday
ramp check. Between audits and after champions, what holds a regulator
upright is demand — some organized interest whose welfare depends on
the agency's rigor. The FAA, capture scandals and all, sits inside a
lattice of such demand: airlines whose business model is safety
reputation, insurers, unions, a tort bar, congressional staff,
journalists on permanent beat. Each is self-interested; their vectors
sum to a field that keeps the institution roughly vertical between
its own disasters. The Nigerian field is thinner by an order of
magnitude: carriers too marginal to monetize safety reputation, a
tort system too slow to price negligence, no permanent aviation
press corps, unions focused on wages, and a flying elite whose exit
options (Chapter 6's secession) drain the demand pool exactly where
influence concentrates. The 2006 reform substituted foreign demand
for the missing domestic kind — brilliantly, but rentals are not
ownership. The regulator's dilemma, in its mature form, is this: it
is strong enough now to resist any single predator, and too alone to
survive a coalition of neglect.

The chapter's comparative coda compresses the point into two
sentences. South Africa's CAA — Africa's other Category 1 veteran —
has oscillated with its state's broader institutional weather,
proving that architecture inherits its climate. Ethiopia's oversight
has held for decades under every climate, because the state itself
treats the aviation system as a crown asset — proving that where
the sovereign is the constituency, no other is needed; and leaving
open, for Part IV, the question of what substitutes when, as in
Nigeria, the sovereign's attention is itself the scarcest input.

## Reflection

The men and women of the thought experiment deserve the chapter's
last word. Every system this book has anatomized was, on its worst
Friday, staffed by some inspector who wrote the finding anyway,
knowing the Monday that awaited it. The 2006 reform did not create
Nigerian regulatory virtue; it stopped taxing it at confiscatory
rates. That is all institutional design ever does — arrange matters
so that ordinary conscientiousness, not heroism, is sufficient — and
it is enough, and the difference it makes is countable in the
crashes that stopped. The question the plateau poses is whether a
country that built such architecture once, under compulsion, can
learn to maintain it without compulsion's return. The rest of Part
III examines the forces — economic, operational, cultural — that
will supply the compulsion if the answer is no.
