# Part Two — The Arguments That Feel Airtight

---

## 8. The God Who Cannot Lose

### *Confirmation Bias*

Dobelli calls confirmation bias the father of all thinking errors and gives it
two chapters, which is more than he gives anything else. His definition: the
tendency to interpret new information so that it becomes compatible with what we
already believe. Not to ignore contrary evidence — that would be too crude and
too easy to catch — but to *process* it. Disconfirming evidence gets a harder
look, a demand for better sourcing, a search for the flaw. Confirming evidence
gets waved through.

His illustration is the "special case." A man believes in a business theory. A
counterexample appears. He does not abandon the theory; he classifies the
counterexample as a special case, an exception, a matter of poor execution. Do
this a few dozen times and you have a theory that has survived contact with
everything and learned nothing from any of it. Dobelli's remedy is the one Karl
Popper insisted on: go looking for the observation that would kill your belief,
and if you cannot name one, you do not have a belief about the world. You have
a commitment.

Now do the exercise honestly. **What would have to happen for you to conclude
that God does not exist?**

Take the ordinary machinery of religious life and watch it absorb everything.
You pray for the sick woman. Three outcomes are possible. She recovers: prayer
answered, God is good. She dies: God had a better plan, she is at peace, he
answered differently than we asked. She lingers: God is testing us, teaching
patience, working in ways we cannot see. Every possible outcome confirms. A
hypothesis compatible with all outcomes has told you nothing about which outcome
to expect, and a claim that forbids nothing predicts nothing.

The same structure covers the whole system. The universe looks designed:
evidence of God. The universe looks like 13.8 billion years of indifferent
physics with life on one damp rock: God's ways are higher than ours. Scripture
is confirmed by an archaeological find: proof. Scripture is contradicted by
archaeology — no evidence for the Exodus, no evidence for a conquest of Canaan
matching Joshua, Nazareth barely a village: the Bible is not a history textbook
and we read it differently now. The faithful prosper: blessing. The faithful
suffer: refinement, or Job, or the mystery of suffering. Prayer studies show an
effect: vindication. Prayer studies show nothing: God will not be put to the
test.

Notice that each individual move is *reasonable-sounding*. That is the whole
danger. Nobody in this process ever says "I will ignore the evidence." Every
step is a locally plausible reinterpretation, and the cumulative result is a
belief that cannot be touched by any conceivable observation. Theology is, in
substantial part, the professionalisation of this activity: an enormous,
brilliant, intricate literature whose function is the generation of special
cases. Two thousand years of the best minds in Europe working on the problem of
why an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good being permits a child to die of
bone cancer, and the output is not a resolution but a genre — theodicy — with a
shelf of competing answers, none of which anyone finds convincing except in
retrospect.

There is a diagnostic here that costs you nothing and is very hard to escape.
Ask a believer what evidence would change their mind. The answers cluster into
three types. The first is a demand that could never be met and is not seriously
offered ("if God appeared and told me himself"). The second is an admission that
nothing would ("my faith is not based on evidence, so evidence cannot remove
it"). The third, and by far the most common, is a change of subject. Try it on
yourself first, in writing. If you cannot complete the sentence "I would give
this up if ______," then whatever your belief is doing for you, it is not
tracking reality — and it never was, which means the evidence you thought you
had was never the reason.

Then run the mirror test, because it is the fastest way to feel the bias from the
inside. You already disbelieve in thousands of gods. Consider the claims you
dismiss without effort: Joseph Smith's golden plates, Muhammad's night journey
on a winged steed, the miracles attested at Hindu temples where statues drink
milk, the healings claimed at Lourdes if you are Protestant, the healings
claimed by Pentecostals if you are Catholic. Each of these has sincere
eyewitnesses, transformed lives, and a community that will tell you the
testimonies are overwhelming. You apply a perfectly calibrated scepticism to all
of it — you ask about verification, base rates, motivated witnesses, later
embellishment. You already own the toolkit. Confirmation bias is what switches
it off for exactly one tradition, and the tradition it switches off for is,
with near-perfect reliability, the one you were born into. That correlation is
the single most damning fact about religious belief, and it has nothing to do
with theology. If the truth of a metaphysical claim were what convinced people,
conviction would not track birthplace to four decimal places.

**In conclusion:** write down, today, what would falsify your belief. If you can
name it, you are doing something respectable, and you should go and look. If you
cannot name it — if every outcome would have confirmed, and every disconfirming
fact turns out to be a special case — then you are not holding a belief that
could be true or false. You are holding a belief that cannot lose, which is the
one kind guaranteed to teach you nothing.
