## 27. Coda: The Dignity of Not Knowing

Dobelli closes his book by declining to offer a system. There is no unified theory
at the end, no ten principles, no promise that you will now think clearly. What he
offers instead is a via negativa: the best you can do is remove errors, and the
person who removes errors will be right more often than the person who accumulates
insights. Subtraction, not addition.

That is the shape of what I have argued, and it is worth stating what it does and
does not amount to.

Twenty-three chapters described mechanisms. Not one of them was invented for this
argument — every single one was discovered by researchers studying people reasoning
about coins, dice, share prices, medical tests, personality profiles, line lengths
and salesmen. Asch's subjects were not thinking about God. Nor were Aronson's, or
Festinger's before he walked into that living room in Chicago, or Forer's students,
or the nurses in the Hofling study, or the people who press disconnected crossing
buttons in Manhattan. The errors were catalogued in a secular laboratory and then
turned out to fit religious belief with a precision that ought to be startling.

That fit is the argument. Every load-bearing pillar of belief corresponds to a
documented failure mode: the fine-tuning argument to a selection effect, the
testimonies to a sampling artefact, the signs to a pattern-detector with no error
report, the personal experience to the Forer effect and the introspection illusion,
the certainty in the pew to Asch, the impossibility of leaving to sunk costs and
group boundaries, the survival of the belief through disconfirmation to Festinger.
And the great tell, the one I keep returning to: what a person believes about the
origin of the universe is predicted almost perfectly by where they were born. No
process that tracked evidence could produce that.

So the honest position is not agnostic paralysis, and it is not certainty either.
It is this: **a claim with no evidence in its favour, whose every argument is a
known fallacy, and whose persistence is fully explained without it being true, is
a claim you should decline.** You already do this effortlessly for ten thousand
other gods. The only thing I have asked is that you apply the standard you already
own to the one case where you were trained not to.

And then there is what remains, which is less than the pulpit promises and more
than its critics usually admit.

You do not know why there is something rather than nothing. Neither does anyone
else, and the people who say they do are, on inspection, renaming the question.
You do not know what consciousness is. You do not know whether the question "why
is there a universe" is even well-formed, or whether it is grammatically fine and
refers to nothing, like asking what is north of the North Pole. These are real
mysteries, and the correct response to them is not a story. It is attention.

What I want to argue for, at the end, is that this position has a dignity that the
alternative does not.

Consider what you are actually being offered when you are offered certainty. You
are offered relief from the pressure of not knowing — the pressure of Chapter 6 —
and the price is that you must stop looking. Every mystery gets a name, every
death gets a purpose, every coincidence gets an author, and none of it is checked,
and the whole structure is maintained by a room full of people who have each
privately agreed not to ask. That is not a small price. It is the surrender of the
one faculty that has ever told you anything true about the world.

The alternative asks more of you and gives you less, and it has one advantage. It
is honest. You get to say "I don't know" and mean it, which is the beginning of
every piece of knowledge anyone has ever acquired. You get a universe that is
under no obligation to be about you, and is therefore genuinely surprising — 13.8
billion years, a hundred billion galaxies, and a species that has existed for an
eyeblink and has already worked out the age of the light. You get your Sunday
mornings, which is roughly two and a half years of waking life, returned to you.
And you get to love the people in your life without believing you will see them
again, which is a harder and less consoling love and, I think, a larger one:
this is the only time there is, and they are not a rehearsal.

None of that is a consolation for what the pew gives, and I have not pretended
otherwise. Chapter 24 was written in earnest. If you leave, go and build the
substitutes, and take the ritual and the rest and the music with you, and be
kinder about the people still in the building than they may be about you — they are
running the same hardware you are, and the only difference between you and them is
that you got a look at the schematics.

Dobelli's whole project rests on one premise: these errors are not signs of a
defective mind, they are the standard equipment of a normal one. That is the note I
want to end on, because it is the difference between an argument and an insult.
Nobody in that congregation is a fool. They are running a pattern-detector tuned
for a world of predators, an agency-detector that cannot be switched off, a
conformity mechanism that overrides the evidence of their own eyes, and a memory
that edits itself to match today's conclusions. So are you. So am I, and I wrote
this.

You cannot remove the equipment. You can decline to spend two hours a week in a
building purpose-built to exercise it.

**In conclusion — the whole book in one instruction:** stop asking whether it is
comforting, whether everyone believes it, whether it makes a good story, whether
you feel it, or whether you have too much invested to reconsider. Ask what would
show it to be false, and then go and look. If the answer is that nothing could,
you have not been given a truth about the universe.

You have been given a very good reason to stay in your seat.
