# The Art of Doubting Clearly

### How Ordinary Thinking Errors Build a God and Fill a Church

---

## The thesis, stated plainly at the start

I am going to argue two things in this book, and it is worth separating them
now, because they are usually confused.

**The first is about belief.** Every serious argument offered for the existence
of God is a known, named, catalogued reasoning error — not a bad argument in
some vague sense, but a specific error that psychologists have already
described, given a name, and demonstrated in the laboratory on subjects who
were reasoning about dice, stock prices, and medical tests. The fine-tuning
argument is the swimmer's body illusion. The testimony of the healed is
survivorship bias. The sign you received on the worst day of your life is the
clustering illusion. The certainty you feel in the third verse of the closing
hymn is social proof plus synchrony. Pascal's Wager is a framing trick built on
loss aversion, and any competent salesperson would recognise it.

**The second is about attendance.** A church service is not a neutral room in
which the question of God gets discussed. It is, structurally, a machine for
amplifying exactly the errors that produced the belief in the first place: mass
agreement, unquestionable authority, costly commitment, group boundaries,
reciprocity, and an atmosphere in which the one sentence you cannot say out
loud is "I don't think this is true." You can be a thoughtful person and go to
church. But you cannot think clearly about whether God exists inside a building
designed to make that question unaskable, any more than you can get a second
opinion from the person selling you the first one.

Note what I am *not* claiming. I am not claiming to have proved that no god
exists. You cannot prove that, and anyone who tells you they have is selling
you the mirror image of what the pulpit sells. What I claim is narrower and
harder to escape: the arguments have no evidential force, and the persistence
of the belief is fully explained by mechanisms we can observe in people
reasoning about things that have nothing to do with God. When a belief's
existence is entirely accounted for by the way brains malfunction, and its
supporting arguments are entirely accounted for by the way arguments
malfunction, the honest position is not agnostic paralysis. It is
disbelief — the same disbelief you already extend, without agonising, to every
one of the ten thousand gods you were not raised with.

## A note on method

This book is built on the frame of Rolf Dobelli's *The Art of Thinking
Clearly*. Dobelli's project was modest in ambition and ruthless in execution:
ninety-nine short chapters, one systematic thinking error each, no unified
theory, no promise of self-improvement — just a catalogue of the ways a normal,
intelligent, well-meaning mind goes wrong in predictable directions.

Three of his commitments drive this book.

**First: the errors are systematic, not random.** A random error scatters. A
systematic error points. If you and I and a billion other people are all
running the same buggy pattern-detector, we will not arrive at a billion
different mistakes. We will converge — on gods, on omens, on the conviction
that the universe is watching us specifically. Convergence is what believers
offer as evidence ("so many people, across so many cultures, cannot all be
wrong"). Dobelli's frame turns that evidence inside out. Universal agreement
about something invisible is precisely what a shared cognitive defect looks
like from the inside.

**Second: knowing about a bias does not disarm it.** Dobelli is explicit that
he still falls for these errors after writing a book about them. This is why
Part Four exists, and why Chapter 25 is aimed at unbelievers. A book that
diagnosed everyone else's biases and exempted its own author would be
performing the very error it describes.

**Third: the remedy is negative.** Dobelli's recurring practical advice is not
"think harder" or "believe better." It is: remove the error, subtract the
nonsense, avoid the situations that reliably fool you. That is the shape of the
conclusion here too. I am not going to offer you a replacement metaphysics.
I am going to suggest that you stop doing one specific thing on Sunday
mornings, and then notice what remains.

## A note on manners

I have tried to write this the way I would want an argument against something I
love to be written.

So: no sneering. The people in the pews are not stupid. The reasoning errors in
this book are not the errors of stupid people — the whole point of the
research, from Asch's conformity experiments to Kahneman's work on intuition,
is that these failures are strongest in normal, intelligent, socially competent
adults. Several of the errors documented here get *worse* with education,
because education gives you better tools for defending a conclusion you already
hold. Nobody reading this is being called a fool.

Also: no pretending the other side has nothing. Chapter 24 is a serious attempt
to state what a congregation actually gives people — mortality handled in
company, a moral vocabulary, casseroles when your mother dies, three hundred
strangers singing in the same key — and to admit that most people who leave
lose those things and do not replace them. Dobelli has a chapter on
*alternative blindness*, the failure to compare an option against the real
alternatives rather than against nothing. An argument for leaving church that
does not deal with what leaving costs is alternative blindness wearing a
lab coat.

And finally: if someone you love believes, this book is not ammunition. The
biases described here are not moral failings and pointing them out in an
argument at dinner will convince no one; Chapter 21, on cognitive dissonance,
explains precisely why frontal attacks on a costly belief make it stronger. The
only mind you can reliably work on is your own. That is where I suggest you
start, and it is where this book ends.
