## 22. How a Congregation Kills a Question

### *Groupthink, Social Loafing, and Strategic Misrepresentation*

Dobelli's chapter on groupthink uses the Bay of Pigs. In 1961 a group of
exceptionally intelligent, experienced men — Kennedy's cabinet, several of them
the most capable people in American public life — unanimously approved an invasion
plan so poor that each of them, individually, could have identified its flaws.
Irving Janis's analysis found the mechanism: in a cohesive group with a strong
preference for consensus, dissent becomes socially costly, so members suppress
their private doubts, and each one's silence is read by the others as agreement.
The group converges on a decision that no individual member fully believed in.

Dobelli's key observation is that groupthink is not produced by stupidity or by
weak character. It is produced by *cohesion*. The tighter the group and the more
its members value belonging, the more reliably it happens — which means a warm,
loving, mutually supportive community is not protected against this. It is the
ideal substrate for it.

A congregation is the most cohesive group most people will ever belong to, and it
has an additional feature that Kennedy's cabinet lacked: doubt is not merely
socially awkward, it is *classified as a spiritual defect*. Consider the
vocabulary available for the state of thinking a claim might be false. Weak faith.
Backsliding. Struggling. A season of doubt. Under attack. Every available term
frames the epistemic act — considering that a proposition may be untrue — as a
malfunction to be prayed through, and the prescribed treatment is more prayer,
more attendance, more scripture, more accountability. There is no vocabulary in
which "I examined this and it does not hold up" is a legitimate outcome rather
than a symptom.

So the doubt does not surface. And here is where Chapter 16's pluralistic
ignorance becomes an active machine rather than a passive state. Each person
privately has questions. Each person looks around at four hundred faces, none of
which are showing questions, and concludes that they alone are failing. So they
keep quiet — not out of cowardice, but because raising it would be, in their
understanding, a confession of personal spiritual weakness. And their silence
becomes part of the wall of certainty that persuades the person next to them to
stay quiet too. Four hundred people, each doubting, each certain they are the only
one, each producing the evidence that silences the rest.

This is why the honest conversations happen where they do: in the car park, at
2 a.m., one-to-one, prefaced with *don't tell anyone I said this*. Almost everyone
who has spent time in a religious community has had at least one of those
conversations, and been startled by it, and then never followed it up. The one
place the question cannot be asked is the one place the community actually
assembles.

Dobelli's social loafing adds a mechanism most people miss. In groups, individual
effort declines measurably — the classic rope-pulling studies found that eight
people pull with less than eight times the force of one — and, importantly,
*responsibility* diffuses along with effort. Nobody in a large group feels
personally accountable for verifying anything, because surely someone has. The
theologians have dealt with this. The scholars have looked into it. Two thousand
years of great minds cannot all have been wrong. This is intellectual
outsourcing to a committee that no member has ever audited, and it is precisely
Dobelli's chauffeur knowledge (Chapter 15) at institutional scale: everyone
relaying, nobody checking. Ask the next twenty believers you meet what the best
evidence for the resurrection is, and how they know the gospels were written when
they claim. The answers will be thin, and the confidence will not be, and that
gap is where social loafing lives.

Then strategic misrepresentation, which Dobelli defines as the systematic
exaggeration that occurs whenever the payoff depends on a performance rather than
on accuracy. His examples are job interviews and budget proposals, where the
incentive is to overstate and everyone does it, and the sober bidder loses.

A congregation runs a continuous market in the performance of belief. Status is
allocated for visible conviction: the testimony, the raised hands, the answered
prayer publicly reported, the confident declaration of what God is doing. Nothing
is allocated for calibration. Nobody has ever gained standing in a church by
saying "I think that was probably coincidence" or "my prayer wasn't answered and
I don't know why." So the reported evidence base of the entire community is
generated by a process that rewards overstatement and punishes accuracy — which
means it is not an evidence base at all. It is a market price, inflated by the
same forces that inflate every other bidding process, and everyone inside it is
reading everyone else's inflated bid as data.

Put the three together and you have an institution with no mechanism whatsoever
for detecting its own error. Doubts are not voiced (groupthink). Verification is
assumed to have happened elsewhere (social loafing). Reported experience is
systematically inflated (strategic misrepresentation). Whatever such a system
converges on, it is not converging by testing. And a system that cannot detect
error will not correct it, no matter how sincere, intelligent or loving its members
are — which is, note, exactly what Janis found about the smartest room in
Washington.

**In conclusion:** find out what the group actually thinks by asking one person at
a time, in private, with the door shut, having first admitted a doubt of your own.
You will be shocked, and the shock is the measurement. Then apply the test to
yourself: is there anything you privately suspect and have never said out loud in
that building? If so, the room is not where you are thinking. It is where you are
performing what you have concluded elsewhere — and everyone around you is doing the
same, for the same reason, and calling the aggregate result unanimity.
