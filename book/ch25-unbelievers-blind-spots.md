## 25. Your Biases Do Not Vanish at the Door

### *The Unbeliever's Blind Spots*

Dobelli says something in his introduction that a book like this one is obliged to
take seriously: he still commits these errors, constantly, after writing a
catalogue of them. Knowing about a bias does not disarm it. Worse — and this is the
finding that should worry anyone who has enjoyed the previous chapters — there is
good evidence for a *bias blind spot*: people who learn about cognitive biases
become better at identifying them in others while their own performance does not
improve. Emily Pronin's work is the reference. Reading a book like this reliably
makes you more confident and not more accurate.

So this chapter is about the errors that operate on the side I am arguing for. It is
not a courtesy. It is the test of whether the previous chapters were an argument or
a weapon.

**Overconfidence and the convert's zeal.** The most obnoxious phase of unbelief
comes about six months after leaving, and it looks exactly like the certainty it
just escaped. If you have just concluded that a two-thousand-year-old tradition
holding a billion people is a pile of cognitive errors, notice what your
probability estimate has just done, and notice that you reached it in a fortnight
of reading. The honest position at the end of this book is not "there is certainly
no God." It is "there is no good evidence for one, the arguments offered are known
fallacies, and I am therefore not going to believe it" — which is a much weaker and
much more defensible claim. Anyone who tells you they have *proved* the
non-existence of God is committing Chapter 15's overconfidence error with the
opposite sign.

**Twaddle in a lab coat.** Chapter 15's test cuts both ways, and a great deal of
confident-sounding secular language fails it. "Science has disproved God" — state
what experiment. "Consciousness is just neurons firing" — the word *just* is doing
work no one has earned; the hard problem is unsolved and pretending otherwise is
bluffing. "The universe came from a quantum fluctuation" — repeated fluently by
people who could not define a quantum field, which is Dobelli's chauffeur
knowledge with a different chauffeur. Popular science journalism is a superb
generator of borrowed certainty, and the fact that the underlying discipline is
sound does not mean your grasp of it is.

**Cherry picking and motivated history.** I have tried to avoid this in the
preceding chapters, and I want to flag the places where the atheist canon does not.
The claim that religion is the primary cause of war does not survive contact with
the actual data — most wars are territorial and dynastic, and the twentieth
century's worst mass killings were carried out by explicitly secular regimes. The
"Galileo was persecuted by anti-science bigots" story is considerably more
complicated than the version in the polemics; so is the history of the Library of
Alexandria, and the alleged flat-earth belief of the medieval church, which is
mostly a nineteenth-century invention. The Christian contribution to the
development of universities, hospitals and the scientific revolution itself is
substantial and gets omitted by exactly the process Chapter 11 described. If you
are going to accuse the pulpit of selecting its evidence, you have to stop doing
it yourself.

**Survivorship bias, running the other way.** Chapter 2 pointed out that testimony
services only hear from the healed. Deconversion communities only hear from the
people who left — and disproportionately from those who left traumatic
high-control groups, because they have the most to say. Reading those forums will
give you the impression that religion is uniformly harmful, and that impression is
generated by the same door policy that produces the impression that prayer works.
The quiet Lutheran who has attended for forty years, been kind, given generously,
raised decent children and never harmed anyone posts nothing anywhere.

**The false-consensus effect, mirrored.** Chapter 18 noted that believers
systematically misjudge unbelievers because their whole social world agrees with
them. Educated secular people do this at least as badly in the other direction.
They are often numerically in the minority worldwide while feeling like the
default, they mistake the loudest fundamentalists for the median believer, and
they have frequently never had a serious conversation with a thoughtful religious
person about why they believe. If your model of the opposition is drawn from
Twitter creationists, you have a caricature.

**Domain dependence.** Dobelli's chapter notes that we apply rigour in one domain
and abandon it in another — the surgeon who is scrupulous in theatre and credulous
about his portfolio. Plenty of people who have correctly dismantled religion go on
to hold beliefs about diet, astrology-adjacent personality systems, crystals,
manifestation, political tribalism, and their own exceptional rationality that
would not survive a single page of Dobelli's tests. Leaving church does not
install a general-purpose scepticism. The vacated need for meaning, certainty and
belonging goes looking for a new supplier, and the market is well stocked with
worse products than Lutheranism.

**The self-serving bias and the real reason.** Dobelli's chapter: we attribute
successes to ourselves and failures to circumstance. Applied here, the
uncomfortable question is whether the arguments in this book are your reasons or
your justifications. A lot of deconversion is driven by something other than
epistemology — a church that treated you badly, a father whose faith you needed to
escape, the desire for a Sunday morning back, or a moral objection you could not
square with the doctrine. Those are not illegitimate. But if the argument came
after the conclusion, you should know that about yourself, because a person who
mistakes their motivated reasoning for pure inquiry has learned nothing from this
book except a better vocabulary.

**And the thing this book cannot do.** Nothing here shows that the universe is
without purpose, that consciousness is fully explained, that we know why there is
something rather than nothing, or that a sophisticated philosophical theism is
false. I have argued that the popular arguments are fallacies and the popular
belief is explained by bias. That is a strong conclusion about *arguments and
believers*. It is a much weaker conclusion about *reality*, and honest atheism
lives in that gap rather than pretending it is closed.

**In conclusion:** apply every chapter of this book to this book. Ask what would
change your mind about *your* position and write it down, exactly as Chapter 8
demanded of the believer. Ask whether your certainty rose faster than your
evidence. Ask whether you have replaced a community that made demands of you with
a comfortable contempt that makes none. The point of Dobelli's project was never to
win arguments with other people. It was to be slightly less wrong yourself, and
that job does not finish when you stop going to church.
