# Chapter 2 — A Flag in the Sky

Emeka — a licensed aircraft engineer whose real name, like all
composite voices in this book, I am withholding by design — joined
Nigeria Airways in 1974, the year the oil money truly arrived. The
way veterans of that era describe their first walk across the Ikeja
apron has a consistency that survives every retelling: the row of
tails in green and white, the flying elephant on the fins, the
sense — overwhelming for a young man from a country fourteen years
old — that this, finally, was what sovereignty looked like parked
on concrete.

Every new nation buys three things first: a flag, an anthem, and an
airline. The flag is cheap, the anthem is free, and the airline is
the most expensive symbol a poor country can own. Nigeria Airways
had been created in 1958 from the dissolving West African
corporation, in transitional partnership with British firms until
it could be fully, gloriously Nigerian — which it was by 1961. Its
route map was a diplomatic statement. Its livery flew to London and,
in the boom years, New York. Its logo was an elephant with wings — a
creature chosen, one assumes, for majesty rather than aerodynamics,
though history would have its joke either way.

It is easy, from the wreckage of what came later, to condescend to
the founders' dream, and this chapter will not. The dream had hard
logic. Fifty-five million people at independence, spread across a
territory twice the size of California with three great population
centers and poor roads between them: Nigeria was a natural aviation
market if anywhere in Africa was. Nor was state ownership
eccentric — in 1960 nearly every airline on earth outside America
belonged to a government. The fork in the road ran elsewhere,
through a question nobody put to a vote: would the state own the
airline as a nation owns an enterprise — disciplined by its books,
the government a shareholder — or as a court owns a treasury,
its jobs part of patronage, its routes part of diplomacy, its
management appointed and removed like ministers?

Ethiopia, the comparison that will not go away, chose the first
model in 1945 and defended it under every subsequent regime.
Nigeria drifted onto the second — not at once, never
irreversibly, but steadily. The drift is this chapter.

## The boom years, from the inside

On paper, the first decades gleamed. The 1960s fleet was modern for
the continent; Nigerian pilots came up through cadetships in
Britain and Canada; Nigerian engineers, Emeka among them, earned
licenses recognized abroad. The airline even survived the civil
war, when airports became targets and the Biafran airlift wrote its
own strange chapter in the night skies over Uli. Then the oil
shocks of 1973 and 1979 transformed the treasury faster than any
society absorbs money well, and the airline expanded with the
national mood: wide-body jets for the intercontinental routes, a
thickening domestic network, over a million passengers a year by
the late seventies, a new Lagos international terminal copied
consciously from Amsterdam's — the most modern in Africa, it was
said at the ribbon-cutting.

The veterans remember the same decade differently. They remember
the arithmetic.

Three numbers tell it. First: by 1980 the airline operated roughly
a dozen distinct aircraft types in a fleet of about thirty — a
maintenance and training nightmare no commercially governed airline
would tolerate, the signature of purchases made as diplomacy and
theater rather than fleet planning. Every additional type meant
separate spares, separate simulators, separate certifications:
complexity bought at boom prices, to be maintained later at bust
prices. Second: management tenure. Across its life the airline ran
through more than twenty chief executives, many lasting months,
each owing his seat to a patron, each freezing his predecessor's
contracts. No maintenance program survives churn like that; churn
is what patronage looks like on an organization chart. Third: the
payroll, which swelled to several times the industry norm per
aircraft — a federal employment program with a flying division. The
individuals were rarely the problem. The arithmetic was. An
airline that exists partly to distribute jobs cannot shrink to its
revenue and cannot say no, and an airline that cannot say no is
accumulating risk somewhere. Usually in the hangar.

What did that feel like at eye level? The senior pilots of the era
describe, with striking uniformity, a private daily calculation
made at the head of the jetway: which of the company's accumulating
compromises would they carry into the air, and which would they
refuse at the cost of a summons to headquarters. The trip flown
with a deferred-defect list that would have grounded the aircraft
on a neighboring registry. The roster torn up overnight because a
VIP charter had commandeered the aircraft. The simulator recurrency
postponed for a budget season. The safety of the national carrier
became, by the late boom, a private subscription service operated
by its professionals against the drift of their employer. When the
exodus began in the 1980s, its first recruits were exactly these
people. Institutions do not only lose their staff to better pay.
They lose them to the exhaustion of winning the same argument ten
thousand times.

## The war in the air, and the first jet grave

Two events of the first decade complicate the pride, and both have
been nearly erased from national memory.

The first is the airlift. During the civil war, the blockaded east
was supplied by a nightly shuttle of chartered and improvised
transports flying blacked-out into a strip of reinforced road at
Uli — church-organized relief on one channel, arms on another,
mercenary and missionary pilots sharing a traffic pattern under
federal guns. The moral history of that operation belongs to other
books. Its institutional deposit belongs here: the war normalized,
at scale and under the highest sanction, precisely the aviation the
rules forbid — unregistered aircraft, false manifests, night
landings on unlicensed strips — and some of that repertoire,
machines and men both, flowed after 1970 into the region's gray
charter markets, which never fully closed. When a funeral charter
falls out of the Lagos sky in 2013 with paperwork that should have
grounded it, the lineage runs back, step by step, to the
exemptions of the war.

The second is the crash of 20 November 1969 — the first great
jet-age loss, and the most completely forgotten disaster in this
book. The airline's flagship VC10, inbound from London in the early
morning, descended into forest short of the Lagos runway and was
destroyed with all 87 aboard. No memorial marks it. No annual
journalism revisits it. No public report was ever issued that an
ordinary Nigerian could read. The war was ending, the state's
attention was elsewhere, and the machinery for public technical
accounting — which Britain would have supplied automatically a
decade earlier — had been repatriated without replacement. What
the VC10 established, at the first full-scale test, was the
national grammar for aviation catastrophe: mourn intensely,
investigate opaquely, publish nothing, memorialize privately,
proceed. Every element of that grammar was in place before the
boom began. The boom merely bought bigger aircraft to apply it to.

## Zaria: the one that worked

Fairness requires this section, because the independence
generation did build one aviation institution on the right model,
and its fate is as instructive as the airline's.

In 1964, with UN and ICAO support, Nigeria opened the Civil
Aviation Training Centre at Zaria — a school for pilots,
controllers, and engineers, technically governed, internationally
supervised, selective. For a generation of African aviation
professionals, "Zaria" carried the weight "Sandhurst" carries in
another profession; its alumni run through every institution in
this book, usually on the creditable side of the ledger. Zaria
proves, in advance, that the lazy explanation for everything that
follows — that Nigeria somehow *couldn't* — is false. Nigeria
could, wherever the design insulated competence from politics: an
entrance exam instead of a patron, a curriculum audited from
Montreal instead of a budget negotiated in Lagos.

And Zaria's later decades — underfunding, obsolete training
aircraft, instructors lost to airline pay, periodic rescue and
renewed decay — prove the theorem's other half. Founding design
protects an institution for a while; only a political constituency
protects it forever. The airline had a constituency: everyone who
could extract from it. The training college had none. Where the
extraction was, the money went. A country's budget is its actual
aviation policy, whatever its gazette says — and the pattern
recurs in every decade of this book: the institutions designed to
be boring produced excellence, and the institutions designed to be
seen produced disaster.

## Kano, January 1973

The Hajj is the largest annually recurring movement of human
beings on earth, and by the early 1970s Nigeria was among its
largest sources. Hajj transport was aviation's strangest market —
a colossal demand spike, weeks long, one direction at a time — and
no airline could keep a fleet sized for it. The universal answer
was the charter: hire aircraft and crews from wherever capacity
could be found, and surge the pilgrims across the Red Sea and
home.

On 22 January 1973, a Boeing 707 chartered from the Jordanian
carrier Alia for the Nigerian operation approached Kano at the end
of a flight from Jeddah, in the poor visibility of the Harmattan
season. The approach went wrong. The aircraft came down hard,
broke, and burned. One hundred and seventy-six people died — at
that moment, the deadliest crash in the history of civil aviation
anywhere on earth.

Sit with that: the world's deadliest crash, Nigerian passengers,
Nigerian soil — and it is barely remembered today, outside the
families and the city that buried the dead in long trenches while
the north mourned through the spring. Part of the amnesia is
general; the 1970s were world aviation's bloodiest decade, and
January's record was surpassed soon enough. But part is specific
and diagnostic. The aircraft was Jordanian, the crew foreign, the
charter arranged through the Hajj apparatus rather than the
airline. In the seams between jurisdictions, accountability bled
out. There was grief. There was no reckoning: no public report
Nigerians could read, no named institutional failure, no reform
with a date on it. The state's operative conclusion, as far as the
record shows, was that a foreign airline had crashed a foreign
plane in bad weather — an act of God with a Jordanian
registration.

The other conclusion was available even then: that Nigeria had
built a system in which the most dangerous flying its citizens did
— the mass charters — was precisely the flying its institutions
oversaw least; and that a state whose citizens board aircraft it
does not oversee has not escaped responsibility, only deferred it.
That conclusion was not drawn. Eighteen years later, at Jeddah, in
an almost perfect echo — chartered aircraft, foreign crew, Hajj
season, dissolved accountability — the deferred bill would arrive
with 261 names on it. Chapter 4 reads it line by line.
Institutions that ignore their first warning are not granted
gentler second ones.

## The elephant, the terminal, the mirror

The emblem earned its afterlife. Nigerians embraced the flying
elephant, teased it, and — as the cancellations mounted through
the 1980s — turned it proverbial: elephants, the market wits
observed, do not fly. The airline died narrated under elephant
metaphors of increasing cruelty, and the emblem survives now in
memorabilia and in the memory of the older diaspora, for whom an
elephant on a Heathrow-bound 707 remains a pride with no available
replacement. When a new national-carrier livery was unveiled at an
English air show in 2018 — a story Chapter 16 tells in full — the
columnists reached for the comparison within hours. Symbols are
not the enemy in this history; nations need them. The enemy is the
sequence. A symbol that crowns a working system compounds its
worth. A symbol procured in place of one becomes its epitaph.

As for the terminal: anyone who transited both Lagos and its
Amsterdam original over the following twenty years received a free
tutorial in the difference between a structure and a system.
Schiphol stayed Schiphol because a dense web of dull institutions
renewed it continuously. Its Lagos twin began dying on opening
day — air conditioning that failed and stayed failed, escalators
that became staircases, generators coughing through the power
cuts — until, by the 1990s, the building was a byword among
travelers and a citation in foreign regulators' files. Same
blueprint, same decade, same equipment; divergent institutions.
Two buildings ran this book's entire argument as an experiment in
concrete, and the terminal was a mirror that foretold: what
happened to the building was about to happen to the airline, the
regulator, and everything else the boom had bought. It required
only that the money stop.

In 1981, it stopped.

## What the flag concealed

One more comparison before the fall, because the 1970s ran a
controlled experiment nobody knew they were running.

In Addis Ababa, Ethiopian Airlines spent the decade doing
unglamorous things: keeping the training academy full, keeping the
maintenance culture intact after its American management contract
ended, banking hard currency. When revolution came in 1974 and a
Marxist junta seized the state — nationalizing everything,
shooting much of the old elite — the Derg left the airline's
management substantially alone, having grasped that it was one of
the only reliable dollar machines the state owned. The airline
flew through terror, famine, and civil war, and made money. The
lesson is uncomfortable precisely because it removes every excuse:
institutional protection is a political choice, available to poor
states, available even to brutal states, the moment a sovereign
decides an institution is worth more functioning than raided.

Nigeria ran the opposite protocol with ten times the money. The
airline was raided continuously — jobs, tickets, contracts,
appointments — and above it, the referee barely existed: civil
aviation oversight was a directorate inside a ministry, budgeted
as an afterthought, structurally incapable of saying no to a
national carrier that outranked it in every currency Nigerian
life recognized. The 1970s made this arrangement affordable,
because oil paid for the slack. Boom-era institutions are never
tested by booms. The test comes when the money stops, and the
deferred maintenance and deferred training and deferred
regulation must all be paid at once, from an empty account.

The flag kept flying through all of it. That was the trouble with
the flag: it flew whether or not anything beneath it worked. A
symbol that cannot fail is the perfect roof under which everything
else can — and in the 1980s, under that roof, everything else
did. That long fall, one unordered spare part at a time, is the
next chapter, and the reason the crashes of Part II should be
read not as lightning strikes but as invoices.
