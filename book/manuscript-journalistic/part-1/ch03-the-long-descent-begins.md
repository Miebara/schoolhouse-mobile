# Chapter 3 — The Long Descent Begins

By the end of the 1980s, you could read the decade in a single
exhibit on the Ikeja apron: a Boeing 747 in flying-elephant livery
that no longer flew and never would again. The engineers called it
the Christmas tree. A hydraulic pump had gone to one sister ship,
instruments to another, seats and valves and slides fanned out
across the fleet — an aircraft kept legally dead so that its organs
could keep the schedule alive. Controlled parts-borrowing is a
legitimate practice at a well-run airline. As a way of life, it is
a family burning the furniture through a winter: rational each cold
night, catastrophic as a strategy, and diagnostic — always — of an
income that has stopped matching a way of living.

Nigeria's income had stopped matching everything. The oil price
broke in 1981 and collapsed in 1986; federal revenue fell by more
than half in real terms; the naira lost over nine-tenths of its
dollar value across the decade. For most industries this was
catastrophe. For aviation it was something closer to an extinction
event, because aviation is a dollar industry that happens to earn
local currency. Aircraft are priced in dollars. So are spare
parts, insurance, simulator time, manufacturer support, even the
charts in the cockpit. A Nigerian airline in 1988 sold naira
tickets at politically supervised fares and owed dollars to
everyone who kept its aircraft legal — and every devaluation
silently multiplied the real cost of airworthiness. No coup
plotter ever listed the exchange rate among his grievances. But
when Part II asks why the aircraft of the 1990s were old, the
spares scarce, the training deferred, the insurance threadbare —
the answer begins here, with the least dramatic villain in this
book and one of the most lethal.

## Where the shortage was sent

The money stopped; politics then decided where the shortage would
be felt. The politics of the 1980s were military rule — a coup in
1983, another in 1985 — and the juntas did three specific things
to aviation beyond the general damage they did to everything.

They finished off management continuity: chief executives rotated
on the coup cycle, each severing his predecessor's procurement
pipeline — the dull artery through which an airline's future
flows — every two or three years. An airline can survive poverty
with patience; Ethiopian proved it that same decade with less
money and a worse war. No airline survives the abolition of its
own memory.

They normalized the exemption. Soldiers do not queue, and regimes
born of emergency govern by exception: this flight will carry whom
it is told; this finding will be waived because the general's
schedule requires it. Each exemption was small. Their sum acquired
a name that professionals of the era still use — *the waiver
culture* — a world in which every rule was understood as the
opening position in a negotiation with power. Part II is largely
the record of its price, and the post-2006 reform, when it
finally came, was at bottom nothing more exotic than the
repricing of waivers. Safety rules work only when they are
unnegotiable; a rule negotiable for a general is negotiable,
eventually, for anyone with his phone number.

And they starved the referee. Civil aviation oversight remained a
directorate inside the transport ministry — inspectors on civil
service pay a first officer tripled, training abroad an early
casualty of the dollar famine, independence a legal fiction under
governments in which the airline's chairman outranked the
director of civil aviation in every register Nigerian life
recognizes. By decade's end, Nigeria possessed a regulator the way
the Christmas-tree 747 possessed engines: the housing was there,
and everyone continued to speak as if the function were too.

## The license factory

In 1986 came structural adjustment — devaluation, deregulation,
privatization — and with it, from 1988, an aviation consequence
nobody had quite intended: the Nigerian private airline. The flag
carrier was a hemorrhage and a monopoly; private capital would
supply what it no longer could. Licenses flowed, and by the early
1990s a swarm of carriers had risen — Okada Air, Kabo, ADC, and
dozens more. Some were serious enterprises. The public, offered an
alternative to an airline that canceled by habit, voted with its
feet; domestic aviation grew even as the economy shrank.

But look closely at the license itself, because its terms shaped
the fleet Part II buries. In mature systems, an air operator's
certificate is among the hardest documents in commerce — years of
proving out manuals, maintenance, training, finances. The Nigerian
AOC of the late 1980s was substantially a political-commercial
document: nominal capital requirements, cursory financial review,
technical examination by the starved directorate. The applicants
ranged from genuine aviation people to traders diversifying out
of shipping — to fronts for whom an airline was chiefly a claim on
foreign exchange at the official rate. The register filled with
entities for which the airline was not the business but the
instrument, and an instrument's maintenance budget is exactly as
deep as its usefulness.

America had run this experiment first, and the comparison is exact
enough to be useful. US deregulation in 1978 also unleashed entry
and marginal operators, and the predicted carnage never came —
because deregulation of the market was paired with reinforcement
of the referee. The FAA's inspectorate, the NTSB, the courts, the
insurers: the institutional immune system was mature before the
pathogens multiplied. Nigeria ran the same experiment with the
immune system switched off. The 1990s would supply the laboratory
results.

## The two fleets

No feature of the era exposes its priorities like the one fleet
that stayed perfect. While the national carrier ate itself and the
navigation aids died, the Presidential Air Fleet — the executive
aircraft of the head of state and his court — was renewed,
expanded, and maintained to standards the civil system could only
remember. Its budget lived outside scrutiny; its hangars were the
best in the country. Treat it, throughout this book, as the
control group: proof, parked in Abuja, that the Nigerian state
knew perfectly well how to keep aircraft airworthy. Airworthiness
followed power, not capability. Every deficiency elsewhere was an
allocation, not a limitation.

Below the presidential stratum, the private era produced its own
aristocracy, and its emblem was Okada Air — the fleet of the Benin
businessman Gabriel Igbinedion, which grew by the early 1990s into
the largest private collection of airliners in Africa, assembled
at scrap prices from the world's retiring BAC 1-11s. Okada was a
genuine achievement — it moved millions the flag carrier stranded
— and simultaneously the era's pathologies incorporated: a fleet
chosen by purchase price, maintenance litigated between ambition
and cash flow, a proprietor whose political weight was itself a
regulatory fact. It faded through the mid-1990s under the
arithmetic Chapter 12 formalizes — old aircraft age expensively —
and its hulks joined the fringes of Nigerian airports, parted out
and sun-bleached. A country's ramps are its financial statements
printed in aluminum. By 1995 the Nigerian ramp read: assets
acquired 1965–1985; depreciation catastrophic; provision for
renewal, none.

## What flying felt like

Statistics anesthetize; millions of Nigerians carry the texture.
You bought a ticket, but the ticket was the beginning of a
negotiation. Boarding could be a physical competition in which the
manifest was reconciled to reality afterward, if at all — hold
that word, *manifest*, because in 1996 and 2005 the country would
discover at the worst possible moments that knowing who is aboard
a Nigerian airliner was not a routine certainty but an
investigative task. A society learns its record-keeping in
ordinary time and pays for it in extraordinary time.

And yet — the half of the truth that dignifies the era — the
system worked daily, in the sense that mattered: the aircraft
mostly arrived. The pilots were often superb precisely because
the environment demanded it. Musa — the retired Lagos-area
controller of this book's composite cast — describes the tower
side of that virtuosity: hand-flown approaches into airports
whose landing systems had died years before, position pictures
built from paper strips and pilots' honesty, radio calls relayed
plane-to-plane across the gaps in coverage. A generation of
aviators and controllers became virtuosos of the workaround, and
the virtuosity was magnificent and corrosive at once: it kept the
schedule alive, and by keeping the schedule alive it hid the
decay from the public and the treasury. The workaround is the
enemy of the work order. Systems that run on heroism are spending
their margins invisibly, every day, until the day the heroism is
one contingency short — and no one can say in advance which day
that will be.

The professionals did not go quietly, and the record proves it.
The controllers' association filed memoranda on the dead radar
and the dying radios with bureaucratic correctness, year after
year. The pilots' and engineers' union raised the fleet's
condition in every negotiation. The World Bank's transport
reviews of the 1980s read today like Part II's table of contents
drafted in advance; ICAO's missions filed parallel findings in
the polite subjunctive of UN documents; the foreign airlines
serving Lagos quietly priced the deficiencies in, tankering their
own fuel and carrying their own engineers. The descent, in other
words, was watched, measured, and minuted by at least five
institutional observers across fifteen years — a documented
emergency answered by no budget. Whatever the failure of the
1980s was, it was never a failure of information. What was
missing was a mechanism to convert known facts into forced
choices. This book calls that mechanism a constituency, and its
eventual construction — out of funerals — is the story of Part
II.

## The airline dies slowly

Nigeria Airways' final act ran through the 1990s to a conclusion
in 2003, but the death was legible by 1990. The fleet shrank
toward single digits; the New York route ended; the European
stations closed one by one; the airline that had flown four
continents finished as an unreliable domestic operator leasing
out its London slot. What stayed vigorous to the end was its
function as an extraction site. The liquidation, when it came,
revealed the expected archaeology: debts in the hundreds of
millions of dollars, assets dispersing into scandal, and — the
wound this book returns to in a moment — a workforce of
thousands whose entitlements now became claims against a carcass.

The exodus deserves its mechanics, because "brain drain" flattens
what was actually a market clearing. The 1980s created a supply
shock — a collapsing airline full of world-certified
professionals paid late in a dissolving currency — and a demand
shock: the Gulf carriers' great expansion, Emirates founded in
1985 on the same oil wealth Nigeria was mismanaging, plus the
standing hunger of Britain and America for experienced crews.
The clearing price was never close. Emeka, our composite
engineer, held out until 1989 — family, national feeling, the
stubborn hope that each new management would be the one — and
then took the Gulf offer that multiplied his salary several
times over, tax free. What is remarkable is not that thousands
left but how the leaving selected: for the training captains,
the check airmen, the senior licensed engineers — the people who
reproduce competence in others. An institution can survive
losing talent. It cannot survive losing its teachers, because
that loss compounds; each departed instructor subtracts not one
professional but a career-long stream of them. By the 1990s,
Nigerian aviation was running on the last cohorts the old
pipeline had finished, and more than one accident in Part II
carries, among its quiet contributing factors, the thinness of
the mentoring bench behind the crews.

## The abandoned

What happened to the airline's people after the end became one of
the longest-running labor tragedies in Nigerian history, and no
journalistic account of this industry is honest without it.

When the liquidation was announced in 2003–04, Nigeria Airways
carried thousands of serving staff and a longer tail of retirees
— pilots, engineers, cabin crew, clerks, many with two and three
decades of service — whose severance and pensions became claims
against the estate of a dead airline owned by the state that had
killed it. The federal government repeatedly promised settlement.
What followed was a generation of waiting. The ex-workers
organized into associations of the retrenched; they picketed the
ministry and the National Assembly through successive
administrations; their leaders aged into professional
petitioners, appearing before committee after committee with
files of verified entitlements. And the associations began
keeping a second ledger — the roll of members who had died
waiting. Former captains and licensed engineers finished their
lives in penury; some were reduced to public appeals for medical
care; by the unions' counts, never contradicted by better
official data, the dead ran into the thousands.

Hauwa's counterpart in this saga — call her Mama Bisi, a cabin
services supervisor of twenty-two years — stood in the queues of
2018 when the settlement finally moved: the Buhari
administration's approval, in 2017–18, of a package reported at
roughly forty-five billion naira, paid in tranches from 2018,
with verification disputes and residual claims running years
beyond. The payment was real. So was its arithmetic: settled
fifteen years late, in a currency that had lost most of its value
across the interval, the entitlements delivered a fraction of the
purchasing power the workers had earned — the difference quietly
confiscated by time and inflation from the people least able to
absorb it.

The saga belongs in this chapter and not an epilogue because its
lesson operated forward. Every serving professional in Nigerian
aviation watched, for twenty years, what the state's gratitude to
its aviation workforce looked like in practice — and priced the
observation. Institutions run on deferred compensation; pensions
are its purest form; and an employer who defaults on the deferred
teaches every current employee to demand everything now, or
leave. The ex-workers of Nigeria Airways paid for the airline's
death twice: once in their livelihoods, and once as the
cautionary tale that helped drain their successors abroad.

## 1990

Set the stage, then, as the decade closes. A country of roughly
ninety million people, treasury gutted, currency a fraction of
itself, governed by a military regime entering its baroque late
period. A flag carrier in terminal decline, bleeding its
competence into the world's cockpits. A new private sector,
vigorous and undercapitalized, flying the rich world's retired
jets into airports built by the boom and maintained by the bust —
navigation aids failing, radar partial, fire services notional. A
regulator that existed chiefly on paper, inside a ministry, under
soldiers, in a culture where every rule had its waiver. A
training college on fumes. An investigation function that had
never once, in thirty years of independence, given the Nigerian
public a full account of why their dead were dead.

No single person intended this. That is the point of these first
three chapters, and the reason they precede the wreckage. The
system that would kill several thousand people over the next two
decades was not built by villains; it was accumulated by deferral
— colonial defaults never revised, boom purchases never
institutionalized, bust economics never confronted, each decade
handing the next a slightly hollower inheritance and a more
practiced vocabulary of denial. By 1990, in the image the safety
literature would later make famous, the holes in the layers of
defense were drifting into alignment. The system needed only
traffic, time, and weather.

The 1990s, generous in every cruelty, would supply all three. The
first invoice arrived in July 1991, at a runway in Saudi Arabia,
in the oldest costume in Nigerian aviation: a chartered aircraft,
a foreign crew, and the pilgrims of the Hajj.
