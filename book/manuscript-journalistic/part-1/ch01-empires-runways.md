# PART I — THE ASCENT (1925–1990)

# Chapter 1 — Empire's Runways

The old men of Kano used to tell it as a story about sound.

On the first day of November 1925, the city heard something it had
never heard before — a drone out of the northeast, thin at first,
then filling the whole sky — and the markets emptied onto the plain
to watch three Royal Air Force biplanes drop out of the Harmattan
haze and settle onto a strip of cleared laterite outside the walls.
The walls were a thousand years old. They had seen caravans from
Tripoli, armies from Sokoto, and, twenty-two years earlier, the
Maxim guns of the British conquest. Now they acquired a new kind of
gate. The emir received the airmen with ceremony. The colonial
Resident wrote glowing dispatches home. And somewhere in the files
of the Colonial Office a precedent was quietly set that would shape
Nigerian aviation for a century: the airplane had arrived in Nigeria
as an instrument of somebody else's system — landing on Nigerian
soil, answering to institutions an ocean away.

Nearly a hundred years later, I stood on what that landing strip
became — the apron of Mallam Aminu Kano International Airport — and
found the pattern still legible in the concrete. The oldest runways
in Nigeria were not built for Nigeria. They were built through it,
by an empire that needed stepping stones across Africa, and the
distinction between hosting aviation and owning it — between
possessing the runways and possessing the institutions that keep
flight safe — turns out to be the oldest theme in this story, and
the deadliest.

## The shortcut across Africa

Follow the route of those 1925 flights on a map and their purpose
announces itself. They did not begin in Lagos, the commercial
capital, or follow the population. They ran laterally across the
Sahel — Cairo to Khartoum to Maiduguri to Kano — linking Britain's
possessions along the old caravan latitudes, a corridor drawn for
the convenience of the colonizer's logistics. When civil aviation
followed in the 1930s, it obeyed the same logic. Imperial Airways'
flying boats and landplanes worked the trunk routes of empire, and
Kano — sitting usefully on the way to somewhere else — became one
of the busiest aviation crossroads in Africa.

For a period between the wars, the Kano rest house was one of the
more cosmopolitan addresses on the continent: colonial governors in
transit, mining engineers, correspondents heading to Abyssinian
wars, aristocrats on shooting tours, all funneled through the
walled city by the range limits of 1930s aircraft. Kano's traders
supplied the operation; its laborers cut the grass and rolled the
fuel drums; its imagination absorbed the idea that the sky was now
a road. The north's relationship with flight — which would carry
the pilgrim traffic to Mecca, and the pilgrim tragedies of 1973
and 1991 — began here, a full generation before Lagos dominated
the story.

It ended the way windfalls end. After the war, the new long-range
airliners flew over the Sahel without stopping, and Kano's transit
traffic evaporated within a decade. Nobody had banked the windfall
into anything durable. It is the first appearance of a pattern this
book will meet at national scale: a rented position mistaken for a
permanent endowment, and nothing invested in the system beneath it
while the rent flowed.

## What the war built, and what it took away

The Second World War gave Nigeria an aviation infrastructure — and
built it, characteristically, for reasons that had nothing to do
with Nigeria. From 1940, with the Mediterranean closed, Britain
ferried its warplanes to Egypt the long way: shipped in crates to
the West African coast, assembled at Takoradi and Lagos, and flown
across the continent in stages — Lagos to Kano, Kano onward to
Sudan and the Nile. At its peak the West African Reinforcement
Route moved hundreds of aircraft a month. The Americans arrived
after 1941 and enlarged everything: runways, hangars, fuel farms,
radio stations — a physical plant that would carry Nigerian civil
aviation for a generation.

Thousands of Nigerians worked that route as riggers, fitters,
loaders, and clerks. A few flew: Peter Thomas, a Lagosian,
became the first Nigerian commissioned into the Royal Air Force,
celebrated in the Lagos press, planting an idea — the cockpit could
be Nigerian too. But the deeper lesson of the war years was one
nobody celebrated, because it was visible only in retrospect. The
wartime system worked brilliantly because it was a closed loop, run
by organizations that owned every link: their aircraft, their
mechanics, their fuel testers, their discipline. When the war
ended, the organizations went home and took the loop with them.
What remained was hardware. From the very beginning, Nigeria's
experience of world-class aviation was the experience of watching
it performed on Nigerian soil by systems that never passed into
Nigerian hands.

## The corporation that worked

There is a might-have-been buried in this chapter, and its name was
WAAC.

The West African Airways Corporation, founded in 1946, was a
public corporation on the model of the BBC — jointly owned by four
colonial governments (Nigeria, the Gold Coast, Sierra Leone, the
Gambia), headquartered in Lagos, run by aviation professionals,
overseen from a Britain too far away to be lobbied. It flew small
de Havilland airliners on a lattice of West African routes, trained
the first generation of Nigerian ground staff and eventually
aircrew, and ran, by the accounts that survive, with a discipline
its successors never matched.

Consider what an ordinary WAAC morning looked like, reconstructed
from its operating culture. The engineer signed the daily
inspection in a bound log. The load sheet was computed and
countersigned. If a magneto check failed at run-up, the flight
canceled — and here is the point — *nobody called anybody*. There
was no one to call. Accountability to four governments meant, in
practice, accountability to no single politician; the decision to
cancel belonged to the system, not to a negotiation. Passengers
grumbled at the notice board and waited for the afternoon Heron.
The airplane did not crash. Multiply that uncancelled phone call
across a decade, and WAAC's safety record assembles itself out of
pure governance — on 1950s technology, in the same skies and
storms that will fill Part II of this book with wreckage.

The corporation did not survive independence politics. As the Gold
Coast moved toward sovereignty, Kwame Nkrumah pulled out to found
Ghana Airways — a flag for the first flag of free Africa — and the
shared corporation's logic collapsed. Nigeria followed, converting
its share into the company that would become Nigeria Airways. A
professionally run airline owned by four states, too big for any
one government to raid, was precisely the kind of institution West
Africa would spend the next half-century failing to rebuild. It
had existed. It was dismantled for the flag. Ghana Airways died in
2004; Nigeria Airways died in 2003. The little Doves of WAAC, in
hindsight, were carrying more than passengers.

## The invisible seats

Who flew, in the 1950s? Increasingly, the men who were about to
inherit the country. The lawyers to the constitutional conferences,
the politicians shuttling to Lagos, the students connecting toward
London — the independence generation was the first for whom air
travel was an ordinary tool of public life, and the nationalist
press covered aviation with the intensity other countries reserved
for sport. "One day Nigerians will fly these planes" was a
platform line of the era, and it was honest, and honorable, and —
read carefully — revealing. The ambition was to fly the planes: to
occupy the visible seats, the cockpit, the boardroom. Nobody
campaigns to inherit the airworthiness inspectorate.

That asymmetry decided more than any single event in this chapter.
When decolonization accelerated — institutions that had assumed a
generation to train Nigerians were given five years — the visible
transfers were celebrated like boxing titles: the first Nigerian
captain, the first Nigerian station manager, the airline bought
out fully by 1961 so the flag would be clean. The invisible
transfers mostly never happened. A pilot took a decade to make; an
airworthiness surveyor — the professional who inspects the
inspectors — took longer, and there was no Nigerian cohort in a
pipeline that had been closed to Nigerians. The expatriate
surveyors ran down their contracts and went home. The British
airworthiness system stopped covering Nigerian aircraft, and no
equivalent capability was built in Lagos at matching depth. Nobody
decided this in a memo. It happened by the sum of attention paid
elsewhere — and it set the pattern this book traces all the way to
2006: in Nigerian aviation, the operating roles Nigerianized
quickly and well, and the oversight roles did not, because
operations had glamour and a wage market, and oversight had
neither. States staff what they love.

## What the first accidents taught — and whom

Colonial aviation was dangerous everywhere, and nobody pretended
otherwise. Aircraft went down along the African routes with grim
regularity; forced landings in the bush were an after-dinner story
genre. What matters for our purposes is the ritual that followed
each wreck. An inspector investigated under Britain's air
navigation rules. The findings went to London, were filed in
London, and changed standards maintained in London. The system
was, for its time, genuinely good — Britain had grasped early that
the purpose of accident investigation is prevention, not
punishment. But observe what it meant for the colony: Nigeria
experienced the crashes, and the metropole accumulated the
learning. The feedback loop that turns accidents into safety — the
whole secret, this book will argue, of aviation's transformation
into the safest way to move — ran through Nigeria without ever
touching down.

And one habit transferred perfectly: publicity. Colonial accident
findings were internal correspondence, shared with a public of
colonial subjects rarely and partially. The idea that a report is
*owed* to the public — that a state must explain a disaster to the
people it fell upon — was simply not part of the inheritance. When
the independent Nigerian state later sat on its accident reports
for years, or forever, it was not innovating. It was continuing an
administrative tradition. Institutions bequeath their reflexes,
and secrecy is a reflex.

## The paper trail

A note on evidence, before the flag rises. The colonial era left
an exhaustive record — the monthly serviceability returns of the
Kano wireless station, the land disputes and labor musters of
every airstrip, the minuted inquiry into every bent undercarriage
— indexed and retrievable in London and, patchily, in the Nigerian
archives. Set that against what a researcher finds for the 1980s
and 1990s: vanished registries, unfindable reports, records lost
to floods and termites and convenience. It is easier today to
reconstruct the aviation administration of 1935 than of 1985.

Hold that inversion. Record-keeping is the load-bearing habit — the
floor under oversight, accountability, memory, and, not
incidentally, this book. States that write things down can be
audited, reformed, and eventually understood. States that stop
cannot — not even by themselves.

## Independence Day

On the first of October 1960, as the green-and-white flag rose over
Lagos, aircraft of the new national carrier flew overhead in
salute. The physical inheritance beneath them was real: good
runways at Kano, Lagos, Port Harcourt; trained ground staff;
membership in the world system framed at Chicago in 1944. The
institutional inheritance was hollower than it looked. Regulation
had been an imported service, now withdrawn. The network faced
outward, toward London. The airline was three parts symbol to one
part system. And aviation's political meaning — as spectacle, as
sovereignty, as the state showing itself to its people from above
— had been fixed by the RAF in 1925 and adopted without revision
by the nationalists who replaced them.

None of it made disaster inevitable. Inheritance is not destiny;
Ethiopia's story, which shadows this book like a control
experiment, proves the institutions of flight can be built
deliberately, even in poverty, when a state decides to want them.
But inheritances set the defaults, and defaults govern whenever
attention lapses. Nigeria began its independent aviation history
with defaults set by an empire that had needed runways, not
institutions — and with a political class that needed, above all,
a flag in the sky.

The next chapter is about that flag: how brightly it flew, what
it cost, and what was quietly not being built beneath it while
everyone watched it wave.
