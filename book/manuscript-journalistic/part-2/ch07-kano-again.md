# Chapter 7 — Kano, Again

Alhaji Sani — the composite voice of the Gwammaja survivors, built
from the testimony the press recorded in the days that followed —
was minding his stall when the sound came over the rooftops:
engines laboring where engines had no business being, low enough
to read the lettering. It was early afternoon on Saturday, 4 May
2002, the markets of the old quarter at full volume, a primary
school in session, the mosques filling toward prayer.

Out at Mallam Aminu Kano International Airport, EAS Airlines
Flight 4226 — a BAC 1-11 more than twenty years old, bound for
Lagos with over seventy people aboard, a serving federal minister
among them — had used a great deal of runway, lifted off late and
heavy into the furnace heat, and then done the thing witnesses
described with terrible uniformity: it stopped climbing.
Staggering at rooftop height, it cleared the airport fence, sank
across the neighborhoods southwest of the field, cut a burning
lane through houses and shops, and came apart. The people in the
cabin had little chance. The people beneath had none. When the
counting was finally done — and in that packed, informal urban
fabric it was never really done — the settled figure approached
148 dead: roughly half from the aircraft, and half from the city
under it.

The people of Gwammaja never bought a ticket. The aviation system
crashed into them anyway.

## The quarter and the carrier

Gwammaja belongs to the oldest continuously inhabited urban
fabric in West Africa — family compounds, Koranic schools,
workshops, woven at walking scale over centuries, its lanes too
narrow for a fire engine and never planned to need one. When the
airport was sited in the colonial era, it stood well clear of the
walls. By 2002, a city of three million had flowed around it, as
every African airport city has flowed around its runways.
Aviation planning has a vocabulary for managing that convergence
— public safety zones at runway ends, obstacle surfaces, land-use
control under the climb funnels where accident probability
concentrates — and the vocabulary existed in Nigerian regulation.
Its enforcement would have required the airport authority, the
state government, the municipal planners, and the emirate's land
customs to act as one; and nothing in this book has yet managed
to be one thing, let alone four. So the funnels filled with
people, and on 4 May 2002 the risk contours that engineers draw
off the end of a runway lay across roofs that held sleeping
families — and collected.

The carrier was the era's median, and that is the finding. EAS
was a mid-tier operator of the deregulation era's second wave:
aircraft legal, crews licensed, existence certified annually by
the oversight system Chapter 11 dissects. The inquiries found no
monstrous deviation. They found the standard condition of the
marginal Nigerian carrier — thin capital, elderly equipment, a
weight culture loose in the way the whole system was loose,
engineering fed by a bazaar of brokered parts. When a system's
typical firm, operating typically, produces catastrophe, the
actuarial word for that system is not "risky." It is priced to
kill on schedule.

As for the aircraft: the BAC 1-11 was Britain's 727 — a rugged,
deafening workhorse of the 1960s, extinct by 2002 in the skies of
the countries that built it, banned by noise rules and retired by
economics, and living out its long retirement on the one
continent whose regulators could not afford to refuse it. Chapter
3 explained the supply side of that fact. This chapter is the
demand side — and the bill, delivered to third parties who never
entered the transaction.

## The physics of a hot afternoon

The performance calculus that failed over Gwammaja deserves plain
language, because every passenger boarding a heavily loaded
flight from a hot-climate airport sits inside it.

Air is an aircraft's working fluid, and heat thins it. A wing
lifts, and an engine thrusts, in proportion to the mass of air
each can process; on a forty-degree Sahel afternoon, the
atmosphere delivers meaningfully less of it per cubic meter than
the "standard day" the manuals assume. Engineers fold the
difference into a number called density altitude: that afternoon,
an aircraft sitting on Kano's runway was performing as if it were
departing a mountain airfield thousands of feet higher. Every
increment compounds — higher speeds needed for the same lift,
longer roll to reach them, less spare thrust for the climb,
thinner margin if an engine falters at the worst moment.

Now degrade the assumptions the way the era degraded them. An
old engine, decades from the test cell, delivers a little less
than book. A load sheet built on standard weights, in a baggage
culture that ran on negotiation, is a little optimistic. The
obstacle survey in the file describes a smaller city. Each
softness is small, deniable, and cumulative — and their sum is
not an approximation of the certified case but a different case,
never computed by anyone. On a cool morning at light weight, the
aircraft that crashed would likely have flown away from the same
engine trouble, and no one would ever have learned how little
margin remained. Hot afternoons are the audit. Margins die by
being lent away in slivers, each borrower certain the others
left enough — and the lesson runs far beyond aviation: systems
reveal their borrowed margins only at load, and a society that
meets its systems only in fair weather knows nothing about them
at all.

## The hospital day

Kano's hospitals learned of the crash the way the state did: from
the smoke, and then from the crowds. Within the hour, the
specialist hospital and the teaching hospital were receiving
simultaneous waves no triage plan had allocated — the burned, the
crushed, and the walking shocked, delivered in taxis and pickup
beds by the same neighbors who had dug them out, distributed
among facilities by proximity and rumor. No casualty-regulating
function matched patient to resource: burn cases arrived at
hospitals without burn capability while theaters elsewhere stood
ready; families searched facility to facility for days, because
no central register existed to search once. Blood ran short by
evening and was replenished the Nigerian way — an appeal,
answered magnificently, by ordinary Kano queuing at the donor
benches.

The uncounted consequence was the second week: the burn and crush
patients who survived the day and died of infection, renal
failure, and delay — the deaths a mature trauma system exists to
prevent. No study ever enumerated them. Nigerian disaster
mortality is counted at the scene or not at all, which flatters
every response the statistics touch — and that, too, is a
finding. A state that does not count its second-week dead has
decided the post-disaster patient is the family's project. The
families of Gwammaja, who had already supplied the rescue,
supplied the nursing too.

## The ledger of the uncounted

What Nigeria owed Gwammaja, and what it paid, is the cleanest
test of a compensation system in this book — the surface victim's
claim being pure liability: no ticket contract, no assumed risk,
no contributory anything.

International aviation law had recognized the ground victim for
half a century. Nigerian practice ran otherwise. The airline's
insurance position — adequate on paper for certification — became,
on the day it was needed, a fortress of process: claims requiring
documents the informal city does not generate (title deeds, death
certificates, letters of administration); adjusters valuing
compound housing with formulas built for insured suburbs;
settlements offered at fractions and accepted from exhaustion, or
litigated into the second decade. Government gestures — a relief
fund, a presidential visit, rebuilding pledges — receded with the
news cycle. Years later, journalists marking anniversaries found
Gwammaja households still holding files. EAS itself did not
survive the year's regulatory aftermath; its liquidation converted
the remaining claims from slow to moot.

The pattern generalizes, and it completes the chapter's argument.
A liability system is how an economy prices harm back onto its
producers. Where courts are slow, records informal, and
defendants corporately mortal, the price of harming third parties
rounds to zero — and a cost that rounds to zero is a cost no
spreadsheet will ever avoid. The uncompensated of Gwammaja are a
moral failure. They are also a market signal, received clearly by
every marginal operator of the era: the downside of falling on a
neighborhood is survivable — for everyone except the
neighborhood. Systems get the accidents they price for.

## The year the country was already bleeding

The crash fell into a nation still in shock. In January 2002, the
Ikeja armoury explosions had killed more than a thousand Lagosians
— many drowned in a canal while fleeing — in a catastrophe whose
emergency-response failures prefigured everything Chapter 13
systematizes. The May headlines set the armoury's canal beside
Gwammaja's rubble and drew the connection the government least
wanted drawn: two disasters, one military and one civil, sharing
one anatomy — hazardous systems sited among the poor, maintained
by deferral, answered by mourning.

And yet 2002's politics differed from every previous chapter's in
one respect that would prove decisive. Nigeria was now a
democracy, three years old, with an election approaching. The
crash received something Jeddah, Ejigbo, and the lagoon never
had: political competition over its meaning. The National
Assembly summoned officials; the opposition framed the disaster
as governmental neglect; the unmuzzled press investigated the
airline, the regulator, and the ministry with a freedom
unimaginable under Abacha. The government responded with probes,
suspensions, pledges — and one durable policy: the move toward an
age limit on imported passenger aircraft.

The age rule deserves its footnote in the history of regulatory
design, because its critics were right and it was right anyway.
Age is a crude proxy — maintenance, not chronology, makes an
aircraft safe, and a rigorously kept twenty-five-year-old is
safer than a neglected ten-year-old. But crude was the point. A
bright line is cheap to enforce, and the NCAA of 2002 possessed
nothing like the inspection depth that condition-based oversight
requires. Where oversight capacity is scarce, rules must be
designed to be cheap to verify — a principle that travels far
beyond aviation. The age rule was a crutch, adopted honestly as
a crutch, by an institution that knew it could not yet walk.

Mark 4226, then, as the hinge where the politics of Nigerian
aviation changed phase: accountability had acquired a market.
Under military rule, a disaster was an information-management
problem for the regime; under democracy it became a resource for
the regime's competitors — and institutions reform when someone
powerful profits from their reform. The machinery was still weak;
the probes joined the national archive of the unpublished. But
the demand now existed, and three years later, when the system
faced its greatest test, it was waiting.

## The twin across the ocean

Nigeria's closest institutional twin in world aviation is
Indonesia, and the resemblance is almost eerie: a sprawling,
populous, oil-exporting state that deregulated aviation in the
1990s, grew traffic explosively on the rich world's hand-me-down
jets, and paid in a drumbeat of disasters that ended in European
bans and an American downgrade in 2007. The Indonesian version of
the 4226 problem — marginal carriers, old fleets, encroached
airports — produced the same policy debates on nearly the same
calendar.

The divergence came later, and it is the instructive part.
Indonesian reform, once seriously begun, was sustained through
growth: the state harnessed its aviation boom as a development
priority, rebuilt its regulator with international partners on
published, dated plans, forced fleet renewal through
consolidation around carriers with real balance sheets, and by
2016–2018 had exited the ban lists and regained American
approval. Two states with the same disease took the same
medicine. Only one stayed on it. The difference was not
knowledge, money, or culture. It was the political decision to
make aviation a growth strategy rather than a patronage arena —
which gave Jakarta's reformers the one thing Abuja's never
durably had: a constituency richer than the constituency for
decay. Grief starts reforms. Only interest finishes them.

## After

There is a moral asymmetry at the heart of this chapter that the
tidy language of "aviation safety" conceals. The passengers of
4226 participated, at least formally, in the system that killed
them: they chose to fly, paid a fare, accepted transport's
ordinary bargain. The people of Gwammaja participated in
nothing. They were killed by a system's externalities while
cooking lunch — by a fleet-age policy never enforced, a weight
culture never disciplined, an obstacle survey never updated, a
city never planned, each deferral executed by someone who would
never live under the climb path of runway 24. Economists call
such arrangements moral hazard. The older word is injustice. A
fair test of any regulatory state is how it treats the people
who cannot exit its failures — and by that test, the system of
2002 failed more completely in Gwammaja than anywhere else in
this book, because every other chapter's victims at least appear
in the system's own ledgers.

Kano has now appeared three times in this history: 1925, the
stage of empire; 1973, the boom's unread warning; 2002, the
deregulated bust's externalized bill. The city had given warning
enough. The country had three years to act on it, and did not —
and the scene now shifts south, to a forest clearing near a
village called Lisa, and a runway at Port Harcourt, in the year
when everything broke at once.
