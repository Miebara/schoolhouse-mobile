# Chapter 8 — The Year of Weeping

The night of 22 October 2005 is preserved, hour by hour, in the
next morning's newspapers, and it reads like a fever chart of a
state losing track of itself.

Bellview Airlines Flight 210, a Boeing 737 bound from Lagos to
Abuja with 117 people aboard, took off into a line of
thunderstorms a little after nine in the evening and, within about
three minutes, ceased to exist as far as the Nigerian state could
tell. Not crashed — *vanished*. Contact was lost in the climb, and
then, for the better part of a night and a morning, the Federal
Republic of Nigeria could not locate a Boeing 737 that had fallen
within a few dozen miles of its largest city.

The search apparatus activated as separate organisms — aviation,
military, police, the young emergency agency — each calling its
own chain, none commanding the others, no joint rescue center
taking charge of the picture. Into the vacuum came the new
information ecology of phones and morning radio: a report,
never satisfactorily traced, placed the crash near Kishi, in Oyo
State, more than a hundred kilometers away. Officials repeated
it. The machinery lurched toward it. Families were told, and some
began driving. For much of the night, the Republic searched the
wrong forest at the wrong end of the country while the actual
wreckage smoldered under canopy at Lisa, in Ogun State — barely
thirty kilometers from the departure runway. The farmers of Lisa
found it at first light, walking toward the smoke their
government's aircraft had flown past: a crater in the forest, the
737 driven into the earth nearly vertically, no survivors, and
for several hours more a site whose custody belonged to whoever
arrived. The passengers had been dead since minutes after
takeoff. The twelve-hour search had been a search for the state's
own situational awareness.

Seven weeks later, on the afternoon of 10 December, the second
blow fell — and it fell, with terrible precision, on the parents
of one school.

## The school flight

Sosoliso Airlines Flight 1145, a DC-9 from Abuja to Port
Harcourt, carried 110 people that Saturday, and their composition
is the fact around which Nigerian memory of the year has
crystallized ever since: sixty of them were students of Loyola
Jesuit College, Abuja — one of the country's finest secondary
schools — flying home as a group for Christmas.

Nigeria is a boarding-school nation: a legacy of colonial
education, reinforced by unity schools that deliberately mix the
regions, and by a professional class dispersed across a
federation whose distances defeat weekend parenting. At every
term's turning, thousands of minors cross the country in
concentrated waves on predictable dates — the "school run,"
booked in seat blocks, younger children chaperoned by older, and
funded by parents precisely as a safety purchase, the flight
being judged, statistically and reasonably, safer than the
highway. The system had thereby aggregated its most precious and
least consenting passengers into peak-density cohorts — and had
attached to that concentration no special diligence whatsoever:
no enhanced dispatch conservatism for the school waves, no
seasonal weather review for the December run into the turning of
the seasons. The tenth of December 2005 was the arithmetic of
that inattention: one aircraft, one storm, one continued
approach.

On approach to Port Harcourt in a thunderstorm, Flight 1145
encountered windshear, struck the ground short of and beside the
runway, broke up along the grass, and burned. The impact was
survivable for many aboard. Then the second disaster began. The
airport fire service reached the burning wreck late — and
reached it, by the convergent testimony of survivors' families
and subsequent inquiry, without adequate water or foam.
Passengers who had lived through the impact died in the fire.
One hundred and eight people were lost. Two survived, both
terribly burned — among them a teenage girl named Kechi Okwuchi,
whose years of reconstructive surgery abroad, and whose eventual
appearance on an American television stage, singing, would
become the tragedy's one publicly bearable thread. Her account
of the fire, told many times on the public record — the impact
survivable, the flames arriving before help — has done more than
any official document to keep one detail lodged in the national
memory: the fire trucks that came without water.

Sixty children of one school. A headmaster reading names at a
carol service that became a funeral. A mass grave on the school
grounds, because some of the children could not be told apart.

## The mothers of Loyola

What the Loyola Jesuit families did next distinguishes 2005 from
every prior chapter of this book, and it began with a
characteristic decision: they organized as the school community,
not as scattered claimants.

The school on the Abuja plain was the reconstruction of the
Nigerian professional class in miniature — civil servants and
doctors, officers and entrepreneurs, north and south, Muslim and
Christian, in a school run by Catholic fathers — and its parents
included senior lawyers who understood process, officials who
understood ministries, and mothers who understood, as one told
the press in words that entered the era's record, that they had
nothing left to threaten. They formed associations. They retained
counsel and attended every adjournment of every proceeding. They
built memorial scholarship funds, demanded the Sosoliso report
and got it, pressed the fire-service findings into the public
record, and turned each anniversary into an audit. They made
themselves, deliberately, the disaster's permanent institutional
witness.

Organized, articulate, unignorable grief is the only force in
this history that has ever outlasted a news cycle. In October
2006, when one more aircraft fell — carrying a king — it was
waiting, fully assembled. The reform that ends this Part was
built on many things; none mattered more than sixty small graves
in Abuja and the parents who refused to let the country look
away.

## The boxes that would not speak

The Bellview investigation failed forensically, and the failure
carries a policy lesson precise enough to legislate from. Flight
recorders are built around armored memory rated to survive
defined crash forces and an hour of fire — rated, that is,
against the envelope of survivable and typical accidents. A
near-vertical, high-velocity impact into laterite concentrates
energy at the extreme edge of any rating. The units recovered
from the Lisa crater emerged damaged beyond the readout capacity
then reachable by Nigeria — no domestic laboratory existed — and
the wreckage-site custody of the first days, a trampled,
scavenged excavation, degraded what remained. The final report,
when it surfaced years later, was an honest document about its
own defeat: the sequence — storm upset, instrument failure,
disorientation, some compound — could only be bounded, never
established.

Flight 210 thus holds a particular place in this history: the
crash about which Nigeria knows least, and the demonstration
that a state which cannot find a wreck for twelve hours, and
cannot read a recorder recovered from its own soil, has
forfeited even the consolation of understanding its disasters.
The translations are direct — recorder standards worth
regulating ahead of minimums, site custody a trainable
discipline, a regional African readout laboratory that would
repay its cost with the first mystery it retired — and Nigeria's
investigators did, in later years, build real readout capability
in Abuja. The families of 210, fifteen years too early, received
instead a single word: undetermined. That is what infrastructure
gaps are called after they happen to someone.

And the mystery's damage compounded outward, because Bellview
was the era's *respectable* airline — the survivor of the
1990s cull, the regional expander, the business traveler's
choice. With no authoritative cause ever established, the
permanent ambiguity was colonized by everything an information
famine breeds: sabotage theories, hidden-failure theories,
causes-known-and-suppressed theories. The airline's brand never
recovered; it declined and ceased operations within a few years,
its corporate records dispersing with it. Investigative
capacity, the case teaches, is not a service to blame. It is a
service to innocence: the crew who could not defend themselves,
the engineers suspected forever without evidence, the public
left unable to distinguish the era's best operator from its
worst — a distinction on which a market's whole safety incentive
depends.

## The go-around that wasn't

The Sosoliso cockpit, in its last ninety seconds, held a
decision structure that aviation psychology has since mapped so
thoroughly it carries a name in every training syllabus: plan
continuation bias — the deepening commitment to a course of
action as evidence accumulates against it, strongest exactly
when the plan is nearly complete.

An approach is the textbook habitat. The crew has briefed it,
configured for it; the runway is two minutes ahead; every mental
model is landing-shaped. Each adverse cue arrives incrementally —
the windshear report, the sink rate, the picture degrading —
none individually crossing the threshold that would have
forbidden the approach outright had it presented at the start.
The go-around, meanwhile, is priced by the brain as an event:
fuel, delay, explanation, the subtle professional confession of
misjudgment. The continuation is priced as nothing. It is the
plan.

The industry's answer, hardened worldwide in exactly the years
after 2005, was to abolish the judgment call: defined gates on
every approach — speed, configuration, descent rate — at which
any deviation *mandates* the go-around, no diagnosis permitted,
the decision pre-made in the briefing room where cognition is
cheap rather than at four hundred feet where it is impossible.
Airlines that enforce the doctrine have driven
approach-and-landing accidents — historically aviation's largest
category — toward the floor. The crews of 1145 and, ten months
later, of 053 flew before it reached Nigerian operations.
Between them, the two crashes taught the Nigerian industry the
sentence its training departments now repeat as liturgy: a
go-around is never an admission of failure. It is the system
working.

## What 2005 established

Read the two investigations together and the year's verdict on
the Nigerian state assembles itself. It could not reliably
prevent: two mass-casualty weather-related losses in seven
weeks. It could not find: twelve hours to Lisa. It could not
rescue: the dry tenders of Port Harcourt, at an airport
certified on paper to international firefighting categories
calibrated — to the minute and the liter — against the physics
of fuel fires and the survival window of an intact cabin. And in
the worst case it could not even explain: the unreadable boxes.
Prevention, response, explanation — the three duties a state
owes the flying public, failed simultaneously, in public, on
live television, before a press that no longer answered to a
censor.

That comprehensive visibility is what made 2005 politically
different from everything before it. After Bellview, the
machinery had produced its standard outputs — mourning, a probe,
contradictory official statements. But the press did not move
on; the private broadcasters and matured newspapers kept the
search fiasco and the radar questions on the front pages for
weeks, and began the cumulative, cross-referencing coverage that
converts events into a record. The international layer engaged —
the ICAO audit cycle already scheduled, foreign carriers'
concerns leaking into the trade press. And when Sosoliso
detonated inside that charged field, the response ran at a speed
no previous disaster had commanded: the president at Port
Harcourt and at the school within days, photographed amid sixty
caskets; a national aviation summit within weeks, its terms of
reference the skeleton of the coming law — regulator autonomy,
investigation independence, infrastructure funding; the
professional diaspora recruited into the drafting; the
international agencies invited into the room. The political
class, facing elections with 2005's images fresh, discovered
that aviation reform had become that rarest Nigerian commodity —
a policy with no organized opposition. Even the operators the
coming purge would kill could not argue publicly against reform
across the graves of sixty children.

States change when the cost of not changing is made legible to
the people who govern them. The terrible achievement of 2005 was
legibility.

## After

Nations, like people, have years that divide their lives into
before and after. What 2005 changed was not the system's facts —
Chapters 3 through 7 had established them in full — but the
system's witnesses: for the first time, the failure was total,
simultaneous, televised, and inflicted on a constituency
organized enough to refuse consolation. The children of Loyola
Jesuit did not die because the system of 2005 was worse than the
system of 1996. They died because it was the same. And the
country, at last, could see it.

At the memorial garden on the school grounds, sixty names are
carved in stone, and the students who read them aloud each
December were not born when the aircraft fell. Visitors describe
the garden's particular silence. It is the silence this book has
been arguing against from its first page — the unpublished
report, the unanswered hazard log, the undrilled airport, the
state that mourns fluently and reforms only under compulsion. In
October 2006, ten months after the carol service that became a
funeral, one more aircraft would fall, and the compulsion would
finally arrive.
