# PART II — THE CRASHES (1990–2020)

# Chapter 4 — Fire Over Jeddah

They boarded in the white of ihram, the seamless cloth of
consecration — farmers and traders from Sokoto and its hinterland,
most of them at the end of the journey of their lives. It was the
morning of 11 July 1991, already furnace-hot on the Jeddah ramp,
and Nigeria Airways Flight 2120 was one more rotation in the great
annual machinery of the Hajj airlift: 247 pilgrims going home, and
a fourteen-member Canadian crew to take them.

The aircraft wore three flags at once. It was a Douglas DC-8,
twenty-three years old, built in America, owned and operated by
the Montreal charter company Nationair, and painted for the season
in the green and white of Nigeria Airways — a wet lease, in the
trade's language: an aircraft rented with its crew, maintenance,
and insurance, flying under one country's brand while answering,
in every operational particular, to another's. Nigeria Airways, by
1991 unable to mount the Hajj surge from its own decaying fleet,
had contracted the lift. Nationair, whose aggressive growth had
outrun its cash, needed the revenue badly. Two institutional
desperations had met in a marketplace, and 261 people were about
to board the result.

From pushback to impact took twenty-nine minutes. The evidence had
begun accumulating days before the aircraft left the ground.

## The soft tires

The Saudi investigators would later fix the origin of the disaster
not in the sky but at a desk. Tire-pressure checks in the days
before the accident had found two of the DC-8's main-gear tires
significantly below limits. The maintenance staff flagged it. The
nitrogen rig needed to service them was unserviceable. Proper
servicing meant delay, and delay, in Hajj economics, is the one
unforgivable sin: revenue arrives per rotation flown, pilgrims
with calendar-bound visas stack up in terminals, and the client —
here the Nigerian state's own Hajj apparatus — measures its
contractor in departures achieved. Management, under that
pressure, let the aircraft keep flying.

An underinflated aircraft tire does not merely risk going flat.
Under load, at speed, it flexes beyond its design, and flexing
rubber generates heat with terrible efficiency. On the long, hot,
heavy takeoff roll of 11 July, one tire failed; its overloaded
neighbor followed; wheel rims ground along the runway in a plume
of sparks. The crew, feeling vibration but seeing no engine
anomaly, flew the aircraft off the runway — and then, following
the procedure of the age, retracted the landing gear, drawing
wheels that were on fire up into wells packed with hydraulic
lines, beneath the cabin floor, where the DC-8 carried no fire
detection at all.

The first unambiguous warnings arrived as systems began dying —
hydraulics, pressurization — and then as smoke. The crew turned
back for Jeddah. Behind them, the fire was burning through the
cabin floor into the passenger compartment. In the final minutes,
as the aircraft descended toward the runway it had left less than
half an hour before, the floor structure failed progressively;
witnesses on the ground saw bodies and debris falling from the
burning aircraft along the last miles of its approach. Control
was lost short of the threshold. There were no survivors.

It remains the deadliest accident in Canadian aviation history,
the deadliest ever suffered by a DC-8, and the deadliest single
disaster in the history of Nigerian civil aviation. It is
commemorated in almost no public place in any of the three
countries whose flags the aircraft wore.

## The operator

Nationair was a child of the deregulated 1980s: founded in 1984, by
1991 one of Canada's largest charter operators, built on the model
of every ambitious charter firm of the era — buy the previous
generation's aircraft cheap, fly them hard, chase the world's
seasonal spikes, and above all the greatest spike of all, the
Hajj. Growth that fast is bought on margin, and by that summer the
company's finances were strained and its labor relations poisonous
— its flight attendants were locked in a dispute that put the
phrase "replacement crews" into the coverage of the disaster.
None of this was hidden. It was the ordinary, visible profile of a
company running hot.

The question the wreckage posed was who, in the world's aviation
architecture, was responsible for reading that profile and acting
on it. Canada licensed the operator — but the operation ran nine
time zones from Montreal, under a contract Canada had no reason to
scrutinize. Saudi Arabia owned the runway — but wielded over a
foreign-registered transient only the blunt instrument of ramp
checks. And Nigeria owned the passengers, the brand on the fin,
and the contract — and had persuaded itself that the whole point
of chartering was to buy capacity and shed accountability in a
single transaction. Three states each held a corner of the
triangle. The center, where 261 people sat, was sovereign to
nobody.

## The dead

Unfold the number, because numbers this size defeat the
imagination. The Hajj pilgrim of that time and place was typically
not young and not rich; the pilgrimage came at the end of decades
of saving, financed by the sale of land or cattle; whole village
savings cohorts traveled together. Their deaths subtracted not
individuals but strata — the hajji as village elder, arbitrator,
creditor, the person whose standing reorganized a community on his
return. They died in the white of ihram, which is also, in Muslim
burial practice, the color of the shroud. The north understood
that terrible completeness at once.

In Sokoto, the returning flights of that season landed into a city
where nearly every extended family held a claim on the manifest.
No bodies came home. The scholars resolved it as scholars had for
centuries of pilgrims lost at sea: the funeral prayer in absentia,
performed for the collective dead in the great mosque and repeated
through the ward mosques for a week. The federal delegation came,
condoled, and left. What settled afterward, in the north's long
memory, was a specific residue: the Hajj flight as an act of faith
in both senses — undertaken in trust of God, because trust of the
system would have been eccentric.

The fourteen Canadian crew have names, dockets, memorials — the
ordinary forensic dignity of a country whose institutions count
its dead. The 247 pilgrims were reconstructed afterward from
pilgrim-board records, approximately. That asymmetry is not a
detail of the aftermath. It *is* the aftermath: the institutional
weight of a death, in this story, depends on which state's systems
were responsible for remembering the life.

## Two countries read one report

The Saudi final report was unusually direct for the genre: the
aircraft had been dispatched with tires known to be below limits;
serviceable equipment had not been available; management, under
commercial pressure, had permitted continued operation. The
technical chain — underinflation, overdeflection, thermal failure,
fire carried retracted into an unprotected wheel well — was
established in forensic detail. The accident entered the world's
maintenance human-factors curricula, where it is taught to this
day as the canonical case of a "minor" ground-handling economy
becoming a hull loss: the fatal decision made not in a cockpit but
at a desk, days earlier, by someone who never boarded.

What happened next depended entirely on which country was doing
the reading.

In Canada, the disaster produced press investigation, regulatory
soul-searching about the charter model, litigation, documentaries
— a public record in which the dead of Jeddah, though foreigners,
entered the national aviation conscience. Nationair's reputation
never recovered; the company collapsed into bankruptcy in 1993.
Ask a Canadian aircraft maintenance engineer about Nationair a
generation later and you receive, in my experience of the
profession's folklore, a short course on dispatch pressure and
the duty to say no.

In Nigeria — the state of the victims — the report went
substantially unread. No public inquiry examined how the national
carrier had come to place a quarter-thousand citizens on an
aircraft whose operator's strain was visible from Montreal. No
reform of charter oversight followed. The airlift resumed, because
the airlift always resumed, under successor arrangements of
varying quality. One document; two national digestions; and the
difference between them is very nearly the whole subject of this
book. Aviation safety is often described as a technology or a
budget. It is more fundamentally a reading practice — the
institutional habit of treating every report as addressed to
oneself. Canada, which lost fourteen citizens, read the report as
an indictment of Canada. Nigeria, which lost two hundred and
forty-seven, read it as news about a Canadian airline.

## The settlement

Money is the aftermath's coldest chapter and its most revealing,
and the settlement geometry followed the accountability geometry
exactly. The Canadian families' claims proceeded through Canadian
channels against an airline sliding into bankruptcy — a documented
trail, at least. The pilgrim families' claims entered a different
universe: a lattice of insurers and reinsurers, the troubled
underwriting of Nigeria Airways, the political promises of the
Hajj apparatus, and the old Warsaw Convention's liability caps,
which valued a human life, in the currency of 1991, at figures
that read today as obscene. Families navigated this from villages
without lawyers, against counterparties paid to wait. Partial
disbursements arrived years late and diminished; other claims
simply exhausted into silence. No Nigerian official document
states, anywhere, what the families of Flight 2120 were finally
paid.

The wider point outlives the case. Compensation is not an epilogue
to safety; it is part of safety's price system. Where the deaths
of passengers cost carriers and states dearly, the cost flows
upstream into maintenance and dispatch decisions. Where deaths are
cheap, cheapness flows upstream too. The families of 2120 were
failed three times — by a maintenance economy, by an oversight
vacuum, and by a settlement system that priced both failures, for
their authors, at approximately nothing.

## The index card

It is fair to ask what a competent Nigerian state would have done
differently, and the answer fits on an index card, every line of
which existed somewhere in the world in 1991. A charterer with
functioning oversight writes technical conditions into the
contract and enforces audit rights. It stations a two-person
technical team with the operation, with authority to ground. It
requires its regulator — not its airline's commercial department —
to approve the operator. And it prepares for the worst in advance:
a manifest system that knows who boarded; a family-assistance
protocol; standing arrangements for joining any foreign
investigation that touches its citizens. Cost of the whole
apparatus, per Hajj season, at 1991 prices: less than the catering
bill. India ran a larger pilgrim airlift through the same decades
with approximately this machinery — imperfect, scandal-prone, but
present, the state inside the operation rather than outside it.

The card was not expensive. It was institutionally impossible,
because every line presumes an agency able to impose costs on
politically connected commerce — and Chapter 3 has described what
stood in that place in 1991. The difference between the disaster
that happened and the routine season that could have happened was
one institution able to say no and make it stick: the cheapest
and rarest component in the whole machinery of flight.

## What the dead eventually bought

The learning did come — a generation late, by the indirect route,
unsigned. International law tightened the wet-lease gap: an
amendment to the Chicago Convention created machinery for formally
transferring oversight in lease arrangements, and the serious
jurisdictions built foreign-carrier assessment programs — ramp
inspections, ban lists — that treat "not our registry" as the
beginning of oversight rather than its end. The gray charter
market that had delivered its worst outcomes to its least-equipped
customers found, decade by decade, fewer places to land. And the
Nigerian Hajj itself was eventually rebuilt: the post-2006
regulator brought the charters inside real certification
discipline, the Saudis tightened admission standards, and a
permanent National Hajj Commission finally gave the operation an
institutional owner. The airlift of recent decades — flown
substantially by Nigerian carriers, on modern aircraft — is among
the reform era's least celebrated successes, because its success
consists of the absence of news.

So the dead of 2120 did purchase their reforms. What they never
received was acknowledgment — the public connection of the change
to the event that taught it. That pattern will recur at every
crash in this Part: the system learns eventually, obliquely, and
the learning arrives unsigned, never credited to the people who
paid its tuition. A country that cannot connect its reforms to
its catastrophes cannot teach its own history. Memory is not
sentiment. It is the medium in which institutional learning
either persists or evaporates.

## After

Every July, the anniversary passes with little notice in the
country the pilgrims came from. They died of arrangements designed
so that no one had the duty to notice: flags borrowed, oversight
assumed away, responsibility priced out of the contract line by
line. Flight 2120 stands first among this book's seven crashes
because it shows the pattern naked, before weather or piloting can
blur the sightline — a known deficiency, a commercial pressure, a
decision to defer, an institutional vacuum where the refusal
should have come from, and then physics, which negotiates with no
one. Everything that follows will be more Nigerian in its
mechanics: Nigerian airlines, Nigerian runways, Nigerian
regulators. Nothing that follows will be different in kind.

Fourteen months after Jeddah, the pattern repeated in a different
costume — not a leased airliner but a military transport, not
pilgrims but the officer corps of the Nigerian armed forces, and
not a foreign report unread but a domestic one never written for
the public at all.
