# Chapter 10 — Dana 992 and the Fragile Peace

The videos are still online: Sunday afternoon, 3 June 2012, the
smoke column rising over Iju-Ishaga, and the crowd — thousands
deep within minutes, filling the narrow streets, passing water in
basins, pulling at hot aluminum with bare hands and generator
extension bars while the fire found the fuel. It was the first
fully documented Nigerian air disaster: streamed, time-stamped,
uploaded by hundreds of phones that had not existed in 2005. The
information monopoly that had sealed 1992 and managed 1996 was
structurally gone. Whatever the institutions failed to learn from
Dana 992, the public would own its own archive.

The aircraft was an MD-83 of Dana Air — one of the reformed
market's successes: an Indian-owned Nigerian carrier, audited to
the international operational standard, flying a
young-for-Nigeria fleet on the trunk routes with a punctuality
reputation that had made it a business-class favorite. Flight 992
was the mid-afternoon Abuja–Lagos service, the same city pair as
Bellview 210 flown in reverse, in a country approaching six years
without a scheduled-airline catastrophe. The paperwork was in
order. What the paperwork did not show was that the aircraft
descending toward Lagos was dying.

Well before arrival, the number one engine had begun losing
thrust, starved by degrees through a fuel-supply fault. The crew
registered the anomaly and continued toward Lagos — no emergency
declared, no diversion to the nearer field behind them. On final
approach, the second engine, drawing on the same fuel system,
collapsed. An airliner without engines is a glider of brutal
arithmetic, and the arithmetic came up two miles short. The
MD-83 struck a two-story building in Iju-Ishaga and burned. All
153 aboard died, with at least six on the ground — and, as at
Kano, the true ground toll was never established to anyone's
confidence.

## The afternoon, hour by hour

The response of 2012 was the reformed system's response, and both
its differences from 2005 and its samenesses are findings.

It was faster. Lagos State's emergency agency — LASEMA, built out
of the state's own post-2005 investments — had crews moving
inside the hour; the federal agencies followed. And it was still
defeated by the two variables no siren solves: access and
equipment. The crowds that had beaten the responders to the site
now stood between them and it; Lagos Sunday traffic did the
rest; and the decisive deficit was lift — the collapsed building
needed heavy cranes, and the first crane equal to the slab
arrived after dark, hours into a fire fought with water tenders
shuttling from distant hydrants. Nobody aboard could have been
saved by speed; the recorders and the medicine established that.
On the ground, under the slab, the crane's timeline was the
negotiation — and no accounting of it was ever published.

Read the crowd correctly, because it is the era's text. Nigerians
came to the wreck because twenty years had taught them that the
state arrives late, that help is a neighbor, and that presence is
the only oversight. The crowd impeding the rescue was the
accumulated residue of every earlier chapter's failed response —
distrust, embodied, at scale, complicating the very operation it
had assembled to compensate for.

## The report's long marination

The Accident Investigation Bureau — the reform's child —
performed the forensic core of its task competently: recorders
recovered and promptly read, engines torn down to their fuel
systems, a technical narrative established that has never been
overturned. Then the report entered the institutional atmosphere
and slowed. Interim statements came; the final report did not
appear until 2017 — five years, for an accident whose essential
mechanics the investigators understood within months, with 153
families waiting, identical aircraft in national service, and a
suspended-and-restored airline flying the fleet.

When it finally emerged, the substance rewarded the wait even as
the tempo indicted it. The findings assembled a textbook
dual-failure narrative: the first engine's fuel-supply
degradation, traceable through component histories; the crew's
decision — against the logic of a sickening engine — to continue
to destination rather than land while options remained; the
second engine's collapse on final. The industry's summary
doctrine, hard-won everywhere, is taught with 992 among its case
studies: the first anomaly buys you options; spend them
immediately, because the second anomaly spends them for you.
And beneath the sequence, the organizational layers — the
maintenance decision chain for the sick engine in the preceding
days, the dispatch and fatigue ecology of a punishing shuttle
schedule, and one recommendation with this book's whole argument
inside it: measures to ensure that commercial pressure could not
override maintenance decisions. Each finding went to a named
addressee. What closed? The narrow technical items,
substantially. The systemic items entered a recommendations
register whose public trail then thins — no published follow-up
in which a citizen, a bereaved parent, or an underwriter could
look up whether the safeguards now exist in auditable form. The
dead of Iju-Ishaga purchased, verifiably, some narrow
protections, and unverifiably, everything else. That ratio is
the signature of the era this chapter names the fragile peace.

The regulatory aftermath carried the same half-reformed
signature. Dana's certificate was suspended within days — the
reformed reflex, unthinkable in 1996 — and restored within
months, amid statements that did not fully align and a durable
public conviction, fair or not, that connections had flown the
airline back onto the schedule. Suspension and restoration would
cycle more than once again before the airline's eventual
grounding a decade later. Each step was defensible on the record
available. The pattern, cumulatively, read as oversight by
oscillation: a regulator strong enough to act, no longer backed
enough to make its actions stick against appeal, litigation, and
political fatigue.

## The decade the reform aged

Four dated episodes give the plateau its texture.

**2013.** Weeks after a charter aircraft crashed at Lagos —
carrying, in a detail of almost unbearable symbolic economy, the
body of a former governor to his own funeral — the government
removed Harold Demuren from the NCAA, months from the end of his
tenure, in the reflexive accountability-theater of a crash his
agency's decade had made exceptional. The signal traveled the
system at once: tenure protection held only until tested by
embarrassment. The office has turned over with governments
since. The charter crash itself marked the reformed perimeter's
edge — an operator whose condition the audits had not caught,
in the sector where Nigerian aviation's oldest risks have always
lived.

**2016–17.** The naira collapsed, and with it the arithmetic of
every airline balance sheet: ticket revenue trapped in a falling
currency, lease and spares and insurance obligations firm in
dollars, foreign carriers' earnings marooned in Nigerian
accounts by the hundreds of millions. The state's bad-asset
manager took the two largest carriers into care. And here the
ledger must be honest in both directions: airlines died, and
their passengers did not. No return of the geriatric fleet, no
collapse of maintenance discipline, no accident cluster. The
floors built in 2006 — fleet-age limits, financial surveillance,
audited operators — held under exactly the economic pressure
that had liquefied the 1980s system. A regulatory system's
quality is measured precisely here, in what its constraints
refuse to yield when everything else is yielding.

**2020–21.** The pandemic stopped Nigerian aviation almost
entirely, then restarted it into a world of parked aircraft and
rusty crews that the global safety community warned was itself a
hazard. The restart produced incidents and no catastrophe — a
qualified pass. And in the same season, the military bill came
due again, twice in three months: a Nigerian Air Force
turboprop down on approach to Abuja in February 2021, seven
dead; another down at Kaduna in May, killing all aboard —
including Lieutenant General Ibrahim Attahiru, the serving Chief
of Army Staff, and much of his personal staff. Twenty-nine years
after Ejigbo: an air force investigating itself, findings
unpublished, a decapitated command, a civil reform whose writ
still stopped at the barracks gate. No stronger proof exists
that the recovery was a civil-institutional achievement rather
than a national transformation. The domain the reforms reached
got safe. The domain they were never allowed to reach kept its
1992 physics intact.

**2022.** The legislative machinery, at least, still converted
lessons into law: the Civil Aviation Act was renewed, and the
AIB was reborn as the Nigerian Safety Investigation Bureau — the
NSIB, with a multimodal mandate covering aviation, rail, and
marine, on the American model. On paper, the most advanced
investigative architecture in Africa. Its early record — final
reports at a tempo its ancestor never sustained, preliminary
statements within weeks on a website that works, first rail and
marine investigations, a recorder laboratory offered to the
region — suggests an institution that understands its own
probation. Whether it becomes the NTSB of Africa or merely wears
its organogram will be read, as everything in this book is read,
in its publication rate. Of all the institutions these pages
audit, it is the only one whose current decade is its best.

## What a plateau looks like

The honest ledger of Nigerian aviation after 2012 has two
columns, and both are real.

The credit column is substantial and too rarely stated: as of
this writing, Nigerian scheduled airlines have gone well over a
decade without a passenger-jet catastrophe — the longest such
run in the country's history, sustained across a doubling of
traffic, a currency collapse worse than the 1980s original,
insurgency, pandemic, and the failure of several carriers.
Nigerians under twenty-five have no memory of the annual mass
funeral that structured their parents' relationship to the
departure gate. By the only measure that finally matters, the
reform worked and kept working.

The debit column is the pattern of this chapter: five-year
reports; suspension oscillations; a charter and business-aviation
periphery under visibly thinner oversight — the sector whose
passengers are the country's most powerful people and whose
scrutiny is accordingly the most negotiated, a standing irony
this book leaves to its readers among them; a military sector
untouched and repeating; the training college and the
inspectorate straining to hold technical staff against Gulf
salaries; and, beneath all of it, the evaporation of the
political constituency that 2005–06 briefly created. The system
is no longer decaying toward 1996. It is plateaued — maintained
by professional inertia, audit exposure, and institutional
memory rather than by any active political demand. Plateaus are
respectable. But aviation is a domain of moving baselines —
traffic grows, fleets age, people churn — and a safety system
standing still is, relative to its risk base, walking slowly
backward. The fragile peace is peace. It is also fragile, and it
knows it.

## Thirteen months later, San Francisco

In July 2013, an Asiana Airlines 777 crashed on landing at San
Francisco; three people died, a modern rarity. The comparison
worth drawing is not casualty counts but metabolic rate. Within
days, the American investigator was briefing the world on
recorder data — publicly, camera-facing, hypothesis by labeled
hypothesis. Within a year: public hearings, a docket of
thousands of pages, a final report, industry-wide training
changes — the entire digestive cycle from wreckage to reform
completed inside the interval in which the Dana file was still
marinating.

That velocity is not wealth. It is design — independence,
deadlines that bind in practice, and above all a public-facing
culture in which the investigation itself is the reassurance,
its openness the product delivered. A state whose citizens can
watch it learn does not need to ask for their trust. The Lagos
crowds of June 2012 — pulling at the wreckage themselves,
trusting nothing they could not touch — were the photographic
negative of that proposition. The distance between the two
images is the remaining distance of the Nigerian reform.

## After

Part II closes where it opened, with fire in a place fire should
not be. Between Jeddah 1991 and Iju-Ishaga 2012 stretch seven
disasters, some three thousand dead across scheduled, charter,
and military flying, and a single demonstrated theorem: nothing
about the dying was necessary. The same state, the same market,
the same professionals — rearranged after 2006 into institutions
with money, autonomy, and witnesses — stopped producing
catastrophe within eighteen months, and have not resumed. The
crashes were never fate. They were policy, in the strict sense:
the predictable output of arrangements chosen, renewed, and
defensible at every point by someone whose interests they
served.

What remains, before this book can argue about the future, is to
understand the arrangements themselves — not crash by crash but
pattern by pattern: the regulator's political economy, the
economics that made cutting corners rational, the help that
never came, and the culture of silence that made every lesson
single-use. That anatomy is Part III, and its shortest summary
can be given in advance: institutions are how a society
remembers. Nigerian aviation history is what forgetting costs.
