# Chapter 14 — The Culture of Silence

Somewhere in Lagos, as this book is written, a woman in her
sixties keeps a file. It holds a boarding-pass stub, a laminated
photograph, a death certificate whose cause-of-death line reads
"air crash," and three decades of correspondence — with an airline
that no longer exists, a ministry that has been renamed twice, and
an investigation bureau that has been renamed once — asking one
question the file has never answered: *why*.

She is a composite, assembled from the documented histories of the
bereaved of Ejigbo, the lagoon, Lisa, and Kano, and her file is
the Nigerian aviation archive in miniature: grief fully
documented, causation forever pending. What she was owed, under a
treaty her country signed before she was born, was a report. This
chapter is about the reports — the ones never written, the ones
never published, the ones published a decade late — and about the
discovery, which is the whole secret of modern aviation, that a
society's safety is a function of what it permits itself to say.

## The honesty machine

Begin with how the system is supposed to work, because its design
is one of civilization's quiet masterpieces. Annex 13 to the
Chicago Convention obliges every state to investigate the
accidents on its territory, with a stated purpose the treaty makes
exclusive: prevention — "not to apportion blame or liability."
From that single sentence unfolds the entire apparatus this book
has invoked chapter by chapter: independent investigation bodies,
walled off from regulators (who may be implicated) and from
prosecutors (whose presence silences witnesses); protected safety
information — cockpit voice recordings, crew statements — shielded
from routine legal use so the system can afford to be told the
truth; public findings and recommendations, addressed to the
world. And layered above the accident machinery, the
voluntary-reporting regimes through which pilots, controllers,
and engineers confess hazards and near-misses by the hundred
thousand, unpunished, so the system can see its failures before
the wreckage stage.

The scholars of the field call the underlying bargain "just
culture": the deal in which the frontline professional trades
candor for protection from blame, and honesty becomes the
system's sensory organ. Every element is counterintuitive to the
punitive instinct, and every element is empirically vindicated.
The industries and countries that punish error into hiding fly
blind. The ones that pay for truth with immunity see everything,
and their accident curves fall for decades. Aviation safety, at
bottom, is not a technology. It is an epistemology with a budget
— a set of institutions for making failure speakable, fast, in
public, protected.

Which is why the state of a country's accident reports is not a
bureaucratic detail but an X-ray of its entire safety system.

## The Nigerian ledger

Compile the ledger and the record sorts into three strata. The
unwritten and the buried: the colonial habit — investigation as
internal correspondence — passed seamlessly to the successor
state; the 1973 Kano disaster produced no public Nigerian
account, and the military line, from Ejigbo 1992 through the twin
crashes of 2021, has never published at all. For roughly the
first four decades of independence, the default disposition of a
Nigerian accident investigation was the file, not the press.

The late: the AIB era brought real investigations — and a tempo
that defeated their purpose. ADC 086's report took years.
Bellview's inconclusive findings took most of a decade. Dana
992's final report took five years for an accident essentially
understood in months. Recall what a report is for — grounding
fleets, rewriting procedures, informing the identical aircraft
still flying — and the arithmetic of delay becomes a safety
deficit in itself: a five-year report protects nobody for five
years. Strong states also miss the treaty's twelve-month norm;
they bridge the gap with loud interim publication, so the
learning flows while the lawyers argue. The Nigerian practice —
silence, then a completed monument — preserved the form of
investigation while dissipating its function.

And the reformed, partial present: the NSIB era shows the curve
bending — reports appearing, faster, in public, on a website that
works. What has bent less is everything around the reports:
recommendations issued but never publicly tracked to closure; no
national voluntary-reporting scheme with credible protections at
scale; and a legal environment in which the just-culture bargain
has never been statutorily struck, leaving every frontline
confession discoverable — and therefore unspoken.

## What silence costs, itemized

Pin the costs to cases already in evidence.

The operational cost is measured in repetitions. Sosoliso
(December 2005) and ADC 053 (October 2006) were, at the level of
decision architecture, the same accident — a crew continuing into
convective weather that forecasts and eyes had flagged, under
schedule and prestige pressure. Ten months separated them. In a
system with a live reporting culture and rapid interim
publication, December's lesson would have been circulating in
every crew room and simulator syllabus by the next rainy season.
The interval was filled instead with mourning and process, and
the lesson arrived in Sokoto's funeral procession.

The commercial cost is priced into everything: insurance loading
(the flat "Nigeria premium" of Chapter 12 is, in part, an opacity
premium — underwriters charge for what they cannot read), lease
terms, bilateral negotiations, and passenger demand itself, which
after every unexplained event swung toward road transport, whose
fatality rate per kilometer exceeds aviation's by orders of
magnitude. Opacity, by displacing travelers onto the highways,
kills statistically even when the fleet flies clean.

And the civic cost compounds longest. Where official causation is
absent or late, explanation is supplied by the market: sabotage
theories, spiritual attribution, elite conspiracy — not Nigerian
credulity but rational inference under informational famine.
Each unexplained disaster deposits another stratum of the
reflexive distrust that Nigerian institutions now meet
everywhere, including when they tell the truth. The woman with
the file does not believe the government's account of anything.
Why would she? On the subject she knows best, it never gave her
one.

## The blame reflex

Why is silence so stable? Because the alternative to the honesty
machine is not neutrality but an older, intuitive system: blame.
In the blame system, an accident is a crime scene; the question
is not "what failed" but "who pays"; information is ammunition,
so every actor — pilot, controller, airline, ministry —
rationally armors up and shuts up; and the inquiry's real
function is the allocation of punishment and the protection of
hierarchy. Most of human history has run on the blame system.
Most institutions revert to it under stress. And a state built
on patronage runs on it structurally, because in a patronage
system responsibility is the currency, and its assignment is far
too valuable to delegate to engineers. Nigeria's silences, read
this way, were not lapses of administration. They were the blame
system defending itself: every unpublished report was a favor
owed, a faction shielded, a hierarchy preserved.

The tension is universal — France's investigators have collided
for decades with its magistrates over exactly this line, and the
safety findings emerge anyway, because the collision happens in
public between strong institutions. Where the blame system meets
no institutional counterparty, it simply wins, and the file
stays closed. The American public docket — hearings on camera,
raw evidence online, findings barred from use as legal proof of
liability — is best understood as the blame reflex's engineered
defeat, built on the wager that a democracy can be trusted to
watch its failures being understood. The wager has paid for
eight decades, and it is the cheapest reassurance technology
ever invented: the state that explains publicly does not need
to ask to be believed.

## The whistle-blower's arithmetic

Between the frontline's protected report and the public's
published record stands a third figure: the insider who sees the
systemic rot — not an error but a practice — and must decide
what speaking costs. Run his arithmetic as the Nigerian system
of the crisis decades set it. Speaking inside the organization:
the hazard report filed into the void — the controllers'
pre-1996 radar memoranda are the documented case. Speaking to
the regulator: a directorate that might be the operator's
patron. Speaking to the press: effective — the era's journalism
was the functioning accountability channel — and personally
catastrophic, in an industry with three employers and one social
network. Speaking to no one: free. The arithmetic solved itself,
and the system's information starvation was the sum of ten
thousand rational solutions.

The mature systems rebuilt the equation deliberately: statutory
whistle-blower protection with real remedies; regulator hotlines
whose confidentiality survives subpoena; and, deepest of all,
the voluntary-reporting culture that makes the dramatic
whistle-blow largely unnecessary because the undramatic report
is answered. Nigeria's whistle-blower framework, adopted with
fanfare for financial crimes, has never seriously been extended
to safety-critical industry. The professional of the 2020s still
runs a milder version of the old equation — and every silence
this book has priced was purchased at that exchange rate, which
is a statute away from repeal.

## The statute on one page

The just-culture law this chapter keeps invoking can be
sketched in its essentials, because the models are public and
the drafting is a month's work for a competent chamber. Section
one defines protected safety information: voluntary hazard
reports, flight-data monitoring records, cockpit voice
recordings, statements to safety investigators. Section two
erects the shield: protected information is inadmissible in
criminal, civil, and disciplinary proceedings against the
person who provided it — with the carve-outs that make the
bargain honest: willful misconduct, gross negligence strictly
defined, sabotage, intoxication, all fully prosecutable on
independently gathered evidence. Section three obliges the
institutions: every operator above a size threshold must run a
confidential reporting system, and the regulator must publish
annual de-identified analyses so the system's yield is visible.
Section four governs the interface with justice: a standing
protocol between investigators and prosecutors, sequencing
access to wreckage and records so that neither inquiry
contaminates the other. Section five protects the protectors:
retaliation against a reporter becomes itself an offense.

Nothing in the page prevents Nigeria from prosecuting the
genuinely criminal. Everything in it prevents the state from
taxing candor at rates that return the system to silence. What
the page requires is only a formal admission — that the state
would rather know than punish — which is the entire philosophy
of modern aviation safety in one clause, and perhaps the reason
it has waited so long in Abuja: it is the clause by which a
state declares what kind of state it intends to be.

## The archive problem

Beneath the unpublished report lies a deeper stratum: the
records themselves. The registries of the directorate era passed
through reorganizations that treated files as furniture;
veterans describe archives lost to floods, termites, and, at
sensitive junctures, convenience. The Nigeria Airways
liquidation scattered the airline's technical memory among
receivers and skips. The colonial strata survive better in
London than in Lagos: a researcher reconstructing 1950s Nigerian
aviation works in a reading room at Kew; one reconstructing the
1980s works in newspaper morgues and living memory, both
perishable. There is no national aviation archive, no systematic
oral-history program capturing the generation — now in its
seventies and eighties — that holds the only remaining record of
entire decades.

The consequence runs beyond historians' inconvenience. An
institution's capacity to learn is bounded by its capacity to
remember, and the memory infrastructure — registries, archives,
databases, librarians — is the learning system's substrate. The
NCAA that re-certified the industry in 2006 first had to
reconstruct, aircraft by aircraft, records that should have been
a query away. Every reform cycle in this history spent its first
year rebuilding a factual picture its predecessor had already
built and lost. Amnesia is expensive on a schedule: the state
has paid for its aviation memory several times and owned it
never. The remedy is unglamorous even by this book's standards —
a records statute with teeth, digitization, a national transport
archive, the oral-history project some university should have
started twenty years ago — and it is close to the argument's
center, not its periphery. Institutions are how a society
remembers. Archives are how institutions do.

## The other publishers

Where the state declined to publish, others published, and the
culture of silence is incompletely described without its
adversaries. The Nigerian press has been, across this book's
whole span, the de facto national accident archive — from the
reporters working Ejigbo's canal bank against a military censor,
to the aviation desks of the 2000s whose cumulative coverage
built the record the AIB was not building, to the digital-era
outlets whose freedom-of-information suits pried documents into
daylight after the 2011 Act gave the tradition a legal
instrument. The professional associations published in their
fashion — communiqués and strike threats saying in public what
filed reports had said in vain. The diaspora published from
beyond the reach of domestic consequences.

This counter-archive achieved real things; the rupture of 2005
ran on it. But its existence also defines the pathology's exact
shape: a society can know its disasters thoroughly — Nigerians
never lacked information about their crashes, in the newspaper
sense — while the institutional circuit of finding,
recommendation, and verified change stays broken. Journalism
generates salience, not closure. It can force the question; it
cannot sign off the corrective action. The press is the smoke
alarm of the system, and Nigeria's has been loud and brave. The
building needed sprinklers.

## After

Of all the patterns in Part III, this one costs least to break
and resists longest — because it is not a budget line but a
reflex, and reflexes are governance's deepest layer. Yet the
record offers its usual Nigerian consolation: the reflex has
been broken before, in this very industry, in this very country.
The 2006 settlement that separated the investigator from the
regulator, and the 2022 statute that deepened it, were exactly
such breaks — the blame system conceding territory to the
honesty machine, under funerary compulsion, one law at a time.
What remains is the last mile: the just-culture shield, the
tracked recommendations, the publication tempo that lets truth
arrive while it can still protect someone. None of it requires a
naira the system does not have. It requires only what the woman
with the file has been requesting for thirty years — that the
state address its citizens as an adult addresses adults: with an
account of what happened.

Part III's anatomy is complete: a regulator whose strength is
architectural and therefore maintainable; an economics that
sorts risk toward weakness and can be re-sorted; a response
deficit purchasable for pocket change; a silence that costs
nothing to end but pride. Four patterns. None of them fate.
What a country might actually do with that knowledge — and what
five other countries have done with it — is Part IV.
