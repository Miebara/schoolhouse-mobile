# PART III — THE PATTERNS

# Chapter 11 — The Regulator's Dilemma

Begin with a Friday, assembled from the sworn recollections of the
men who lived versions of it. It is Lagos, some year between 1988
and 2005 — the year hardly matters, which is the point. An
inspector of the civil aviation authority, Zaria-trained and
seasoned abroad, walks the ramp and finds an aircraft he cannot
legally release: a defect beyond deferral, a certification lapsed,
a crew out of time. He grounds it. The airline's owner — a retired
general, a party financier, a friend of the minister; the
portfolio varies, the physics does not — makes two telephone
calls. By Monday, one of three things has happened. The finding
has been overruled from above the inspector's head. Or the
inspector is "on course" in a distant city. Or — the commonest
outcome, because systems teach faster than they punish — nothing
has happened at all, because the inspector, knowing the first two
outcomes by heart, wrote the finding narrowly enough to be
waivable. The aircraft flies Monday evening. Nothing about the
weekend appears in any record an auditor will ever see.

Multiply that weekend by twenty years and you have Part II. The
question this chapter asks is not why individual Nigerians failed
to stop such weekends — many tried; some paid for it — but why
the institution was built so that trying was heroic. A regulatory
system that requires brave inspectors is already broken. The
entire art of institutional design is to make the safe finding
the cheap one.

## Capture is universal; architecture is not

Regulatory capture — the drift by which an agency comes to serve
the industry it oversees — is not an African pathology. It is the
default tendency of all regulation, named by the economist George
Stigler half a century ago and observable from Tokyo to Toulouse.
The regulated party is concentrated, informed, and permanently
interested; the public is diffuse, technically outmatched, and
interested only after disasters. The asymmetry never sleeps.

If you doubt its universality, consider the most consequential
capture scandal of the modern era, which happened in Washington.
The 737 MAX catastrophes of 2018 and 2019 — 346 dead in
Indonesia and Ethiopia — exposed an FAA that had delegated core
certification judgments to Boeing itself, under schedule pressure
from a commercial deadline, with internal warnings filed and
buried. Change the nouns and Chapter 4's soft tires inflate
themselves. Capture happens everywhere. What varies — the only
thing that varies, and therefore this chapter's whole subject —
is what happens next. After the MAX: congressional hearings with
subpoena power within months; the internal messages extracted and
printed; independent review boards; criminal settlement in the
billions; reform legislation rewriting the delegation system —
the captured system audited its own capture in public and
re-priced it. The Nigerian analogues of that aftermath, for two
decades, were a filing cabinet. Every country has these problems.
Not every country's problems get subpoenaed.

## Three regimes, one experiment

Nigerian oversight history is best read as a natural experiment
in the pricing of capture, run in three regimes.

Regime one, the directorate (1960–1999): oversight as a ministry
department — no legal personality, no budget of its own, its head
removable at pleasure, its inspectors on civil-service scales a
first officer tripled. In this architecture, capture required no
corruption, no conspiracy, not even a phone call: the regulator
was simply *inside* the political structure whose interests
conflicted with its function. The waiver culture was not a
distortion of the design. It was the design, expressing itself.

Regime two, the NCAA on paper (1999–2006): an authority created
as a formally distinct body but funded by allocation, led at
pleasure, and legally toothless against the ministry — autonomy
as letterhead. The era's record (Kano 2002, the catastrophes of
2005) demonstrates the central finding of the comparative
literature: formal independence without fiscal and tenure
independence is capture with better stationery.

Regime three, the 2006 architecture: statutory levies, tenured
leadership, published rulebook, imported witnesses. Same country,
same industry, same salary differentials, same generals'
telephones — and a measurably different output within two years.
The experiment's power lies in everything it held constant. No
cultural transformation occurred between 2005 and 2008; no
generation turned over; the officials were substantially the same
people. The architecture changed, and the Friday of this
chapter's opening began ending differently — because the Monday
overrule now had to survive a paper trail auditable from
Montreal, a Director-General who could not be quietly reassigned,
and a levy stream the minister could not pinch.

Four load-bearing members did the work, and each is worth
naming because each is exportable — and each, as Chapter 10
showed, is reversible. Funding by formula: an agency financed by
statutory levies can be disciplined only by changing the law in
public, and capture's whole comparative advantage is operating in
the dark. Tenure at the top: an office occupiable by someone
planning beyond the next reshuffle becomes worth the reputational
investment of a serious professional. Transparency as a ratchet:
a waiver granted against a published rule is a documented act; a
waiver granted against an internal circular is a conversation.
And imported witnesses: the deliberate coupling to ICAO audits,
the FAA's assessment, the industry's operational audit — which
gave the domestic regulator a constituency for its own integrity
that outranked its domestic predators. After 2010, every act of
interference carried a legible price tag denominated in Category
1: direct flights, lease rates, insurance premia, national
prestige. The reform, in effect, took the country's aviation
reputation hostage against its own political class. It is the
single most sophisticated institutional maneuver in this book.

## The ministry problem

One actor has so far escaped focus, and the regulator's history
is illegible without it: the Federal Ministry of Aviation — the
political principal above the whole alphabet of agencies, and
the channel through which every pathology in this book has, at
one time or another, flowed.

The ministry's structural situation invites its behavior. It
controls, formally or informally depending on the statutory era,
the appointments to every aviation board. It sponsors the
sector's legislation and defends its budget. It negotiates the
bilateral air services agreements that allocate international
routes — the sector's richest discretionary prize. And it owns
the ceremonial surface — the terminal commissionings and
national-carrier announcements from which political credit is
harvested. It is, in short, a machine for converting aviation
into politics, sitting directly atop the machine this chapter
wants for converting aviation into safety. The ministerial
roster tells the story in tenure: aviation ministers since 1999
have averaged barely two years — each arriving with a roadmap, a
slate of nominees, and a flagship project; each departing before
the roadmap's second budget cycle. The agencies below learned to
metabolize the turnover the way the old airline metabolized its
chief executives: by treating each minister as weather.

Serious reform proposals have therefore converged, for decades,
on a quiet structural heresy — shrink the ministry: fold aviation
into a general transport portfolio with the agencies as
autonomous authorities, on the pattern of much of the developed
world; strip the appointment and commercial powers to a
policy-only mandate; nominate professionally qualified boards
through public process. No Nigerian government has yet found a
reason to legislate away its own patronage surface, which is the
proposal's realistic prognosis in one sentence. But name the
apex predator accurately: the NCAA's deepest dilemma is not that
it faces industry, which every regulator faces. It is that its
own hierarchy is the industry's most reliable advocate.

## The inspector's market

Beneath the architecture sits a labor market, and no account of
regulatory capacity is honest without it. An airworthiness
surveyor is a licensed aircraft engineer with a decade's hangar
seniority, retrained in regulation and audit — a profile the
airlines, the lessors, the Gulf carriers, and the international
agencies all bid for, in dollars, with school fees attached. The
flight-operations inspectorate needs current airline captains,
whose price is set in Dubai. Every capable authority on earth
manages this squeeze with some mix of three tools: pay pegged to
industry rather than civil-service scales (the levy funding made
a version possible after 2006, and its erosion is among the
plateau's quietest dangers); secondment cultures that borrow
industry professionals behind conflict-of-interest firewalls;
and prestige — the intangible that lets the great regulators
hold people the market prices higher, because the work confers
standing money cannot.

Prestige is the interesting one, because it is manufactured by
exactly the things this book keeps prescribing for other
reasons: published work, visible independence, international
respect, the institution's name meaning something. A regulator
whose findings are overruled by telephone cannot pay in
prestige; one whose Category 1 the whole industry cites can. The
Demuren-era NCAA's diaspora recruitment succeeded on that
currency as much as on salaries — engineers took pay cuts to
join an institution that had begun to mean something — and every
subsequent erosion of standing raises, invisibly, the real price
of staffing the place. Institutional dignity is a compensation
line. Governments that spend it down discover the deficit in
their vacancy lists.

## The economics of saying no

Enforcement, finally, is not a decision but a budget. Follow a
single "no" through the system's accounting. The inspector's
finding costs his time; defensible documentation costs more of
it. If the operator contests, the authority pays counsel through
months or years of litigation, against firms whose lawyers bill
more per week than the inspector earns per year — while the
aircraft, under interim orders, may fly on. If the operator
escalates politically, the authority spends its scarcest
currencies: leadership attention and political capital. And if
the operator simply defies — operating while suspended is a
documented Nigerian genre — the authority discovers that its
ultimate instruments run through police, courts, and prosecutors
it does not control.

Every "no" is therefore an expenditure, and an agency's
enforcement posture is exactly as strong as its enforcement
budget. The comparative literature finds effective regulators
distinguished less by their rulebooks than by their litigation
war chests, their summary powers — fines and groundings that
take effect before appeal, not after — and their
institutionalized alliances with prosecutors. The 2006 reform
funded the Nigerian version partially: the levy stream paid for
lawyers, and the Act's summary powers survived their first court
tests. The plateau erodes it quietly: every raid on the war
chest, every settlement that rewards defiance, every appeal that
suspends a grounding shifts the industry's calculated price of
non-compliance downward. Operators price regulatory risk as
accurately as fuel. The question "is the NCAA strong?" is
answerable at any moment by a number no audit publishes: what
does defying it actually cost, in naira, this year?

## After

The men and women of the opening Friday deserve the chapter's
last word. Every system this book has anatomized was, on its
worst days, staffed by some inspector who wrote the finding
anyway, knowing the Monday that awaited it. The 2006 reform did
not create Nigerian regulatory virtue. It stopped taxing it at
confiscatory rates. That is all institutional design ever does —
arrange matters so that ordinary conscientiousness, not heroism,
is sufficient — and it is enough, and the difference it makes is
countable in the crashes that stopped. The question the plateau
poses is whether a country that built such architecture once,
under compulsion, can learn to maintain it without compulsion's
return. The next chapters examine the forces — economic,
operational, cultural — that will supply the compulsion if the
answer is no.
