# Chapter 13 — When Help Doesn't Come

Line up the scenes this book has already witnessed, because
together they form a single photograph taken five times.

Ejigbo, 1992: residents wading into a canal with ropes while the
military organizes a cordon. The lagoon, 1996: fishermen poling
through debris the state has not yet located. Lisa, 2005: a Boeing
missing for twelve hours inside the Lagos commuter belt, found by
farmers. Port Harcourt, 2005: fire tenders arriving at a burning,
survivable wreck without water. Iju-Ishaga, 2012: ten thousand
volunteers and no crane. Five different decades' worth of
aircraft, weather, and causation — and one constant, so reliable
it amounts to a national signature: in the window between impact
and institutional arrival, the Nigerian state is represented at
its own disasters by whoever happens to live nearby.

The first disaster belongs to physics. The second belongs to the
state. This chapter is about the second — and it carries a
disproportionate share of the book's argument, because emergency
response is the purest test of state capacity that exists. It
cannot be faked, deferred, or performed selectively. It is the
state's whole apparatus — communications, logistics, command,
medicine, honesty — summoned without notice, against a clock,
before witnesses. Elections can be managed and statistics
re-based. The golden hour cannot.

## The physics of the second disaster

The survivability literature, built over decades from accident
forensics, establishes a fact that reorganizes how Part II should
be read: a large fraction of transport-aircraft accidents are
survivable or partially survivable at impact. Runway excursions,
undershoots like Sosoliso, post-impact fires like Kano and
Iju-Ishaga — in these, the impact distributes survival unevenly
through the cabin, and the toll is then *negotiated*: between
fire and extinguishing agent, between trauma and the clock,
between the trapped and whoever arrives with cutting equipment.
The negotiation is over in roughly the time it takes to read
this chapter.

The international standards are calibrated to that physics. The
firefighting rules specify, for an airport of Port Harcourt's
class, response to any point of the runway within about three
minutes, with rated quantities of water and foam — because an
intact cabin resists a kerosene fire for a survivable interval
measured in low single digits of minutes. The difference between
two minutes and ten is not eight minutes. It is everyone the
fire reaches second.

Against that physics, tabulate the Nigerian record as Part II
documented it: response by neighbors, always first, never
equipped; airport fire services late, under-provisioned, or —
the finding that became a national synecdoche — dry; no drilled
interface between airport and municipal services; hospitals
receiving mass burns without burn units, learning of the influx
from the influx. Then note the detail that turns description
into indictment: every airport in this ledger was *certified*,
on paper, at a firefighting category adequate to its traffic.
The vocabulary of the standard had been purchased — the trucks
procured, the categories declared — without the substance: agent
stocks, pump maintenance, access planning, and above all drills.
Certification as costume, and response is where the costume
meets its only honest audience.

## The hospital at the end of the chain

An emergency system is a race run in stages — detection,
dispatch, access, extrication, transport, definitive care — and
its performance is the *product* of its stages: a failure at any
one multiplies through the rest. The stage least examined in
Nigeria is the last. Severe burns, aviation's signature injury,
are among the most resource-intensive conditions in all of
medicine: dedicated units, sterile environments, specialist
teams around the clock. Nigeria's burn capacity through the
crisis decades was a handful of units in two or three cities;
a mass-burn event would have overwhelmed the national
capability even if every ambulance had flown. The medical
odysseys of the Sosoliso survivors — years of reconstructive
care sourced abroad, funded by family means and public appeal —
were not exceptions. They were the system stating its capacity
honestly: the definitive-care stage for the injuries its
airports were certified to produce did not exist in-country.

And the toll of the second disaster has never actually been
counted. Kano's hospital day — simultaneous casualty waves
distributed by rumor, no register to search, blood replenished
by public appeal — was followed by a second week of deaths from
infection and delay that no study ever enumerated. Nigerian
disaster mortality is counted at the scene or not at all, which
flatters every response the statistics touch. A state that does
not count its second-week dead has decided the post-disaster
patient is the family's project.

## The archipelago

Nigeria does not lack emergency institutions. It lacks an
emergency *system*, and the distinction organizes everything in
this chapter.

Inventory the archipelago as a disaster finds it: the airport
fire service, jurisdictionally confident to the fence line and
legally uncertain beyond it. The state fire services,
chronically the most starved agencies in the federation. NEMA,
the national coordinator, created in 1999 with a mandate whose
breadth always exceeded a budget sized for press statements.
The military, possessor of the actual heavy capabilities —
engineering, airlift, divers — but constitutionally a guest at
civilian disasters, arriving through liaison channels improvised
each time. The police, who control scenes. The Red Cross and its
volunteer kin, who often outperform everyone. The hospitals,
admitting by their own rules. Every island is staffed by people
who mostly try. No bridge between the islands is load-tested
before the day it must carry a disaster — and the after-action
finding at every mass-casualty event in this book, had anyone
written after-action reports, would have been the same phrase at
the top: *interface failure*. The catastrophe falls into the
water between agencies.

One island has grown since 2005 and earns its paragraph: Lagos
State's emergency agency, built by a government wealthy and
embarrassed enough to invest, was fielding equipment and
doctrine visibly ahead of the federal layer by the Dana response,
and has handled the state's grim portfolio of building collapses
and tanker fires with accumulating competence since. It matters
analytically because it demonstrates the sub-national route:
response capacity, unlike regulatory sovereignty, is buildable
at state level, where a governor's incentives can align with it.
A serious national doctrine would formalize what Lagos
improvised — the metropolitan emergency authority as the
load-bearing unit, federally certified, locally owned.

## The offshore exception

One sector of Nigerian aviation has run, for decades, a response
and safety regime close to world standard, and its existence
inside the same country, under the same state, is this chapter's
most clarifying exhibit.

The oil and gas fields of the delta are serviced by one of the
world's denser helicopter economies — crew changes, logistics,
medevac — flown under contract to the international majors. The
flying is objectively hazardous: offshore platforms, salt
corrosion, night medevacs. Yet the sector's practices would be
recognizable in Aberdeen: aircraft with flotation gear and
emergency beacons; passengers — the roughnecks of the delta —
put through underwater-escape training as a condition of the
manifest; flight-data monitoring years before the airlines
adopted it; and a standing search-and-rescue capability,
contractually guaranteed, with dedicated aircraft and trained
crews, response times measured against the survival window of a
man in tropical water. None of this was provided by the Nigerian
state. It was specified, audited, and paid for by the customers
— the oil majors, whose liability and reputation machinery made
safety performance a condition of the contract.

The exception proves the theorems at once. The geography and
weather were never the constraint: the same delta skies that
hosted Sosoliso's undefended passengers hosted world-standard
rescue — for those whose deaths would have cost someone money.
The roughneck flew safe because an institution existed that was
structurally punished by his death. And the capability was
purchasable at Nigerian prices, on Nigerian soil, by
organizations that decided to want it. The offshore sector is a
privatized preview of the public system this book's final
chapters specify. The scandal is not that it exists. It is what
its existence certifies about everything beside it: the state
has watched, for forty years, a demonstration of exactly what
its citizens' survival would cost, and has declined, so far, to
place the order.

## The volunteer nation

Every response narrative in this book has the same first
responder — the neighbor — and a serious doctrine would stop
treating that fact as an embarrassment to be cordoned off and
start treating it as the system's largest unregistered asset.

The research literature on disasters converged long ago on what
practitioners call the first-responder myth: in mass-casualty
events everywhere — Mexico City, Kobe, the great urban
catastrophes of the record — the majority of live rescues are
performed not by professionals but by bystanders in the first
hour, before any siren arrives. The professional system's
highest-leverage move is therefore not to replace the crowd but
to equip and interface with it: community emergency-response
training on the model many countries run — light rescue, fire
suppression, triage, taught to ordinary residents in a weekend;
protocols that let an incident commander convert a crowd from
obstruction into organized labor; and the registration of
standing community assets — the fishing fleets of this book's
coastal chapters, the neighborhood associations that already
possess rosters and discipline — as auxiliary units with
call-out lists.

Nigeria is, by civic structure, almost ideally suited to the
doctrine: its neighborhoods are organized — age grades, traders'
associations, religious brigades — to a density most rich
countries lost generations ago, and its crowds have proven, from
Ejigbo to Iju-Ishaga, both willing and brave. What they have
never been offered is the weekend course, the interface
protocol, or the respect of being planned for. A state that
trained one percent of Lagos in light rescue would have fielded,
at Dana 992, ten thousand assets instead of ten thousand
obstacles — at the cost, roughly, of a ministry's annual
conference line. Of all this chapter's cheap purchases, this one
is the cheapest, because the workforce has already volunteered.
It has been volunteering, unpaid and unthanked, since September
1992.

## The doctrine on one page

Since this chapter keeps demanding a national response doctrine,
it should sketch one — the skeleton any competent drafting team
could flesh out, offered to show how little mystery the
assignment holds.

Command: one incident commander per event, designated by
pre-agreed rule — the airport authority inside the fence, the
metropolitan emergency authority outside it — with every other
agency subordinated for the duration by statute, not
negotiation. Dual command is no command, and every failed
response in this book had five. Communication: a national
mutual-aid radio protocol; contact trees tested monthly by
actual calls. Capability: firefighting substance audited
unannounced; every international airport paired by written,
exercised agreement with its municipal fire, ambulance, and
hospital network; regional caches of the heavy kit — cranes,
cutting gear, lighting — mapped and contracted in advance, so
that the Dana crane is never again sourced by telephone on the
afternoon. Casualty flow: a regulating function that assigns
patients to hospitals by capability and load; a single casualty
register opened within the hour, staffed to answer families.
Families: notification standards, a family information center at
every event, remains management under forensic protocol, a named
liaison per family — the machinery America legislated after its
own 1996 disasters, at almost no cost, which changed the civic
meaning of its accidents: the state's competence now extends to
grief. Learning: the after-action report mandatory, published,
and answered.

One page. Nothing novel. Every line already paid for somewhere
in this book.

## After

There is a version of this book's argument that counsels
despair: if crashes are symptoms of institutional depth, and
depth takes generations, the dying continues until history is
fixed. This chapter is the rebuttal. The second disaster — the
dry tender, the twelve-hour search, the undrilled airport — is
not deep. It is shallow, cheap, and separable. A country can
rescue competently long before it regulates competently, and
every life so saved is banked regardless of the deeper reforms'
fate. Run the arithmetic honestly and the response package
prices out among the cheapest life-saving investments available
to the Nigerian budget — cheaper than most health interventions,
cheaper by orders of magnitude than the terminals that outrank
it annually — because the same capacity answers the tanker
fire, the collapsed building, and the flood, and Nigeria's
calendar supplies those without pause.

If Nigerian public life took a single sentence from this book
into its next budget cycle, it should be this one: fill the
trucks, write the doctrine, run the drill. The dead of Port
Harcourt were failed twice, and the second failure was the one
any state, that year, could have declined.

What a state does after its disasters extends past the scene.
The trucks answer for the first hours. The report answers for
the years — and the report, in Nigeria, has its own history of
not arriving. That silence, the most self-inflicted of all the
patterns, is the next chapter.
