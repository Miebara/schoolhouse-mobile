# Epilogue — The Names

At the Loyola Jesuit College campus in Abuja there is a memorial
garden with sixty names, read aloud each December by students who
were not born when the aircraft fell. At Ejigbo there is a canal a
neighborhood renamed with its grief. At Lisa, a clearing the
forest has taken back. At Gwammaja, newer brick marking an old
scar line. Nigeria's aviation dead are memorialized the way this
book found everything else — privately, locally, improvisationally,
by the communities the state left holding the memory as it once
left them holding the rope at the canal's edge.

Other countries build the other kind of memorial, and the point of
a national memorial is not marble. It is the state placing itself
on record: these were citizens, this was our failure, this stone
is our receipt. Nigeria owes that receipt at a dozen sites named
in these pages, and the debt is not sentimental. A state that will
not memorialize its failures is declaring its intention to be
surprised by them again.

But the truer memorial, this book has argued from its first page,
was never stone at all. It is the published report, the tracked
recommendation, the drilled airport, the funded inspector, the
protected confession, the rule that holds on Friday against the
Monday phone call. And one name is missing from every list in
these pages, because it belongs to the future: the passenger of
some ordinary Tuesday in the 2040s whose flight lands without
incident because an inspector's finding held, because a fire
tender's pump was tested in March, because a report was published
on schedule in a year nobody remembers. She will never know her
benefactors — the reformers, the association men, the
investigators, the mothers who refused to stop asking. That is
the point of the whole enterprise. Safety's highest achievement
is a debt nobody knows they owe. This book was written so that
the debt, at least once, in at least one place, stands
acknowledged in advance.

# Appendix B — The Institutions

**NCAA** — Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority: the safety and
economic regulator. Created 1999; given real autonomy and levy
funding by the Civil Aviation Act 2006; re-chartered 2022.
**NSIB** — Nigerian Safety Investigation Bureau (until 2022 the
AIB): the independent accident investigator, multimodal since
2022. **NAMA** — the airspace agency: control, radar, navigation
aids. **FAAN** — the airports authority, including the fire
services. **NiMet** — the weather service. **NEMA** — the
national emergency agency; **LASEMA**, its Lagos State
counterpart. **NCAT, Zaria** — the training college, founded
1964. **NAHCON** — the National Hajj Commission, the airlift's
permanent owner since 2006. **AMCON** — the state's bad-asset
manager, custodian of distressed carriers from 2016. The
**professional associations** — pilots and engineers, air
traffic controllers, licensed engineers' guilds. International:
**ICAO** (standards and audits), the American **FAA** (Category
1/2 assessments), the **EU** (air safety list), **IATA**
(operational audit).

# Appendix C — Glossary

**Airworthiness** — the condition of an aircraft conforming to
its approved design and safe for flight, maintained by inspection
and certified work. **AOC** — air operator's certificate, an
airline's license. **ARFF** — airport rescue and firefighting.
**Annex 13** — the Chicago Convention's investigation rulebook:
independent, public, for prevention "not to apportion blame."
**Category 1/2** — the FAA's rating of a country's oversight;
Category 1 permits service to the United States. **CVR/FDR** —
the crash-protected cockpit voice and flight data recorders.
**Density altitude** — the performance-effective altitude after
heat is accounted for; hot air is thin air. **Go-around** —
abandoning an approach to try again or divert; modern doctrine
makes it mandatory at defined "stabilized approach" gates.
**Just culture** — the bargain protecting honest error reports
from punishment so safety information keeps flowing.
**Manifest** — the definitive list of persons aboard.
**Microburst/windshear** — a storm's concentrated downdraft and
the abrupt wind change it causes near the ground; lethal to
aircraft at low altitude. **MRO** — maintenance, repair, and
overhaul. **Plan continuation bias** — the decision trap of
pressing on as evidence mounts against the plan. **Procedural
control** — air traffic control without radar, by position
reports and timing. **Swiss cheese model** — James Reason's
image of layered defenses whose aligned holes admit the
accident. **TCAS** — the onboard collision avoidance system.
**TRACON** — the national radar program commissioned in 2010.
**USOAP** — ICAO's audit of states' oversight; its score is the
percentage of the oversight function that actually exists.
**Wet lease** — renting an aircraft with crew, maintenance, and
insurance; **dry lease**, the aircraft alone.

# A Note on Sources

The factual spine of this book rests on: official accident
reports where published (the Saudi report on Flight 2120; the
AIB/NSIB reports on ADC 086, EAS 4226, Sosoliso 1145, ADC 053,
Dana 992, and the 2013 charter); the ICAO accident digests and
the Aviation Safety Network database for cross-checking dates,
registrations, and figures; ICAO audit results, FAA assessment
records, and EU air-safety-list decisions; the Civil Aviation
Acts of 2006 and 2022 and the NSIB Act; National Assembly
records, including the committee investigation of the Nigeria
Air project; and the archives of the Nigerian and international
press — the Daily Times and West Africa for the early era; the
Guardian, PUNCH, ThisDay, Daily Trust, BusinessDay, Premium
Times, Reuters, AP, and the BBC thereafter — supplemented by the
memoir literature of Nigeria Airways veterans and military
officers, the Loyola Jesuit community's memorial publications,
and the public statements and interviews, over many years, of
the named figures in these pages.

Where a final report exists, this book follows its findings and
casualty figures, and says so. Where none was published — Kano
1973 in any Nigerian form, Ejigbo 1992, the military losses of
2021 — the text marks the absence explicitly, because the
absence is part of the argument. Nigerian casualty figures
frequently differ across sources; where they conflict, the
report's figure is used if one exists, and the conflict is
flagged for pre-publication verification against primary
documents — as are the settlement figures in the ex-workers'
saga, the corporate chronology of the successor airlines, and
the status of legal proceedings noted in Chapter 16, on which
the text takes no position.

The composite voices — Musa the controller, Ngozi of the ADC
families, Alhaji Sani of Gwammaja, Emeka the engineer, Hauwa and
Mama Bisi of the widows' and ex-workers' queues, and the keeper
of the file in Chapter 14 — are identified as composites at
every appearance. Each is constructed strictly from the
documented testimony, press interviews, petitions, and communal
records of people in their position. None is quoted saying
anything the record cannot support; no dialogue in this book is
invented; and no named real person speaks except from the
documented public record. All remaining errors are the author's.
