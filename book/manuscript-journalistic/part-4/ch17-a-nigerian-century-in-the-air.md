# Chapter 17 — A Nigerian Century in the Air?

Every forecast about Africa is a forecast about Nigeria wearing a
disguise.

Open any of the industry's twenty-year outlooks — the
manufacturers' market forecasts, the demographic tables from which
they are all ultimately derived — and the same shape emerges. The
mature aviation markets of the world are flattening. Asia's great
boom is maturing. And the steepest remaining curve on every chart
belongs to Africa — a continent whose population will approach two
and a half billion by mid-century, urbanizing at historic speed,
with a ground-transport deficit that makes it, like Indonesia's
archipelago, a geography flight does not merely serve but
completes. Within that curve, one country *is* the curve: on the
central projections, Nigeria will be the third most populous
nation on earth by the 2050s, its largest city contending for the
largest on the planet — and Nigerians today board aircraft at a
small fraction of world-average rates, which makes the growth
arithmetic almost embarrassing to type. Nigeria will fly more in
the next thirty years than in its entire previous history. That
sentence is not a prediction about policy. It is a prediction
about demography and distance, the two forces policy cannot
cancel.

The only open question — the question this book has been
assembling — is what will be waiting for all those passengers.

## Two boarding calls

Run the scenarios honestly, because both have full precedents in
the preceding chapters.

The first is 2005 at scale. Traffic multiplies faster than the
plateaued institutions beneath it. The inspector corps, already
outbid for talent, thins against a tripled movement count.
Surveillance is a coverage function, and coverage failures
compound the way the response stages of Chapter 13 multiplied —
so the margin the reform era banked is drawn down at a rate set
by traffic growth itself, annually, silently, in exactly the
pattern every chapter of Part II began with. Somewhere in the
2030s, the arithmetic resumes, now with four-hundred-seat
aircraft and a media environment that will make the crowds of
2012 look quaint. Nothing exotic is required for this future.
Only the plateau, extended, while the denominator grows.

The second is Indonesia completed — or, at full stretch, Ethiopia
begun. The page of Chapter 16 executed. The growth coalition
assembled — the carriers, lessors, hub ambitions, and diaspora
capital that all get richer as the system gets safer. Lagos
competing seriously for the West African hub: geography has dealt
Nigeria a hand it has never played, a natural catchment of four
hundred million people within two hours' flying, positioned for
the Atlantic crossings — a prize currently distributed among
Addis Ababa, Istanbul, Dubai, and Lomé, each carrying Nigerian
traffic that Nigerian institutions forfeited: in the flag
carrier's collapse, in the bilateral giveaways of the lean
decades, in every audit cycle that priced Nigerian operations
above their neighbors'. Hubs concentrate maintenance bases,
training academies, cargo logistics, and the high-wage technical
employment this book has called the promise-keeping economy. The
hub question is the whole book restated as economics: the prize
goes to the jurisdiction whose system can be trusted at scale,
and systems are what this history has been about. Ethiopia built
one on a fraction of Nigeria's endowment. The endowment waits.

Between the scenarios stands no mystery, no missing knowledge, no
unpriced technology — only the arrangements of Part III and the
agenda of Chapter 16, all of it demonstrated feasible on Nigerian
soil within living memory. Rarely has a national choice been so
completely specified in advance, with both branches already
field-tested on the same population. And the choice will be made
the way every choice in this book was made: not once,
dramatically, but continuously — in appointment letters and
appropriation lines and the disposition of report drafts — by
people most of whom will never think of themselves as having
chosen anything.

## The new weather

Two forces absent from this book's history will shape its sequel.

The first is technology's changed direction. For the century this
book covered, aviation technology flowed one way — designed in
the strong-institution world, absorbed later and older everywhere
else: the sorting machine. The coming decades scramble the
pattern at the edges. Unmanned systems are already outrunning the
regulatory apparatus built for crewed flight, and their accident
physics — cheap, numerous, low-altitude, over cities — will
stress exactly the capacities this book found thinnest. Electric
and hybrid regional aircraft, if their economics mature, suit the
African thin-route problem almost perfectly and will arrive as
*new* technology available to first movers rather than
hand-me-downs — an inversion of the entire second-hand history
this book has told. And the data revolution — satellite
surveillance ending the radar-coverage problem outright,
flight-data streaming ending the lost-recorder problem — cheapens
precisely the infrastructure whose procurement sagas filled
Chapter 6. None of it self-installs; each lands, or fails to, on
the same institutional soil as everything else. But the soil
requirements have dropped, and for the first time the frontier is
affordable at Nigerian budget levels. The excuse inventory
shrinks annually.

The second force is climate. Aviation's carbon politics will
constrain the industry's growth everywhere, and Africa's position
in that bargaining — one percent of historical aviation
emissions, the steepest legitimate growth need — will be a
defining diplomatic file to which Nigeria, as the demographic
principal, holds the continent's proxy whether it prepares or
not. Meanwhile the operational climate itself turns hostile in
exactly the dimensions this book's crashes made familiar: the
convective weather of Sosoliso and Flight 053 intensifying on
every credible projection, the coastal runways of Lagos sitting
meters above a rising lagoon. The windshear decision that killed
a Sultan will be presented to Nigerian crews more often in 2050
than it was in 2006. Whether it kills again is, as ever, not a
weather question.

## The generation

The people who will run the Nigerian century in the air are alive
now, mostly under thirty, and they arrive with assets no previous
Nigerian aviation generation possessed. They arrive into a
functioning core: the cadet who joins the industry in the 2020s
enters a system with radar, a real regulator, published
regulations, and a domestic safety record her instructors could
not have imagined at her age — a baseline, not a battlefield.
They arrive networked: the diaspora that drained the 1980s now
functions, in the era of cheap connection, as an accessible
faculty — the training captain in Doha mentoring Lagos cadets by
video, the associations' webinars erasing the isolation in which
the 1990s workforce fought its lonely arguments. They arrive
with the archive in their pockets: the smartphone-documented,
freedom-of-information, published-report information environment
means the next generation's version of this book's history is
checkable from a dormitory — and a generation that can check is
harder to govern by liturgy.

What they do not arrive with is the memory. The last Nigerians
who watched the whole arc — Zaria's founding, the boom's waste,
the canal, the lagoon, the school, the reform — are leaving the
industry and the world. Memory does not transfer by inheritance.
It transfers by institution — the archive, the curriculum, the
memorial, the annual hearing — or it does not transfer at all,
and Part III measured how little of that machinery exists. The
gravest risk this generation faces is not the plateau, the
naira, or the ministry. It is inheriting quiet skies without the
invoice — mistaking, as their grandparents' politicians did in
1975, the artifacts for the system. The remedy is the one this
book has tried to be: the history, written down, argued,
sourced, and put where the cadets can reach it.

## The case against hope, answered

It is customary for books like this one to end in optimism, and
the custom has usually embarrassed the books. So state the
strongest version of the pessimist's case first. Every argument
this chapter has made from demography and interest was equally
available in 1960, in 1977, in 2010 — and each time the
structural tailwinds blew, and each time the institutional sails
were not raised, or were raised and then struck. The pessimist
does not deny that reform is possible. He observes that in sixty
years it has been sustained for roughly eight, and asks why the
base rate should not govern the forecast.

Three things have changed that the base rate does not price.
First, the information environment: every prior relapse happened
substantially in the dark, and the plateau of the 2020s is the
first to occur under continuous public documentation — decay that
must happen in daylight happens slower and costs more. Second,
the interest structure: for the first time, the growth arithmetic
makes safety directly and visibly profitable to actors with power
— fleets financed on covenants that price the system's integrity,
a hub prize that is contested and lucrative, an industry in which
someone powerful now loses money when it rots. Third, the
demonstration stock: 1960's optimists had theory; 2010's had one
turnaround; today's have a shelf — Nigeria 2006, Indonesia,
Korea, Ghana, the offshore enclave — of executed, documented,
imitable repairs, including two performed by Nigerians on
Nigerian institutions. Base rates govern systems whose parameters
are static. The pessimist's pattern was real, and its parameters
are moving — not guaranteeing the second act, but repricing it
from miracle to decision.

## Seat 14A, again

Return, finally, to the evening flight of the prologue — Abuja to
Lagos, the darkened middle country scrolling beneath the wing,
the orange horizon of the delta, the improvised electric sea of
Lagos rising. The view has not changed. The question has. It was:
how did a nation that cannot keep its lights on keep its
airplanes up? The book's answer is now assembled. Because, for
one compelled interval, it built arrangements — funded, tenured,
witnessed, published — that let its ordinary competence
accumulate. And everything before that interval — the whole
broken-winged chronicle of Kano and Ejigbo and the lagoon and
Lisa and Port Harcourt and Iju-Ishaga — is what the same
competence produced when the arrangements forbade it to
accumulate. Institutions are how a society remembers. They are
also — it is the same sentence — how a society intends.

Somewhere below the wing, in the dark between the cities, are
the places this book has visited: unmarked, most of them,
privately mourned, publicly forgotten. And ahead, past the
seatbelt chime and the humid jetbridge air, is the century in
which more Nigerians will fly than all the dead of this history
ever dreamed — schoolchildren to Port Harcourt at Christmas,
pilgrims to Jeddah in the white of ihram, traders to Kano, the
whole nation airborne at last, as the founders' flypast promised
in 1960. They will board trusting, as passengers everywhere
trust, that the system beneath them is being maintained by
people they will never meet, against pressures they will never
see, in offices they will never enter.

That trust is either the next chapter of the reform or the first
chapter of another book like this one. Nigeria has now run both
experiments and knows both prices. Broken wings mend — in
workshops, by method, on schedules, every day. The question, the
only question there ever was, is whether the mending is treated
as an event to be commemorated, or as what it has always
actually been: a discipline — daily, unglamorous, and owed.
