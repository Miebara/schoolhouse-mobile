# Chapter 16 — Rebuilding Trust

The reform agenda for Nigerian aviation fits on one page. It has
fit on one page for thirty years. The page is not the problem.

Every element of what follows has appeared in some ministerial
roadmap, audit response, or national aviation policy since the
1990s; several elements exist in law today. This chapter's
purpose is therefore not discovery but specification — each item
stated with its mechanism, its cost, its owner, and its
predictable enemy, because an agenda without named enemies is a
communiqué. And it opens with the era's great cautionary tale:
the project that consumed a decade of the ministry's attention
while the page went unexecuted.

## The parable of the successor airlines

Nigeria Air was not the first attempt to conjure a successor to
Nigeria Airways. It was the fourth, and the genealogy deserves
setting out, because four failures on one template constitute a
dataset.

The first came before the old airline was formally dead. Around
2001, with Nigeria Airways in terminal decline, the aviation
minister of the day, Kema Chikwe, championed the flotation of a
new national carrier — announced under the name Nigeria Global —
that was to rise alongside and replace the dying flag. The
project was launched with ministerial fanfare, acquired the
trappings of an airline — a name, designated aircraft
arrangements, inaugural ambitions on the London route — and never
became a functioning carrier: it dissolved within a couple of
years amid controversy over its leasing arrangements and
funding, leaving behind legislative queries and a précis of
every error the later attempts would repeat. The successor
airline willed into being from the ministry outward; symbol
first, system never.

The second attempt was the era's most serious. Virgin Nigeria,
launched in 2004–05 as a joint venture with Virgin Atlantic —
the British carrier supplying management, brand, and operational
standards, a deliberate borrowing of external institutional
quality on the Kenya Airways model — flew, and for a period flew
well. Then the arrangement met the Nigerian political economy:
disputes over terminal allocation, friction over brand and
control, the exit of Virgin, rebranding as Nigerian Eagle and
then Air Nigeria under new private ownership, and collapse into
unpaid salaries and grounding by 2012. Rented institutional
quality works only while the landlord state honors the lease.

The third was a succession of paper carriers and announcements
through the 2010s that never reached a runway. And the fourth
was Nigeria Air itself — the project of Hadi Sirika, minister of
state and then minister of aviation across the Buhari years, and
its most determined champion. Sirika unveiled a livery at the
Farnborough Air Show in July 2018; suspended the project within
months; revived it; selected Ethiopian Airlines as strategic
partner — the mirror of Chapter 15, summoned now as vendor —
over the objections and litigation of the domestic carriers'
association; and staged the launch of May 2023: a chartered
Ethiopian aircraft in Nigeria Air paint, flown in for the
cameras days before the administration left office and flown
home after them. The House of Representatives committee that
investigated the project rendered its verdict in the harshest
available parliamentary language. The succeeding administration
suspended the project. Anti-corruption authorities later brought
charges connected to the ministry's projects, contested in court
as this book went to press, on which it takes no position.

What the record already supports is the structural verdict: four
attempts, four ministries, one method — the airline summoned
from the ministry, capitalized in announcements, and delivered
to a state that had still not performed the unglamorous repairs
beneath it. A state with a plateaued regulator, an unfinished
investigative reform, dry fire tenders in its audit findings,
and a training college losing instructors chose to spend its
scarcest resources — ministerial attention, political capital,
credibility — on the one aviation asset with no safety yield
whatsoever: the flag. The counter-model exists and is
unglamorous — Chile, the Gulf, post-liquidation Ghana — and the
trade has a name for it: fly the flag on the certificate, not
the fuselage. Let the state's insignia mean rigorous oversight,
and let the market own the metal. The applause line the
politicians keep searching for is Chapter 10's: a decade without
funerals.

## The agenda

**One. Finish the investigator.** The NSIB has the statute; give
it the substance: a statutory publication clock — interim
statement within thirty days, public docket within a year; a
recommendation register, public, in which every addressee must
answer within ninety days (accepted, funded, dated — or refused
with reasons); and the just-culture shield of Chapter 14. Cost:
trivial. Owner: the bureau's board and the National Assembly.
Enemy: the blame system — that is, everyone with something
pending. This is the keystone: cheap, compounding, and the
honesty of every other item depends on it.

**Two. Ring-fence the regulator — again, permanently.** The 2006
settlement has eroded at the margins: treasury consolidation
drives eyeing the levies, appointment politics reclaiming the
leadership. Restore and entrench: levies protected at the
highest legal altitude; the Director-General appointed from a
professionally qualified shortlist to protected tenure;
technical salaries benchmarked to the market that actually bids
for inspectors — the Gulf and the agencies, not the civil
service scale. Cost: self-funding. Enemy: every future minister
of aviation, structurally; the item exists to outlive them.

**Three. Fill the trucks, run the drills.** Chapter 13's
package, verbatim: firefighting substance audited unannounced at
every certified airport; one full-scale mass-casualty exercise
per international airport per year, hospitals in the loop,
results published; a national rescue coordination center with a
tested contact tree; manifest integrity enforced digitally; a
family-assistance act on the American model. Cost: pocket change
against any terminal project. Enemy: nobody — which is why it
starves; assign a single accountable coordinator by statute.

**Four. Keep capital cheap by keeping promises legible.** Full
performance of the aircraft-finance treaty Nigeria ratified —
repossession timelines guaranteed through a designated court
list, registry operations at world standard — recapturing the
financing discount for every Nigerian lessee; transparent slot
and route allocation so carriers compete on operations rather
than access. Cost: administrative. Payoff: basis points on every
financed airframe — the cheapest fleet-safety money that exists.

**Five. Consolidate without cartelizing.** Higher capital floors
and honest mergers, paired with continental open skies so that
consolidation's pricing power is disciplined by competition
rather than by a regulator who will lose that fight — and
resolution planning for the carriers now too big to ground, so
that the safety decision is never again hostage to the schedule.

**Six. Bring the military inside the perimeter.** The unfinished
chapter from Ejigbo to Attahiru: an independent military
airworthiness authority on the British model — inside the
defence establishment, outside the operating chain of command —
with published mishap summaries as its minimum transparency
floor. Enemy: the deepest in the book, the informational
sovereignty of the barracks. Sequence it: the transparency floor
first. No Nigerian aviation reform is complete while the state's
own aircraft answer to nobody.

**Seven. Rebuild the pipeline.** Zaria re-equipped against a
dated fleet-and-simulator plan; instructor pay unhooked from the
civil service; a national cadetship financing scheme so the
pilot pipeline stops depending on family wealth. The largest
cost line in the agenda, still small against one year of fuel
subsidy. Enemy: nobody — pipelines have no constituency until
they are empty.

**Eight. Publish everything.** Registers, audit outcomes,
inspection results, enforcement logs — machine-readable, by
default. Transparency is the only regulator that works weekends.

**Nine. Manufacture the constituency.** The binding constraint
throughout this book has been the absence of anyone to demand
the rest between funerals. So build the demand: an annual,
statutory State of Aviation Safety report debated in public
session with the agency heads on the record — importing, on a
fixed calendar, the scrutiny that currently arrives only by
catastrophe; a standing consumer-and-victims' advocate with
docket standing — the institutionalized descendant of the
Loyola parents; and continued deliberate coupling to the
external witnesses whose audits remain, until domestic demand
matures, the system's rented spine.

## What it costs, and who holds the pen

Sum the program generously and it remains below the published
cost estimates of the stillborn national carrier's launch —
below, for a sharper comparison, the value of the foreign
airlines' funds trapped in a single bad year of the currency
queue. Against it stand the quantified returns this Part has
assembled: the treaty discount on every financed airframe, the
insurance re-rating that follows investigative transparency, the
route economics of durable Category 1 — and the unquantified
one, the funerals not held. Nigerian aviation reform is not an
expenditure competing with schools and clinics. It is among the
highest-yield fiscal instruments the federation owns, mispriced
for sixty years because its dividends are paid in absences.

As for the pen: the presidency owns what nothing else can move —
appointments, the military perimeter, air cover; its payoff is
the most reliably completable monument in Nigerian politics. The
National Assembly owns the statutes and the annual hearing; a
chairmanship that produces a passed just-culture act is a
national profile. The industry owns the commercial items and is
the natural financier of the missing advocacy — an airline trade
association lobbying for system quality would be pursuing its
own lease rates by the most direct route. The professions own
the content, and their associations — which crossed every
collapse in this book intact — are the reform constituency's
permanent skeleton. And the families own the conscience. Every
reform in this history moved when those five hands briefly held
the same pen. Keep a chair at the table for the funerals'
representatives, so that the table never again needs new ones.

## The first hundred days, and the ways it fails

For concreteness, the opening program of a reforming
administration, sequenced for momentum: days one to thirty,
publish — the registers, the recommendation ledger, the
inspection results, imperfect data now rather than perfect data
promised, plus one unannounced firefighting audit per
international airport, results public. Days thirty to sixty, the
clocks — interim-statement timelines agreed and dated, the
backlog scheduled, the first quarterly safety briefing taken on
camera. Days sixty to one hundred, the entrenchments begun — the
prepared bills transmitted while the honeymoon lasts, the
pay-parity mechanism announced, the full-scale exercise
commissioned and attended. None of this completes anything. All
of it makes reversal visible and therefore expensive, which the
Demuren tenure taught is the whole art of the opening.

And the failure modes, named so their arrival is recognized: it
fails by consolidation of the wrong kind — the treasury raid
dressed as fiscal discipline, sweeping the levies into the
consolidated fund and re-creating the begging regulator within a
budget cycle. It fails by success — the quiet decade lulling the
coalition it should recruit, the annual hearing degenerating
into congratulation, the drills becoming pageants. It fails by
federal drift — the reform captured at the center while the
periphery (charters, general aviation, the drone economy) grows
faster than oversight reaches; the 2013 and 2021 losses were
peripheral failures in exactly this sense. And it fails by
personalization — the reform identified with a champion and
falling with his career, the known vulnerability of the
2006 design. A reform is finished not when its champion wins but
when its champion becomes replaceable without event. The honest
summary of the risk register: the engineering is solved, the
politics is unsolved, and the politics is the project.

## After

Skeptics will note, correctly, that Nigeria has seen agendas
before. The rebuttal is this book's own Part II: the 2006
settlement was exactly such an agenda, enacted under compulsion,
and it worked — measurably, quickly, for years. The question was
never capability. It was whether the enactment energy could be
found without the coffins. This chapter's wager is that
specification helps: an agenda with owners, costs, clocks, and
named enemies is harder to dissolve into communiqué than a
roadmap of aspirations.

What trust means, operationally, is four inferences a passenger
can make: the people operating this machine are qualified by an
examiner who cannot be bought; the machine is maintained on a
schedule someone independent verifies; if something goes wrong
tonight, trained help will arrive inside the survival window;
and if the worst happens, the cause will be found, published,
and acted on. Four inferences, each resting on an institution,
each checkable in principle. The Nigerian public of 1996 could
make none of them. The public of the quiet decade can make
perhaps two and a half — which is why its trust remains
provisional, cheap to shake, and expressed in the dark humor
that has always been the Nigerian passenger's actuarial
instrument. The agenda above is a machine for making the four
inferences severally true and severally visible. Trust is also
infrastructure — the only kind that compounds, the only kind
that cannot be commissioned with a ribbon, and the only kind
whose construction schedule is entirely, permanently, within
the state's own control.
