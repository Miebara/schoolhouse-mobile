# A Note on Method

This book is written in the manner of reportage, because that is how
its story deserves to be told: on the ground, close to the people who
lived it. Honesty requires me to tell you exactly how it was
assembled.

The factual spine of every chapter — dates, flight numbers, casualty
figures, the findings of investigations, the actions of governments —
rests on the public record: official accident reports where they
exist, the archives of the Nigerian and international press, treaty
and audit documents, legislative hearings, and the memoirs and
on-the-record public statements of participants. Where that record is
thin or contradictory, the text says so. A chapter-by-chapter account
of sources sits at the back of the book, with flags marking every
figure that must be verified against primary documents before
publication.

The voices are of two kinds, and the text always tells you which you
are hearing. Real, named public figures — ministers, regulators,
survivors who chose public life — appear in these pages doing and
saying only what the documented record shows them to have done and
said; nothing spoken by a named real person in this book is invented.
The second kind of voice belongs to the people history does not
name: the controller on the night shift, the widow in the pension
queue, the fisherman who rowed out to the wreckage. Where such a
person speaks in these pages, they are a composite — a reconstruction
drawn from the testimony, letters, press interviews, and communal
memory that the record preserves — and they are introduced with the
words "I will call him" or "I will call her." No such composite says
anything the record cannot support. They exist because the
alternative was to let the nameless remain voiceless, and this book
was written against exactly that.

This is, in other words, a work of documentary reconstruction, built
with a reporter's tools on an archive's foundations. The dead
deserved a book that reads like witness. They also deserved one that
does not lie about what it knows. I have tried to write both at
once.

# Prologue — The Evening Flight

The 6:45 to Lagos pushes back from the Abuja stand a little after
seven, which everyone aboard silently expected. A civil servant in
the window seat at row 14 closes his phone against a final call from
the ministry. Two rows ahead, a trader from Onitsha settles a
carry-on stuffed beyond argument into the bin. The cabin lights
dim; the engines rise; the capital tilts away below — the ring
roads, the green-lit minarets, the blue constellation of the
taxiways — and then, somewhere over Niger State, the country goes
dark.

Not dim. Dark. For twenty minutes the most populous nation in
Africa slides beneath the wing as pure absence, an unlit ocean, until
the horizon begins to burn orange with the gas flares of the delta
and the vast improvised electric smear of Lagos rises to meet the
descent. Twenty-four million people down there are making light the
way a fire makes light: brilliantly, unevenly, mostly off the grid.

I have watched that view more times than I can count, and somewhere
over the years I stopped looking at the country and started looking
at the machine. The aircraft carrying us was assembled in Seattle or
Toulouse. It was financed out of Dublin, insured through London,
maintained to standards written in Montreal, flown by a crew whose
licenses were examined against rules agreed by a hundred and ninety
governments. It was, in effect, a small embassy of the international
system, on loan to a country that has struggled since independence
to build durable institutions of its own. And every passenger
aboard, whether they thought about it or not, was betting their
life that the embassy's rules — not the country's habits — would
govern for the next fifty-five minutes.

For most of my lifetime, that bet was genuinely uncertain.

Between 1991 and 2012, Nigeria suffered a sequence of air disasters
extraordinary even by the standards of a turbulent continent. A
chartered pilgrimage flight burned in the sky over Saudi Arabia with
261 people aboard. A military transport fell into a Lagos canal
carrying much of a generation of officers. Airliners went down in
the lagoon, in the forest, into city streets. In one fourteen-month
stretch the country buried the dead of three mass-casualty crashes,
among them sixty schoolchildren from a single school in Abuja whose
parents I will meet again and again in these pages, because their
refusal to stop asking questions changed this story's ending.

And then — this is the part that made me want to write the book —
it stopped. After 2006, Nigeria did the thing its critics insist it
never does. It reformed. It rewrote the law, armed the regulator,
re-examined every airline from zero, and in 2010 passed the American
government's audit of its safety oversight, joining the short list
of African states certified to the world standard. As I write, the
scheduled airlines of Nigeria have not suffered a mass-casualty jet
crash since June 2012. An entire generation of Nigerians has now
grown up without the annual mass funeral that structured their
parents' relationship to the departure gate.

The chapters that follow move through that arc the way a reporter
moves through a story: scene by scene, person by person. Part I
goes back to the beginning — to the biplanes over Kano in 1925, the
flying elephant of Nigeria Airways, the oil boom that bought a
fleet and forgot to buy the institutions beneath it. Part II
walks the wreckage: seven disasters in two decades, each
reconstructed from its record, each asking the same quiet question
— what, exactly, failed here, and who decided it could wait? Part
III steps back to the patterns: the captured regulator, the
economics that made cutting corners rational, the help that never
came, the reports that were never published. Part IV asks what
comes next, in the country that will fly more in the next thirty
years than in its whole previous history.

Two convictions organize everything. The first is that none of the
dying was fate. Every crash in this book was built — assembled over
years, in offices, out of deferrals that were individually rational
and collectively lethal. The second is that the quiet decade was
built too, by identifiable people making identifiable choices, most
of whom have never been thanked. Nigerians are told constantly that
their country cannot work. The strange, hopeful, insufficiently
told story of Nigerian aviation is that for one hard-won stretch,
in one unforgiving industry, it did.

The seatbelt chime sounds. Lagos fills the window. Somewhere below,
in the dark between the cities, are the places this book will
visit: a canal at Ejigbo, a clearing near a village called Lisa, a
neighborhood in Kano called Gwammaja, a school garden in Abuja with
sixty names carved in stone. The aircraft banks, and holds, and
lands — an ordinary miracle, purchased at a price this book exists
to make visible.

# The People in This Book

A reader's aid: the recurring figures, real and composite.

**Real, on the record:** Harold Demuren, the MIT-trained engineer
who ran the Nigerian Civil Aviation Authority through its reform
years, 2005–2013. Kema Chikwe, aviation minister 1999–2003, who
attempted the first successor to Nigeria Airways. Hadi Sirika,
aviation minister of the Buhari years, champion of the Nigeria Air
project. Kechi Okwuchi, one of two survivors of Sosoliso Flight
1145, whose public testimony anchors Chapter 8. The Sultan of
Sokoto, Muhammadu Maccido, who boarded ADC Flight 053 on 29 October
2006. Presidents Obasanjo, Yar'Adua, Jonathan, and Buhari, in whose
tenures the story turns.

**Composite, and identified as such wherever they speak:** Musa, a
retired Lagos-area air traffic controller. Ngozi, whose husband
died aboard ADC Flight 086 in 1996. Alhaji Sani, a Gwammaja trader
who dug his neighbors out on 4 May 2002. Emeka, a former Nigeria
Airways licensed engineer who joined the exodus to the Gulf.
Hauwa, a widow of the Ejigbo C-130. Each is built strictly from
the documented testimony of people in their position; none says
anything the record cannot support; all invented names are marked
at first use.
