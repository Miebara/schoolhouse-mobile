## 20. Why It Is Hardest to Leave When You Have Given Most

### *Effort Justification, the Sunk Cost Fallacy, and the Endowment Effect*

Dobelli's sunk cost chapter has the cleanest instruction in his whole book: **the
money and time you have already spent is gone, and it is irrelevant to what you
should do next.** The only rational input is the future. He gives the standard
cases: sitting through the second half of a bad film because you paid for the
ticket, holding a collapsing stock because of what you paid for it, continuing a
doomed project because of the years invested. The strength of the pull is
proportional to what has been sunk, which means it is strongest exactly where the
losses are already largest.

Effort justification is the same mechanism running forward, and it is the more
important of the two here. The founding experiment is Aronson and Mills, 1959.
Women were admitted to a discussion group after an initiation that was either
mild or genuinely embarrassing. Those who endured the severe initiation rated the
group — a deliberately dull discussion of the sexual behaviour of animals — as
significantly more interesting and valuable. The reasoning, which is not conscious,
runs: I would not have gone through that for something worthless, therefore this
is valuable. **Cost creates value retroactively.** Dobelli notes the commercial
version, the IKEA effect: we overvalue what we have assembled ourselves.

Now look at what religious practice extracts, and notice that it is
extraordinarily well designed as an effort-justification engine — again, not by
design, but by selection, because the traditions that asked nothing did not
persist.

The initiations are costly and public: baptism by immersion in front of the
congregation, confirmation, bar mitzvah after a year of study, the shahada spoken
before witnesses, the year-long convert course. Ramadan is a month without food or
water in daylight. Lent, Yom Kippur, Orthodox fasts. The hajj costs a year's
income for many pilgrims. Then the routine payments: ten percent of gross income,
for life. One day a week. Two hours of every Sunday for fifty
years — approximately two and a half years of continuous waking life, spent in one
building. Add missionary service: two years of a young Mormon's life, at their own
family's expense, cold-calling strangers who are largely rude to them. The
correlation between how much a tradition demands and how tightly it retains its
members is one of the best-established findings in the sociology of religion, and
it runs in the direction effort justification predicts: the demanding groups grow
and hold, the undemanding ones bleed members. That relationship makes no sense if
belief drives commitment. It makes perfect sense if commitment drives belief.

The sunk cost fallacy then locks the door. Consider a woman of sixty-eight who has
attended for fifty years. What is invested? Roughly a hundred thousand pounds in
tithes. Two and a half years of waking life. Every close friendship she has. A
marriage built on shared faith. Children raised in it, and grandchildren she
prays with. Career decisions made on the basis of a calling. Her own testimony,
given publicly, many times. And a self-description — a woman of faith — that is
not an opinion she holds but the load-bearing wall of who she is.

For her to conclude now that it was not true is to accept a loss of a magnitude no
person can easily absorb. Every incentive in her psychology, and every mechanism
in Dobelli's book, is arranged to prevent it. And notice the cruelty of the
gradient: the more sincerely and expensively someone has served, the more
impossible honest reconsideration becomes. **The people best placed to evaluate
the claim — those with fifty years of data — are the people least able to.** This
is why deconversion clusters heavily among the young and among people who have
recently moved, lost a community, or otherwise had their sunk costs
involuntarily written off.

Dobelli's endowment effect supplies the last piece. We value what we own more than
the identical thing unowned; the classic demonstration gives half a group a mug
and finds that owners demand roughly twice what buyers will pay. Religious belief
is spoken of, always, in the possessive: *my* faith. Notice that grammar. Nobody
says "my belief that water is H₂O" — beliefs about the world are not owned, they are
just held, and if they turn out wrong you have lost nothing but an error. But a
faith is a possession, and the endowment effect means that surrendering it
registers as a loss of property rather than as the correction of a mistake. Losses
hurt twice as much as gains please, per Chapter 12. So the arithmetic of leaving is
weighted against it before any evidence is considered.

There is one honest complication, and it is the sunk cost argument turned back on
me. Someone might say: by your own logic, the years I have spent are gone whether
I stay or leave, so why should I incur the pain of leaving? Sunk costs are
irrelevant in *both* directions. That is correct, and it is exactly the right
question, and it is what Chapter 24 and Chapter 26 are for. The case for leaving
cannot rest on recovering what was wasted — it is unrecoverable. It has to rest on
what the remaining years are worth, which is a live decision and the only one you
actually have.

**In conclusion:** ask the question that strips out the sunk costs entirely.
*Knowing what I know now, if I were twenty-two and encountering this for the first
time, with no friendships in it, no money in it, no testimony given, and nothing
to lose — would I join?* Answer it honestly, in writing, and notice that the answer
you give has nothing to do with what you have already spent. That answer is the
only relevant one. Everything else on the scale is a receipt.
