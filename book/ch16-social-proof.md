# Part Three — The Pew as a Machine

*Part Two argued that the reasons for belief are reasoning errors. Part Three is
about the room. A church service is not a neutral venue in which those errors
happen to occur; it is an arrangement of chairs, sounds, money and social
consequences that reliably produces and maintains them. If you designed a
building to make one particular question unaskable, you would end up with
something very close to what already exists — not by conspiracy, but by two
millennia of selection for whatever kept people coming back.*

---

## 16. If Fifty Million People Say Something Foolish

### *Social Proof*

Dobelli's chapter title is borrowed from W. Somerset Maugham — if fifty million
people say something foolish, it is still foolish — and it covers the error he
considers the most socially powerful of all: **we take the behaviour of others as
evidence about reality.** Not as a heuristic we consciously apply, but as a direct
input to perception.

The canonical demonstration is Solomon Asch's, from 1951. Subjects were shown
lines of obviously different lengths and asked which matched a reference line.
Alone, error rates were about one percent. Seated among confederates who
confidently gave the same wrong answer, about three-quarters of subjects
conformed at least once, and a third conformed consistently. They were not lying
to fit in — many reported that they had come to *see* the lines differently.
Dobelli's other examples are the canned laughter that makes a joke funnier, the
half-full restaurant we avoid for the crowded one, and the bystander effect, where
the presence of others who are not helping is read as evidence that no help is
needed.

Now describe a church service in these terms, without any theological vocabulary
at all.

Between fifty and five thousand people are seated facing one direction. They rise
at the same moment. They sit at the same moment. They speak identical sentences
in unison, at a shared cadence, from memory. They sing together — for twenty or
thirty minutes, in some traditions much longer — which entrains breathing and
heart rate across the group; the physiological synchronisation of group singing is
well documented and produces measurable oxytocin release and pain-threshold
increases. Many of them raise their hands, close their eyes, or kneel at the same
time. And every single one of them appears entirely certain.

This is Asch's experiment, run for ninety minutes, weekly, for your entire life,
with music. And unlike Asch's line task — where the truth was visible on the card
in front of you — the proposition here is invisible, unfalsifiable, and impossible
to check independently. Asch's subjects abandoned the evidence of their own eyes
under mild social pressure from seven strangers about the length of a line. What
would you expect them to do about a proposition with no evidence either way, under
overwhelming social pressure from everyone they love, about the meaning of their
life and death?

The answer is not "be persuaded." That is the wrong verb. What social proof
produces is *not experiencing the question as open*. Nobody in that room decides
God exists. The room removes the sensation that a decision is available.

Two features of the design deserve specific attention.

**Unison speech and song are not incidental.** Every large religion independently
evolved synchronised group vocalisation, and the ones that did not, largely died.
The reason is not that congregations enjoy singing, though they do. It is that
synchrony produces group cohesion and a felt sense of merged identity, and it
bypasses argument entirely — you cannot disagree with a hymn while you are singing
it, because your body is already committed. Try attending a service and simply
not singing. Stand with the others and stay silent. What you will notice is not
boredom. It is acute discomfort, and a strong pull to join, and that pull is the
mechanism becoming visible.

**The uniformity is manufactured, not observed.** The certainty you read off the
faces around you is a performance, and everyone is performing it while reading it
off everyone else. Sociologists call the result pluralistic ignorance: a state in
which most members of a group privately doubt a norm and each believes they are
the only one. The surveys that get past the performance are consistently
startling — substantial minorities of regular attenders report that they are not
sure they believe, and studies of clergy find a non-trivial population of pastors
who have privately lost their faith and continue preaching because they have no
other career, no other community, and no way to say it out loud. The Dennett and
LaScola interviews with such clergy make painful reading. Every one of those men
was, on Sunday, part of the wall of certainty that other people were reading as
evidence.

So the room is running a feedback loop. Your certainty is calibrated on their
displayed certainty, theirs on yours, and none of it is anchored to anything
outside the building. Dobelli's advice on social proof is severe and correct: the
fact that millions believe something tells you nothing about whether it is true,
and the *strength of your feeling* that it must be true because millions believe
it is precisely the bias, felt from inside.

Then apply Chapter 2's lesson to the sight of a full stadium. A megachurch of
twenty thousand people looks like overwhelming evidence. It is a survivorship
artefact: it is the assembly of everyone for whom this worked, drawn from a city
of millions, with everyone who tried it and left invisible by construction. The
building selects for agreement and then displays the agreement as proof.

**In conclusion:** to find out what you think, you must be somewhere that other
people's certainty cannot reach you. Skip one week and sit with the question
alone — not to rebel, just to take a single measurement outside the instrument.
And when the strength of a conviction rises with the number of people around you
holding it, mark that as a reading of the room rather than of the world. Fifty
million people can be wrong. They usually are, in unison, and it feels from the
inside exactly like being right.
