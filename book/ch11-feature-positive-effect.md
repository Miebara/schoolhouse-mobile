## 11. The Silence You Never Notice

### *The Feature-Positive Effect and Cherry Picking*

Dobelli has a late chapter on the feature-positive effect, and it is one of the
quietest and most important errors in his catalogue: **we notice what is present
and systematically fail to notice what is absent.** Presence generates a
perception. Absence generates nothing at all, and nothing is not something the
mind flags.

His example: we count what happened and neglect what didn't. Sales figures that
rose are visible; the customers who never came are not. A newspaper reports the
train that crashed and never the ten thousand that arrived. Drug side effects
observed in a trial get recorded; the side effects the drug *prevented* are
invisible. Dobelli's advice is uncomfortable because it requires imagining a
category of thing that produces no evidence of itself: you must deliberately
construct the non-events.

Religious belief lives entirely inside this blind spot, and I want to make the
case with the two examples that make it hardest to look away.

**First: the prayers.** You remember the answered one. You cannot remember the
unanswered ones, and here is the thing — it is not that you suppressed them. An
unanswered prayer produces no event. Nothing happens. There is no moment, no
scene, no phone call, nothing for memory to encode. So your mental ledger of
prayer contains a column of hits and a blank space where the misses should be,
and the blank space does not look like data. It looks like nothing, and nothing
weighs nothing.

This is why the prayer journal in Chapter 26 is such a threatening exercise.
Writing prayers down, dated and specific, with the outcome recorded later, is not
a spiritual discipline; it is the construction of the missing column. The reason
almost no one does it is not laziness. It is that the feature-positive effect is
doing you a service, and some part of you knows it.

**Second: the amputees.** This example is usually stated crudely and dismissed as
crude, so let me state it carefully, because it is the strongest single piece of
evidence in this book.

In the entire recorded history of religious healing, across every tradition,
every shrine, every revival and every mass prayer campaign in every century, in
an era of universal photography and medical records, there is not one verified
case of a severed limb regrowing. Not one. There are millions of claimed
healings: pain gone, cancer in remission, tumours shrunk, vision improved,
paralysis lifted, hearing restored. What all of those share is that the
diagnosis was subjective, fluctuating, uncertain, or self-reported — the exact
categories Chapter 10 predicts. What amputation shares with none of them is that
it is unambiguous, permanent, publicly visible, and not subject to regression to
the mean, misdiagnosis, or placebo.

The distribution of miracles is therefore not random with respect to
verifiability. It correlates almost perfectly with it. Miracles happen precisely
and only where the evidence is soft. That is not what divine intervention looks
like. It is exactly what a statistical artefact looks like, and there is no third
option on the table.

The feature-positive effect is why this observation lands as a novelty rather
than as a commonplace. Nobody in a congregation notices the absence of regrown
limbs, because absence generates no experience. You have to go looking for the
hole, and the hole is enormous, and once you have seen it you cannot unsee it.

Cherry picking, which Dobelli treats separately, is the institutional version of
the same thing. His definition is the practice of presenting the metrics you
happened to do well on and quietly omitting the rest — the corporate annual report
that celebrates the one division that grew. Religious institutions do this as a
matter of routine and without any sense of dishonesty, because the mechanism is
selection rather than deceit. The mission newsletter reports the church planted,
not the four that folded. The prophecy conference revisits the predictions that
can be read as hits and never audits the misses; every generation since the first
century has identified itself as the last, and the failure of each cohort is not
so much denied as unmentioned. Harold Camping named a date in 1994, missed, named
another in 2011, missed, and retained followers through both. The Jehovah's
Witnesses' literature on 1914 and 1975 was extensively revised rather than
retracted. Dobelli's chapter on the *falsification of history* explains the
mechanism: we do not experience ourselves as rewriting the record, because the
old version is simply gone.

And notice the asymmetry in how the two columns are treated. A hit is reported as
God's action. A miss is reported as *not data* — God's timing, a no that is
really a not-yet, an answer we didn't understand. So the miss column is not only
invisible to memory; it is reclassified on arrival so that it can never be
counted even if noticed. Between the feature-positive effect and that
reclassification rule, there is no path by which negative evidence can ever reach
the ledger. Which is another way of stating Chapter 8: the God who cannot lose.

**In conclusion:** for any claim you hold about how the world responds to you,
build the missing column. Write down the predictions before the outcomes. Count
the silences. Ask what category of evidence is conspicuously absent and whether
its absence has a pattern — and if the pattern is that the evidence gets softer
exactly where verification gets easier, you already have your answer. The most
important data in your life is the data that never happened, and it will never
volunteer itself.
