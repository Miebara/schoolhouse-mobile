## 17. The Man in the Robe

### *Authority Bias and the Halo Effect*

Dobelli's chapter on authority bias reports the finding that our deference to
authority is not proportional to the authority's actual reliability, and that the
markers we respond to are almost entirely cosmetic: the title, the uniform, the
platform, the confidence, the position of the body relative to the audience.

His examples are drawn from medicine and finance, where the consequences are
measurable. Doctors' orders are followed by nurses even when the dosage is
obviously wrong — the classic 1966 Hofling study had nurses prepared to administer
double the maximum dose of an unfamiliar drug on a phone instruction from a
"doctor" they had never met. Economists' forecasts move markets despite a track
record barely distinguishable from chance. Airline crashes have been caused by
first officers who noticed the captain's error and did not say so with enough
force, which is why the entire discipline of crew resource management exists: the
industry had to be *engineered* against authority bias, at enormous cost, because
training and intelligence were not sufficient.

And Milgram, which everyone has heard of and few people have properly absorbed.
Ordinary adults, in a university building, instructed by a man in a lab coat,
administered what they believed to be dangerous electric shocks to a stranger who
was screaming, because the man in the coat said the experiment required it. The
methodology has been criticised, and the criticisms are fair, but the replications
and variants have consistently found the core effect. The relevant variable was
not sadism. It was the coat.

Now consider the architecture and stagecraft of a religious service, treating it
as a machine designed to maximise the markers Dobelli lists.

The speaker is elevated, physically, above the audience — in a pulpit, on a stage,
often lit while the room is dark. He is dressed differently from everyone else,
either in vestments that signal centuries of institutional weight or in the
deliberate ordinariness that signals authenticity. He is the only person permitted
to speak at length. He is introduced by a title that is also a kinship term:
Father, Pastor, Reverend, Shepherd, Elder. He cannot be interrupted, questioned,
or contradicted during the event; there is no floor, no Q&A, no right of reply,
and the format itself has no mechanism for disagreement. And his claims are, in
principle, not checkable — which distinguishes him from every other authority in
your life. Your doctor can be second-opinioned. Your accountant can be audited.

Then add the escalation available only here: he does not claim to have expertise.
He claims to be relaying the words of the being who made the universe. There is no
higher authority marker available in human language, and it is deployed weekly, to
a seated, silent, synchronised audience.

The halo effect finishes the job. Dobelli's version: a single salient positive
trait spreads to unrelated traits — attractive people are judged more competent,
tall candidates more capable, a company with strong recent earnings is assumed to
have good management. Religious authority runs a powerful halo: a man who is
genuinely kind, who visited your father in hospital and buried your mother
gently, whose personal warmth is not in doubt, becomes for that reason credible
about cosmology, history, sexual ethics, the afterlife, and the moral status of
your friends. But kindness at a hospital bedside is evidence about kindness. It
carries no information about whether a tomb was empty in 33 CE, and the halo makes
that separation nearly impossible to feel.

Now the part that should be decisive, and which I will state as carefully as I
can, because it is where the institution's own history convicts it.

If a system's core design feature is unquestionable authority combined with
unfalsifiable claims and no external audit, then abuse is not an aberration. It is
the predicted output. And that is what the record shows: decades of documented
child abuse across the Catholic Church, the Southern Baptist Convention, the
Anglican communion, the Jehovah's Witnesses, and independent evangelical
networks — abuse that was concealed, for years, by institutions that moved
offenders between parishes and pressured victims into silence. The Boston Globe
investigation, the Australian Royal Commission, the Pennsylvania grand jury, the
2022 Guidepost report into the SBC: the same pattern, in different countries,
under different theologies. Overwhelmingly, the victims were not disbelieved
because people were wicked. They were disbelieved because authority bias made the
priest's word heavier than the child's, and because the halo made "he is a holy
man" feel like evidence.

The reason this belongs in a book about thinking errors, rather than in a book
about scandal, is that it is the strongest available test of the institution's own
claim about itself. An organisation with privileged access to a perfectly good
being, staffed by people receiving his guidance, should not have a *worse* record
on this than secular institutions with no such access. If the guidance is real, it
failed at the largest possible scale, repeatedly, in the most obvious cases, for
generations. If the guidance is not real, the outcome is exactly what Dobelli's
chapter predicts for any hierarchy with unaccountable authority. Only one of those
two hypotheses was expecting these findings.

**In conclusion:** strip the markers and re-run the claim. Imagine the identical
sentences spoken by someone your own age, in ordinary clothes, standing at your
eye level, in a room where you were free to interrupt and ask for evidence. If the
claim gets weaker when the robe comes off, it was never the claim persuading you.
And ask the diagnostic question about any authority in your life: what would
happen if I said, out loud, in this room, "I think that's false"? Where the honest
answer is social consequences rather than an argument, you are not in a place
where truth is being determined.
