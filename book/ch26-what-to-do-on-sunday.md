## 26. What to Do on Sunday Morning

### *A Practical Program*

Dobelli ends most chapters with an instruction rather than a conclusion, and his
instructions are almost always subtractive: stop doing this, avoid that situation,
remove this input. This chapter collects the whole book into things you can
actually do. None of them requires you to have decided anything in advance.

---

### The four experiments

**1. The prayer ledger. (Chapters 2, 7, 11, 13.)**

Buy a notebook. For the next six months, write down every prayer you make that has
a checkable outcome — before the outcome is known. Be specific: not "bless my
mother" but "my mother's scan on the 14th will show improvement." Date it. Leave
space for the result. Record the result honestly when it arrives, including the
ones where nothing happened, and count the blanks as data because they *are* the
data.

This single exercise defeats survivorship bias, the feature-positive effect and
hindsight bias simultaneously, and almost nobody has ever done it. If prayer
works, you will have the first decent evidence anyone has assembled. If it does
not, you will have the missing column that your memory has been silently deleting
your whole life.

**2. The falsification sentence. (Chapter 8.)**

Complete this sentence in writing, today: *I would stop believing this if
______.*

If you can fill it in, you are doing something intellectually respectable. Go and
check whether the thing has already happened. If you cannot fill it in — if you
find yourself writing conditions you know can never be met — then note what you have
just discovered about the kind of belief you hold. It is not one that could ever
have been informed by anything.

**3. The four Sundays. (Chapter 23.)**

Do not go, for four consecutive weeks. Do not announce it, do not resign, do not
frame it as anything. This is the only way to find out whether you are a believer
or a person who has never opted out of a default set for you at birth. Use one of
those mornings — the same hour, at a desk — to write down what you actually think.
Handwriting, not typing; it is slower and harder to bluff.

What you are measuring is what you feel on the fifth Sunday. Longing is a real
result. So is relief. Both are informative and neither is a betrayal of anyone.

**4. The stranger's test. (Chapters 8, 18, 23.)**

Take one claim from your own tradition and one from a tradition you reject — the
angel and the golden plates, the night journey to Jerusalem, the statue drinking
milk. Write the evidence for each in parallel columns: who reported it, how long
after the event, what they had to gain, whether it was independently verified,
what the base rate is for that kind of report. Do it properly, on paper.

You will find that you have been running two different standards, and you will be
able to see the exact point where the second standard switches on. That switch is
the whole subject of this book.

---

### The nine questions

Ask these of any religious claim, or any claim at all. They are the book,
compressed.

1. **Who is missing from this sample?** Who had the same experience and the
   opposite outcome, and where are they now? *(Survivorship bias.)*
2. **What is the base rate?** How often does the claimed thing happen, and how
   often is this kind of report mistaken? *(Base-rate neglect.)*
3. **What would falsify this?** Name the observation. *(Confirmation bias.)*
4. **Was the target defined before or after the arrow landed?** *(Clustering
   illusion.)*
5. **Would the same intervention have looked effective if it did nothing?** Was it
   applied at the worst moment? *(Regression to the mean.)*
6. **What is absent that I have not noticed?** *(Feature-positive effect.)*
7. **Would I believe this if the person saying it had no robe, no stage, and no
   title?** *(Authority bias.)*
8. **What does it cost me socially to conclude otherwise?** *(In-group bias.)*
9. **Would I join this today, knowing what I know, with nothing invested?** *(Sunk
   cost fallacy.)*

### If you decide to leave

**Do the replacement first, not afterwards.** Chapter 24 is not decoration. Before
you stop attending, line up a standing weekly commitment involving the same people
each time — choir, a sports team, a class, volunteering with a fixed rota, a
regular dinner you host. It must recur on a schedule and involve obligation, or it
will not survive contact with a bad week. This is the single most common failure
mode of people who leave: they subtract two hours and add nothing, and then
mistake loneliness for the discovery that they were wrong.

**Do not de-convert anybody.** Chapter 21 explains exactly why: pressure on a
costly belief makes it stronger, and dissonance reduction will convert your best
argument into their renewed conviction. If they ask, answer honestly and briefly.
If they don't, don't.

**Expect grief and do not misread it.** One to three years, mostly social, is the
typical figure. Missing it is not evidence that it was true. You would miss any
community you left.

**Be careful about how you tell people, and in what order.** Tell one safe person
first. Do not do it at Christmas, at a funeral, or in an argument. And be realistic
about the specific relationships at risk — in some families and some traditions the
cost is severe, and you are entitled to weigh that cost and to decide that quiet
non-attendance serves you better than a declaration. That is not cowardice. Chapter
18 priced the stakes honestly and you are allowed to act on the price.

**Special case: if the cost is genuinely dangerous** — if you are financially
dependent, a minor, or in a community where apostasy carries real physical
risk — then none of the above applies as written. Your obligation is to your own
safety, not to intellectual consistency in public. Private clarity is a complete
and honourable position. Nothing in this book asks you to be a martyr for a
negative claim.

**Keep what works.** You are permitted to keep the practices without the
metaphysics, and you should. Rest one day a week. Say something before a meal.
Sit in the cathedral. Sing the Bach. Read Ecclesiastes and the Psalms, which are
better literature than most of what has been written since. Ritual is a technology,
and the technology does not care what you believe about its origins.

---

**In conclusion:** you do not have to resolve the existence of God this year, or
ever. What you can do is stop letting a room full of synchronised, kind, certain
people do your thinking for you, and take four measurements outside the
instrument. That is a small, concrete, entirely achievable thing, and it is the
only recommendation in this book that matters. Start with the notebook. Start on
Sunday.
