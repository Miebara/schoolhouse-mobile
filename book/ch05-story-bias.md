## 5. The Gospel Is a Very Good Story, and That Is the Problem

### *Story Bias*

Dobelli's chapter on story bias makes a claim that sounds cynical and turns out
to be technical: **we are attracted to stories in inverse proportion to their
information content.** A story achieves its emotional force by discarding
everything that does not serve the arc. The facts that survive are the ones that
fit. Two accounts of the same company, one a coherent narrative of a visionary
founder and one an honest accounting of market conditions, luck, timing and
survivorship — the first will sell a million copies and the second will not be
published. The narrative is more compelling *because* it contains less
information.

He recommends a specific discipline: when a story moves you, ask what was left
out to make it move you.

Now consider that Christianity is, by common consent, one of the most
extraordinary stories ever assembled. I mean that as a genuine compliment. A
child born in an occupied province, a ministry among the poor, a betrayal by a
friend for money, a state execution, three days of silence, an empty tomb. It has
a wronged innocent, an unjust authority, a reversal at the darkest point, and a
promise that death — the thing you fear most and cannot negotiate with — has
been beaten. Then it is nested inside a larger story with a beginning, a fall, a
long middle, and a guaranteed ending in which everything is explained and the
accounts are settled. It is not accidental that this story spread. It is the
single most competitive narrative structure available to human attention.

But story bias tells you something uncomfortable about that fact: **narrative
power is not correlated with truth, and to the extent that it is correlated with
anything, the correlation runs the wrong way.** True accounts of complex events
are ragged. They have loose ends, unexplained coincidences, people who appear
once and are never mentioned again, and no thematic resolution. The universe as
described by physics has no plot; it has initial conditions and dynamics. The
history of life has no protagonist. Real causation is diffuse and unsatisfying.
So when you encounter an account of everything that has a plot, a protagonist, a
crisis and a resolution, the strong prior should be that you are holding a
constructed artefact — because that is the only kind of object that has those
properties.

You can see the compression happening in the text itself, and this is worth
knowing if nobody has told you. The earliest gospel, Mark, has no birth
narrative, and in its oldest manuscripts it ends with frightened women and no
appearance of the risen Christ; the resurrection appearances at the end of Mark
are a later addition, which modern Bibles footnote and sermons rarely mention.
Matthew and Luke, written later, add genealogies that contradict each other, and
two incompatible accounts of how a family from Nazareth came to be in Bethlehem.
John, later still, has Jesus speaking in long theological monologues that the
earlier gospels know nothing about, and moves the date of the crucifixion to make
a symbolic point about the Passover lamb. Paul, whose letters are the *earliest*
Christian documents we have, shows no knowledge of a virgin birth, an empty tomb
narrative, or almost any incident from Jesus's life.

This is what you would expect from a story being edited toward narrative power
over decades: the arc tightens, the theology deepens, the loose ends get
resolved, the details grow more vivid the further you get from the events. It is
exactly the opposite of what you would expect from independent eyewitness
reports, which converge on mundane facts and diverge on interpretation.
Historians of every religious background recognise this pattern; it is not a
sceptic's invention. What the pulpit gives you is the finished product, with the
seams sanded down and the disagreements harmonised into a single "gospel story"
that no gospel actually tells.

Story bias also explains why the arguments in this book will feel weaker than
they are. I am offering you base rates, selection effects, denominators and
control texts. It is thin gruel next to an execution and an empty tomb. Dobelli
notes that we systematically undervalue statistical arguments relative to
anecdotes even when we *know* the statistics are better evidence — a single named
child outperforms a mortality table in every charity trial ever run. So the
asymmetry you feel while reading is not a signal that the pulpit has the better
case. It is a signal that the pulpit has the better *format*, and has had two
thousand years to optimise it against an audience whose weaknesses have not
changed.

And notice how the story is doing work in your own life, not just in scripture.
The most powerful religious sentence available is not a doctrine; it is *my life
is part of a story that means something*. Under that frame, suffering becomes
plot rather than noise, waiting becomes preparation, and your death becomes a
chapter break rather than a full stop. That is an enormous psychological gift and
Chapter 24 will not pretend otherwise. But it is precisely the gift that story
bias predicts we would pay almost any epistemic price to receive — and the price
here is believing something about the structure of reality on the grounds that it
would be a better story if it were true.

**In conclusion:** when a claim about reality arrives in the shape of a
beautifully told story, treat the beauty as a warning rather than as evidence.
Ask what had to be left out. Ask what the ragged version looks like — the one
with the contradictory genealogies, the added ending, the letters that don't
mention the tomb. If the compelling version and the complete version differ, you
have learned something about the storytellers, not about God.
