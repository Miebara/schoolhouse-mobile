## 2. The Testimony of the Drowned

### *Survivorship Bias*

Dobelli opens his book with survivorship bias, and he is right to. It is the
error that most efficiently converts noise into meaning, and it is the engine of
almost every religious testimony you will ever hear.

The mechanism: you systematically overestimate your chance of success because
you only ever meet the successes. Musicians who made it give interviews.
Musicians who didn't work in insurance and are not invited to speak at your
school. Bestselling authors describe the discipline of writing a thousand words
a day; the ten thousand people who wrote a thousand words a day and sold nothing
publish no memoirs about it. Dobelli's advice is to visit the graveyard of
failed ventures deliberately, because nobody will bring it to you.

Now consider Sunday's testimony slot.

A woman stands up and says her tumour is gone. The congregation prayed for six
weeks and the scan came back clear, and she is weeping, and she is telling the
truth. Everybody in the room draws the obvious conclusion, and nobody in the
room notices the structural fact: the people whose tumours were not gone are not
standing up. Some of them stopped coming. Some are too ill to attend. Some are
dead. And the dead do not take the microphone to report a negative result.

This is not a snide remark about grief. It is a statement about the shape of the
data set. Every testimony service in the world is a sampling procedure that
selects for positive outcomes with an efficiency no honest researcher would
tolerate. If a pharmaceutical company ran a trial this way — publicising the
recoveries, losing track of the deaths, permitting the recovered to describe
their own case in emotional terms to an audience of investors — we would call it
fraud, and we would be right. In a church it is called a blessing.

Dobelli's cemetery advice has a precise religious application, and it is
devastating: **go and look at the people the prayers didn't work for.** They
exist in enormous numbers and they are systematically invisible. Children's
oncology wards are full of parents who prayed with a fervour you cannot imagine
and buried their child anyway. Their absence from the testimony slot is not
evidence that prayer works. It is evidence that testimony slots have a door
policy.

The bias runs deeper than illness. Consider the whole history of religions. You
were born into a tradition that has survived, so you inherit the impression that
it possesses something that made it survive — truth, presumably. But the
graveyard of dead religions is vast and almost entirely unvisited. The cult of
Mithras, the state worship of Sol Invictus, Manichaeism, the Norse pantheon,
Zoroastrianism reduced from an empire's faith to a scattered remnant, the
thousands of local gods whose names did not survive their villages. Every one of
those had believers as certain as you, testimonies as moving as yours, and
answered prayers they could recount in detail. They are gone for reasons that
have to do with armies, trade routes, printing presses, birth rates and imperial
politics — not with which one was true. Survivorship bias makes the winner look
inevitable and therefore look correct. Ask any historian of religion whether the
faiths that grew were the ones with the better evidence.

There is a nastier variant, which Dobelli would file under self-selection.
Religious communities frequently report better health, longer life and higher
happiness than the general population, and this is offered as evidence that the
belief is doing something. Some of it is real and Chapter 24 takes it
seriously — social connection is genuinely one of the best-evidenced predictors
of longevity we have. But a great deal of the effect is a selection artefact.
Regular attendance requires that you be well enough to attend, mobile enough to
get there, and socially functional enough to stay. The chronically ill, the
severely depressed and the isolated drop out of the attending population and
into the comparison group. The measurement instrument selects for health and
then reports health as an outcome. Swimmers again.

And then the version that catches even careful people: the survivorship bias of
your own memory. You remember the prayer that was answered. You do not remember
the ninety you offered in the same year and forgot about, because an unanswered
prayer generates no event to remember — nothing happens, and nothing is
famously hard to recall. Your own head runs the same door policy as the
testimony service. This is why Chapter 26 recommends the single most useful
experiment in this book: write your prayers down, dated and specific, before
the outcome is known. Almost nobody does this. The people who have done it
report a consistent, uncomfortable result, and it is the same result Francis
Galton got in 1872 when he checked whether the British royal family — the most
prayed-for people in the empire, prayed for weekly by an entire nation — lived
any longer than comparable aristocrats. They did not.

One caution, so I do not fall into my own trap. Survivorship bias does not prove
that no prayer has ever been answered. It proves something more limited and more
useful: that the *evidence* you have been shown is worthless, because it was
collected by a procedure that would produce the same result whether prayer
worked perfectly or not at all. When a measurement gives the same reading under
both hypotheses, it is not a measurement. It is a mirror.

**In conclusion:** before you accept a testimony, ask who was eligible to give
it. Ask what happened to everyone who entered the same situation and got the
other outcome, and where they are now, and whether the room has any mechanism
for hearing from them. If the answer is that the room hears only from the
survivors, then the room is not learning anything about God. It is learning who
survived. Go and visit the cemetery. Nobody is going to bring it to you.
