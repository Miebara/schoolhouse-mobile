## 15. The Sermon as Fluent Nonsense

### *The Twaddle Tendency, Chauffeur Knowledge, the Overconfidence Effect, and the Forecast Illusion*

Dobelli's chapter on the twaddle tendency makes a claim that sounds rude and is
actually a testable observation: **when people have nothing to say, they do not
fall silent — they produce fluent, grammatical, confident language that carries no
information.** He is scathing about corporate strategy documents and academic
prose, and his diagnostic is a good one: try to state the claim in plain words and
see whether anything remains. If the passage cannot be summarised because there is
nothing in it to summarise, you have found twaddle.

He pairs it with chauffeur knowledge, a distinction he borrows from a story about
Max Planck's driver, who heard Planck's lecture so many times he could deliver it
himself — flawlessly, and without understanding a word. Chauffeur knowledge is
the ability to produce the sounds of expertise. It is extremely convincing,
because our detector for competence is fluency, and fluency is cheap.

Now open any book of theology, or listen to next Sunday's sermon with a notepad,
and apply Dobelli's test. Write down each claim in plain language and mark whether
it forbids anything — whether the world would look different if it were false.

You will find some sentences that pass. "Give ten percent of your income" is a
clear instruction. "Jesus rose bodily from the tomb in the first century" is a
historical claim, false or true. Good; those are the ones this book has been
arguing with.

But a very large share of religious language turns out, under this test, to have
no content at all. *God is the ground of being.* *He works in ways beyond our
understanding.* *The Spirit moves where he wills.* *Faith is not certainty but
trust in the midst of uncertainty.* *He is not absent, he is silent, and silence
is its own form of speech.* These sentences are beautiful. Several are moving. Not
one of them forbids any observation whatsoever. Try to specify what you would
expect to see if "the Spirit moves where he wills" were false and you will find
there is nothing to specify — which means the sentence conveys no information about
the world, in exactly the technical sense Dobelli means. It conveys mood, group
membership, and reassurance, and it is delivered in the grammatical form of a
factual claim, which is why it functions as one.

The professional incentive here is worth naming without malice. A pastor must
speak for thirty minutes, weekly, for decades, on a subject about which no new
information has arrived in two thousand years, to an audience that will not
tolerate "I don't know" as the answer to the questions that matter most. There is
no honest way to fill that slot. The output is structurally guaranteed to be
mostly language, and the best preachers are the most fluent, and fluency is what
we mistake for insight. Dobelli's warning about mistaking the chauffeur for
Planck is not an insult to the chauffeur. It is a warning about our detector.

Then the overconfidence effect, which Dobelli demonstrates with calibration
studies: ask people for ranges they are 90 percent confident about and the true
value falls outside the range far more often than ten percent of the time.
Confidence and accuracy are only weakly related, and — this is the part that
matters — confidence is what we use to assess accuracy in others. Note the
confidence gradient across the topics in a sermon. On matters that can be
checked — historical detail, the meaning of a Greek word, the state of the
sciences — the tone is careful. On matters that cannot be checked at all — the
nature of God, the condition of the dead, the purpose of your suffering — the tone
is most certain. That gradient runs the wrong way for anyone tracking evidence,
and exactly the right way for anyone whose authority depends on not being
checkable.

Finally the forecast illusion, where Dobelli is at his most useful because there
is hard data. Philip Tetlock's twenty-year study of expert political
predictions — 284 experts, 28,000 forecasts — found that their accuracy was barely
better than chance and that the most confident, most famous, most media-visible
experts were the *least* accurate. Dobelli's practical advice: ask any forecaster
for their track record, and if there isn't one, ignore them.

Apply it to prophecy. Religious prediction has a track record and it is
catastrophic. The apocalypse has been announced, with dates, in essentially every
generation since the first — beginning inside the New Testament itself, where the
expectation of an imminent return is explicit and unmistakable, and continuing
through the Millerites in 1844, the Jehovah's Witnesses' 1914 and 1975, Hal
Lindsey's 1980s, the sequence of dates through 1988, 1994, 2000, 2011, 2012. The
hit rate is zero across roughly two thousand years and thousands of specific
claims, which is a worse record than any forecaster Tetlock studied, and it has
had no effect whatsoever on the credibility of the enterprise. That immunity is
the phenomenon. Chapter 21 explains it.

**In conclusion:** run the plain-words test on everything you are told from a
platform. Write the claim down in a sentence a twelve-year-old would understand,
and ask what it forbids. If it forbids nothing, you have received language and
not information, however beautiful it was and however moved you were — and being
moved by it is not a reason to believe it, it is the reason it was phrased that
way. Then ask the speaker for their track record, and notice that no one has ever
asked before.
