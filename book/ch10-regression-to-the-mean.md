## 10. The Healing That Was Going to Happen Anyway

### *Regression to the Mean and the Intention-to-Treat Error*

Dobelli's chapter on regression to the mean is, in my judgment, the most
practically useful chapter in his book, and it is the one that dismantles faith
healing without requiring anyone to be a liar.

The mechanism: extreme measurements tend to be followed by less extreme ones,
because part of any extreme value is temporary fluctuation. A student who scores
far below average on one test will probably do better on the next, whatever you
do in between. A region with an unusually bad flu season will probably have a
milder one next year. Dobelli's example is a manager who berates his
worst-performing salespeople and promotes his best, then observes that the
berated improve and the promoted decline, and concludes that criticism works and
praise spoils. He has learned nothing about management. He has measured the
weather.

The critical consequence: **any intervention applied at the moment of maximum
suffering will appear to work.** Not because it does anything, but because
maximum suffering is, by definition, the point from which things are most likely
to improve.

Consider when people seek healing. Not on an average Tuesday. They go to the
healing service, the shrine, the prayer chain, at the worst point — the flare-up,
the crisis, the pain that has become unbearable, the depression at its deepest.
That is precisely the moment when regression to the mean is largest. Chronic
conditions are, almost universally, cyclical: rheumatoid arthritis, multiple
sclerosis, back pain, migraine, IBS, eczema, most psychiatric illness, and
subjective symptoms of nearly every kind fluctuate. Intervene at the trough and
you will observe improvement in a large fraction of cases with no causal
contribution whatsoever.

Add spontaneous remission, which is real and more common than most people
realise. Cancers regress without treatment — rarely, but well-documented, and
best-documented for exactly the cancers most often featured in healing
testimonies, because they are the ones people survive long enough to testify
about. Add diagnostic error: some fraction of "cured" cases were never correct
diagnoses to begin with, and the base rate for misdiagnosis in radiology and
pathology is not small. Add the placebo response, which is genuinely powerful
for pain, nausea, fatigue and functional symptoms — the *reported* symptoms that
faith healing overwhelmingly targets — and near-zero for tumour mass, blood
glucose, or bone regrowth.

Now put those four together and you have a machine that will generate a steady
stream of sincere, honest, verifiable-sounding healing testimonies at a
predictable rate, in a universe with no God in it at all. The healer need not be
a fraud. The patient need not be lying. The recovery need not be imaginary. Every
element can be genuine and the causal claim still be false — which is why
testimony volume tells you nothing. A busy healing ministry will produce more
testimonies than a quiet one for the same reason a bigger sample produces more
extreme values.

Then Dobelli's intention-to-treat error, which is the sharpest instrument here
and the one nobody applies. In clinical trials, the rule is that you must analyse
every patient you enrolled, including the ones who dropped out — because dropouts
are not random. People drop out because they got worse, died, or gave up. Analyse
only those who completed the protocol and even a harmful treatment will look
beneficial. Dobelli's illustration: a study of an exercise programme for cardiac
patients that only counted people who kept exercising has proved that people
healthy enough to keep exercising are healthier.

Every healing ministry in the world reports on completers only. The man who was
prayed over and got worse does not come back to the microphone. The woman who
died three months later is not mentioned at next year's conference; her testimony
from *before* she died may well still be in the promotional video. The follow-up
does not exist. There is no registry, no denominator, no attempt to trace
outcomes for everyone who came through the doors — and the absence is not
incidental, because a ministry that published complete follow-up would cease to
have a ministry.

This has been checked, in the few cases where checking was possible. In the
1980s the psychologist and physician investigations of the American faith-healing
circuit — most famously the work of William Nolen, who followed up Kathryn
Kuhlman's cases, and James Randi's investigations of Peter Popoff — found no
verified organic cures on follow-up, and found several people who had abandoned
medical treatment and died. Nolen's finding that stayed with me: a woman
proclaimed healed of spinal cancer threw away her brace, walked at the healer's
instruction, and collapsed with a vertebral fracture the next day. She died four
months later. She had been, at the moment of testimony, entirely sincere.

**In conclusion:** whenever an intervention is applied at the worst moment,
expect improvement and attribute nothing to the intervention. Before believing a
healing claim, ask three questions: what were the numbers who came and did not
improve, what happened to this person twelve months later, and was there a
diagnosis on paper before and after. If nobody kept the denominator, nobody has
evidence. They have a highlight reel of the mean reverting.
