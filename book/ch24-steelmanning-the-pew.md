## 24. What Church Actually Delivers

### *Alternative Blindness*

Dobelli has a chapter on alternative blindness: we compare an option against one
alternative — usually a straw one — rather than against the full set of real
alternatives. His example is the investor choosing between two bad funds who never
considers that both are worse than an index tracker. The error is not choosing
badly among the options presented. It is failing to notice that the presented set
was not the actual set.

An argument for leaving church that compares *church* against *not church* is
committing exactly this error, because "not church" is not a thing. It is an
absence, and absences get filled by whatever is nearest, and what is nearest is
usually worse than what was there. So this chapter is the one where I argue
against myself, as honestly as I can manage. If the previous twenty-three chapters
were correct, then a very large number of people are receiving something valuable
from a false belief, and pretending otherwise would be the same cherry-picking I
accused the pulpit of in Chapter 11.

Here is what the pew actually delivers. I am going to state it at full strength.

**A community that shows up without being asked.** This is the big one, and it is
not sentimental — it is the best-evidenced item on the list. Social connection is
among the strongest predictors of longevity and mental health we have; the
meta-analyses put the effect size in the same range as smoking cessation. And what
a congregation provides is not just friendship but a specific, rare structure:
non-selected relationships across age, class and temperament, with an obligation
attached. Your friends are people you chose because you like them. A congregation
gives you eighty people who will bring food when your mother dies whether or not
they like you, because that is what the group does. Almost nothing in secular life
generates this. Modern friendship is elective, dissolves under inconvenience, and
is catastrophically age-segregated. If you leave, the loss of this is the thing
that will actually hurt, and it will hurt for a year or two, and no amount of being
right about cosmology will touch it.

**Ritual around the unbearable.** Death, birth, marriage, catastrophe. Religion
has, over millennia, developed extremely well-engineered procedures for these:
prescribed words when nobody can think of any, a defined sequence of things to do,
a role for everyone present, and a container for grief with a beginning and an
end. Secular alternatives are thin and everyone knows it. A funeral with no
liturgy tends to become a series of nervous speeches. Sitting shiva, or the
Requiem, or the forty days, work *as procedures* — and their working has nothing
to do with whether the metaphysics is true.

**A framework for mortality.** Not merely comfort about your own death — though that
too — but a way of holding the fact that you will lose everyone. The naturalistic
alternative is true and it is thinner. I am not going to pretend that "you will
cease and so will everyone you love and there is no reunion" is equally
consoling. It is not. It has the single advantage of being, so far as anyone can
tell, the case.

**Moral formation with teeth.** Congregations are among the few remaining
institutions that ask people to do things they do not want to do for the benefit
of people they will never meet, and then check whether they did. Religious
adherents give more to charity and volunteer more hours than the non-religious in
most datasets, and while a substantial part of that giving goes to the institution
itself, a substantial part does not. Food banks, prison visiting, hospice work,
refugee resettlement, addiction recovery: walk into any of these and count the
denominational logos.

**Beauty and time out of the market.** Bach, plainsong, gospel choirs, cathedrals,
four hundred people singing in harmony, and one hour a week in which nobody is
selling you anything and your phone is in your pocket. The last of these is now
almost extinct elsewhere.

**Practices that work regardless of the metaphysics.** Confession is unstructured
therapy with a confidentiality guarantee. Sabbath is enforced rest that a culture
of overwork cannot otherwise produce. Grace before a meal is a gratitude practice,
and gratitude practices have measurable effects. Contemplative prayer overlaps
heavily with meditation, which is one of the better-supported psychological
interventions we have.

That is a serious list, and anyone who leaves and does not replace these items is
worse off. Most people who leave do not replace them. This is the honest cost, and
the deconversion literature is unambiguous about it: the reported difficulty is
overwhelmingly social and ritual, not intellectual.

Now, three things the list does not do.

**It does not make the claim true.** This is the whole point and it must be said
plainly. Everything above is a fact about human beings — that we need company,
ritual, and rest. None of it is evidence that the universe was made on purpose. A
belief's usefulness and a belief's truth are independent, and conflating them is
the error at the bottom of every "but faith gives people hope" conversation. Note
that the argument, if it worked, would work equally well for any tradition, and
therefore selects none.

**It does not require the metaphysics.** Every item on the list is, in principle,
separable. Some traditions have already demonstrated this — Unitarian
congregations, Quaker meetings without creeds, humanist assemblies, Sunday
Assembly, secular Buddhist sanghas, Jewish communities where a substantial share
of members are frank atheists and attend anyway. The results are mixed and I will
not overstate them: several secular congregation projects have struggled, and the
reason is instructive. What holds a demanding community together is *cost*, per
Chapter 20 — and a group that asks nothing and threatens nothing has trouble
generating commitment. The lesson is not that community requires God. It is that
community requires demands, and if you leave you must go and find something that
demands things of you.

**And the goods come bundled with harms that are equally real.** The same
mechanism that produces the casseroles produces the shunning. The same authority
that comforts the dying protected the abusers in Chapter 17. The same certainty
that carries someone through chemotherapy tells a gay teenager that they are
broken, and drives the suicide rate in that population up in exactly the
communities that are warmest to everyone else. The moral formation includes
formation into positions many members would be ashamed of if they held them for
any other reason. You cannot take the ledger's credit column and leave the debit
column in the building.

**In conclusion:** do not compare church against nothing. Compare it against the
best available arrangement of substitutes, and then go and actually build the
substitutes, because they will not assemble themselves: a standing weekly
commitment to a group of people you did not choose, a practice of rest, something
that asks you for money and time and checks whether you gave it, and a plan for
what happens when somebody dies. If you leave without doing this, you will be
right and lonely, and Dobelli's chapter is clear that being right about the option
you rejected is no consolation for having chosen badly among the ones you had.
